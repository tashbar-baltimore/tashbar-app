# The Missing Esrog · Chapter 8: In the Daylight

Yehuda did not plan to knock. His hand did it by itself.

Rav Mizrachi and Rabbi Feldman both turned around. For a moment the Rav's frown stayed where it was. Then he saw six boys squeezed into his doorway, holding a notebook, a drawing and a tiny plastic bag, and his eyebrows went all the way up.

"Rabbi Feldman," said the Rav, "I believe your talmidim have come to visit."

Rabbi Feldman's face went through about four different feelings at once. Surprise came first. Then something that might have been the beginning of "What are you doing on the third floor?" And then, when he saw the looks on their faces, plain curiosity.

"Boys," he said, "is something wrong?"

"Not wrong," said Yehuda. His voice came out squeaky, and he started again. "Rebbe, we think the spot wasn't your fault. We think it was there before you touched the esrog."

The Rav put the esrog down very gently on a soft cloth on his desk. "Come in," he said. "Slowly. And tell me how you know."

So they told him. They had practiced on the walk to school, and each boy had his part.

Yehuda went first. He took out the yellow note, still stuck inside the cover of his notebook, and handed it to Rabbi Feldman. "We found this behind the radiator. The wind blew it there. You did leave us a note."

Rabbi Feldman read his own handwriting and laughed softly. "So I did."

Then Avrumi unrolled his drawing and laid it on the desk. "I drew the esrog in the morning, before recess. Before you picked it up. Look at the top, by the stem." He pointed to the dark, filled-in dot. "I saw a speck right there, so I drew it."

Rav Mizrachi took out a large magnifying glass with a wooden handle. He looked at the drawing. Then he looked at the esrog. Then he looked at the drawing again.

"It is in the same place," he said quietly.

Then Nosson stepped forward and held up the little plastic bag. Inside was a pinch of soft white flax. "This came from the box the esrog was shipped in," he said. "Yesterday morning I carried the box to the garbage for the rebbe, and it stuck all over my sleeve. My father says that sometimes a tiny piece of wrapping sticks to an esrog and looks like a spot. He said only the Rav can decide. But he said to ask: is it really part of the esrog?"

The room was very still. Even the radiator seemed to stop ticking.

Rav Mizrachi picked up the esrog again and carried it to the window. This time he did not look at it from the front. He held it level with his eye and turned it slowly, so that the sunlight came across the skin from the side. He looked for a long time.

Then he reached into his desk drawer and took out a small, soft brush, the kind a sofer uses to sweep dust off a parchment. With one tiny, careful stroke, he brushed the top of the esrog beside the stem.

He looked again.

Then he held the brush up to the light, and the boys could see something on the very tip of it: a thin wisp of fluff, so small that it almost wasn't there.

"Flax," said the Rav.

He turned the esrog toward them. Where the dark speck had been, there was nothing at all. Only smooth, bright, bumpy yellow, the color of sunshine on a Shabbos afternoon.

"It was never part of the esrog," said Rav Mizrachi. "It was only sitting on top. In the shadow of the stem, a little piece of white fluff looked dark." He handed the esrog to Rabbi Feldman, and for the first time, he smiled. "Your esrog is beautiful, Reb Feldman. And it always was."

Rabbi Feldman held the esrog in both hands, the way he had held the silver box on the first morning. He did not say anything for a moment. When he looked up, his eyes were shining.

"Boys," he said. "Do you know what you just did?"

"We found your note?" said Dovid.

"You did much more than that." He looked at each of them. "Yehuda, you wrote down everything you noticed, even the things that seemed small. Avrumi, you drew what you saw, exactly as it was. Dovid, you built a tool to reach what nobody could reach. Binyamin, you kept everyone calm and thinking. Tzvi Hirsch," and here the rebbe paused, "you had the courage to say you were wrong."

Tzvi Hirsch turned pink, but he grinned.

"And Nosson." Rabbi Feldman turned to the new boy. "Yesterday, some people thought you were the one who took the esrog. Today, your flax is the reason we know it is kosher."

Nosson looked down at his shoes, but he was smiling so widely that everybody could see.

"Now," said Rabbi Feldman, "all of you, downstairs, before Mrs. Rivkin finds six boys on the third floor before davening." He paused at the door. "And this afternoon, I have a surprise for the whole class."

"What kind of surprise?" asked Tzvi Hirsch.

The rebbe only smiled, and put the esrog back in its silver box.
