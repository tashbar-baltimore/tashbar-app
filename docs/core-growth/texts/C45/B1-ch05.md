# The Missing Esrog · Chapter 5: The Rebbe's Story

Rabbi Feldman did not sit down. He stood next to his desk with one hand resting on the white handkerchief, and for a long moment he did not say anything at all.

Yehuda had never seen his rebbe look like this. Rabbi Feldman always knew what to say. He knew what to say when someone dropped a whole tray of cups at the class siyum. He knew what to say when the bus broke down on the way to the zoo. But now he looked down at the handkerchief as if he were trying to find the right words written on it.

"This morning," he began, "I told you something. Who remembers what it was?"

Avrumi raised his hand. "You said, 'Look with your eyes, not with your fingers.'"

"I did." Rabbi Feldman nodded slowly. "I said that fingers leave oil, and oil can leave marks. That is true. And then, during recess, when the room was quiet and I was alone with the esrog, I wanted to see the bottom of it. Just for a second. So I picked it up." He held up his right thumb. "With this."

Nobody moved.

"When I put it back down, I saw a tiny dark spot. Right here." He pointed to a place on his own thumb, as if the esrog were still in his hand. "Exactly where my thumb had been."

Tzvi Hirsch let out a low whistle. Binyamin kicked his chair.

"I was very worried," the rebbe went on. "An esrog with a mark on it might not be hadar anymore. And this esrog belongs to my friend in Eretz Yisrael, who grew it on his own tree and sent it all the way here. So I wrapped it in my handkerchief, I grabbed my hat, and I ran upstairs to ask Rav Mizrachi. I was in such a hurry that I forgot my coat."

"The hat and the coat," whispered Yehuda. He wrote it down without looking.

"I wrote you a note," Rabbi Feldman said. "On a yellow paper. I stuck it right here on the desk: 'Back soon. The esrog is with me.' Did nobody see it?"

Twenty-two heads shook.

The rebbe frowned. He lifted the papers on his desk, then looked under the silver box. "Strange. I know I wrote it."

Dovid's hand shot into the air so fast that his chair squeaked. "Rebbe! The window was open! The wind blew papers off the desk. Some of them went behind the radiator!"

Rabbi Feldman raised an eyebrow, just the way Mr. Gomez had. "You boys have been busy."

There was a short, uncomfortable silence. Then Nosson spoke up, very quietly.

"Rebbe, we heard you. Upstairs. You said the boys must never find out what you did."

Every face in the room turned toward the front. For a moment, Yehuda was sure the rebbe would be angry that they had been on the third floor.

Instead, Rabbi Feldman laughed. It was a small, tired laugh, but it was real.

"Yes," he said. "I did say that. I was embarrassed. Here I was, teaching you not to touch the esrog, and I was the one who touched it. A rebbe is supposed to be an example." He sighed. "But Rav Mizrachi said something to me that I want you to hear too. He said, 'A rebbe who never makes a mistake teaches his talmidim one thing. A rebbe who makes a mistake and tells the truth about it teaches them two things.'"

He looked around the room, at each boy, one at a time.

"So I am telling you the truth. I made a mistake. I am sorry I scared you, and I am sorry I broke my own rule."

Yehuda felt something warm in his chest. He looked across the room at Tzvi Hirsch, who was looking at Nosson. Nosson was smiling.

"But Rebbe," said Avrumi, "is the esrog ruined?"

Rabbi Feldman put his hand on the handkerchief again. "That," he said, "is the question. Rav Mizrachi looked at it for a very long time, with a much stronger glass than ours. He said he wants to look again tomorrow morning, in the daylight, before he decides."

"Tomorrow?" groaned Tzvi Hirsch. "That's forever!"

"It is one night," said Rabbi Feldman. "Sukkos is the day after. We will find out together."

He unwrapped the handkerchief just enough for the class to see a curve of bright yellow peeking out, and then he wrapped it up again, very gently, and put it back in the silver box.

That afternoon, when the bell rang, Yehuda did not run for the bus. He waited until the room was almost empty. Then he walked over to the radiator by the window and knelt down.

The radiator was cooler now. Between the metal fins, far in the back, he could see the edges of the papers that had blown off the desk.

And between two of them was a corner of something small and yellow.
