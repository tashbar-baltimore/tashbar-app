# The Missing Esrog · Chapter 1: The Silver Box

On Monday morning, three days before Sukkos, Rabbi Feldman walked into the classroom carrying something small and shiny. He held it with both hands, the way Yehuda's Abba held a sefer Torah.

"Boys," he said, "please clear your desks. I want everyone to see this."

Twenty-two desks went quiet at once. Even Tzvi Hirsch Gold, who usually had a joke ready, closed his mouth.

The shiny thing was a silver box, about the size of a loaf of challah. Swirly leaves were carved into its lid. Rabbi Feldman set it down on his desk, lifted the lid, and folded back a soft white cloth.

Inside lay an esrog.

It was not like the esrogim the boys had seen at the store on Reisterstown Road. Those were lumpy and pale. This one was bright yellow, the color of sunshine on a Shabbos afternoon. Its skin was bumpy in a neat, even way, and at the top a tiny brown stem stood up straight, like a soldier.

"Whoa," whispered Dovid Lerner.

"This esrog," said Rabbi Feldman, "came all the way from Eretz Yisrael. A friend of mine picked it from his own tree. Today we are going to learn how to examine an esrog, to see if it is *hadar*, beautiful enough for the mitzvah."

He passed around a magnifying glass. One by one, each boy came up and leaned over the box. Rabbi Feldman would not let anyone touch the esrog. "Look with your eyes," he said, "not with your fingers. Fingers leave oil, and oil can leave marks. The glass helps us enjoy the bumps up close, but the halacha goes by plain eyes."

When it was Yehuda Benaim's turn, he looked for a long time. Yehuda always looked for a long time. In the back pocket of his pants he kept a small blue notebook, and in it he wrote down things other people did not notice. He noticed now that the esrog was a little wider at the bottom, like a pear. He noticed a tiny scratch on the silver lid. He noticed that the white cloth smelled sweet and sharp, like lemons.

"Nu, Yehuda," said Rabbi Feldman with a smile, "are you going to buy it?"

The class laughed, and Yehuda went back to his seat, his ears hot.

Avrumi Katz did not laugh. Avrumi was drawing. He drew everything: the classroom, the bus route, the inside of a clock. Now he was drawing the esrog in the corner of his math page, with tiny dots for the bumps. Near the stem he pressed one dot extra dark.

Then the bell rang for recess.

"Out you go," said Rabbi Feldman. "The esrog stays right here, and I will stay with it."

The boys grabbed their jackets. October in Baltimore can be warm in the sun and cold in the shade, and today the wind was blowing hard. Red and orange leaves were swirling over the yard like sprinkles on a cake.

Recess was the usual. Binyamin Yusupov sat on the bench and played chess against himself on a small magnetic board. Dovid tried to make a paper airplane fly into the wind, and the wind kept throwing it back at him. Tzvi Hirsch organized a game of punchball and lost the ball over the fence twice.

Only one boy stayed inside. His name was Nosson Abergel, and he was new. His family had moved from Montreal in the summer. He was quiet and polite, and he spoke with a little French in his voice. On the way out, Yehuda saw him walking toward the office stairs, holding something behind his back.

When the bell rang again, the boys ran back up the stairs, red-cheeked and loud. They burst into the room.

Then they stopped.

Rabbi Feldman was not there.

His black coat still hung on the hook by the door. But the hook next to it, where his hat always hung, was empty.

The silver box sat on the desk where he had left it. The lid was open.

Dovid reached it first. He looked inside and turned pale.

"It's empty," he said. "The esrog is gone."

Twenty-two boys crowded around the desk. The soft white cloth lay inside the box, folded neatly in half. There was nothing else. No esrog. No stem. Not even a crumb of yellow.

"Somebody took it!" Tzvi Hirsch said. "Somebody stole the rebbe's esrog!"

"Don't touch anything," said Yehuda quickly. He did not know why he said it. It was just what detectives said in books.

He leaned close to the box. The cloth still smelled of lemons, strong and fresh, as if the esrog had been lifted out only a few minutes ago. And the cloth was folded. Yehuda thought about that. If you were stealing something, would you stop to fold the cloth?

He took out his blue notebook and wrote three lines:

*Coat here. Hat gone.*
*Cloth folded. Smells fresh.*
*Who was inside at recess?*

Behind him, the boys were all talking at once.

"Maybe the rebbe went home sick."

"Without his coat? It's freezing!"

"Maybe a bird flew in the window and took it."

"Birds don't eat esrogim, Shimmy."

Binyamin Yusupov, calm as always, said, "We should tell the office."

But before anyone could move, the classroom door creaked. Every head turned.

It was not Rabbi Feldman.

It was Nosson Abergel, the new boy. He stood in the doorway with his hands behind his back. When he saw twenty-two faces staring at him and the empty silver box on the desk, his eyes went wide.

"What happened?" he asked.

Nobody answered.

Yehuda looked at Nosson's hands. They were behind his back. Why were they behind his back?

Yehuda's pencil stopped moving. Somewhere in the building, far above them, a door closed with a heavy thud.
