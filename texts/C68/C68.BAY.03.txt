title: Oyster Reefs: The Bay's Water Filter

An oyster does not look like much. It is a lumpy gray shell that sits on the bottom of the bay and never moves. It has no eyes and no legs. Yet this quiet creature does one of the most important jobs in the Chesapeake. It cleans the water.

An oyster feeds by pumping water through its body. Tiny hairs move the water over its gills, which catch bits of algae and floating dirt. The oyster eats the algae and wraps much of the rest in mucus, dropping it to the bottom. A single adult oyster can filter up to about 50 gallons of water a day. That is more than a bathtub full.

Now imagine millions of oysters working together. Scientists believe that before the big harvests of the 1800s, the oysters of the Chesapeake could filter a volume of water equal to the whole bay in a week or less. Early settlers wrote that the bay was crowded with oysters.
===
Oysters do not live alone. Baby oysters, called spat, need something hard to attach to, and their favorite place is the shell of another oyster. Over hundreds of years, generation after generation of oysters grew on top of one another and built huge mounds called reefs. Some old reefs rose so high that sailors had to steer around them.

A reef is like an underwater apartment building. Small fish, worms, crabs and many other creatures hide in its cracks and crevices. Bigger fish come to hunt there. A healthy reef is one of the busiest places in the bay. Reefs also break up waves and help protect the shoreline from wearing away.

In the late 1800s, oysters made the Chesapeake famous. Baltimore had dozens of packing houses, and oysters were shipped by train all over the country. Thousands of boats worked the bay. Maryland's harvest reached its peak in the 1880s. People thought the supply would never run out. They were wrong.
===
Maryland's oyster harvest, about how many bushels a year:

1880s (best years): about 15 million

1950s: about 2 million

1980s: about 1 million

2000s: about 100,000

2010s: about 300,000

These numbers are rounded, and the harvest went up and down from year to year. The chart shows a steep fall: the harvest in the 2000s was only about one hundredth of what it was in the 1880s. In the 2010s, the numbers began to rise a little, but they are still far below the old days.
===
What went wrong? The first cause was overharvesting. Boats dragged heavy iron dredges across the reefs, scooping up oysters faster than new ones could grow. The tall reefs were scraped flat, and flat shell beds are easily buried in mud.

Next came disease. Starting in the late 1950s, a parasite called MSX began killing oysters. Another disease, called Dermo, spread widely in the 1980s. Neither harms people, but both can wipe out whole beds of oysters.

Pollution made things worse. Too many nutrients from farms, lawns and cities feed thick blooms of algae. When the algae die and rot, they use up the oxygen in the water, and oysters and other creatures suffocate. Mud washed off the land also smothers oyster beds. Today, scientists estimate the bay has only about one percent of the oysters it once had.

The good news is that people are working hard to bring them back. In oyster gardening, families hang cages of young oysters from their docks, care for them for a year, and then return them to a reef. Maryland has also set aside oyster sanctuaries, areas where no harvesting is allowed. In Harris Creek, on the Eastern Shore, government scientists and their partners rebuilt about 350 acres of reef, one of the largest oyster restoration projects anywhere. Checks since then have found many oysters growing there.

Restoring reefs is slow work. But every oyster that grows is another small filter, cleaning the water of the Chesapeake one gallon at a time.
--- words
algae: tiny plants that float in water
spat: baby oysters that have attached to something hard
reefs: large mounds built from many layers of oyster shells
overharvesting: taking so many of a plant or animal that not enough are left to replace them
sanctuaries: protected places where no one is allowed to harvest
