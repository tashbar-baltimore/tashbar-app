title: The Telegram That Pulled America into War
grade: 7
kind: article
topic: Code breakers
source: original
---
In January 1917, Europe had been at war for two and a half years. Millions of soldiers had died in the First World War, fighting in muddy trenches across France and Belgium. Germany and its allies were on one side. Britain, France and Russia were on the other. The United States had stayed out of the fighting. Most Americans wanted to keep it that way.

The British desperately hoped America would join them. They had a secret weapon that might help: a small team of code breakers working in a room at the British navy's headquarters in London. The office was known simply as Room 40. Its staff included professors, language experts and puzzle lovers. Their job was to read the secret messages that Germany sent by telegraph and radio.

Early in the war, the British had done something clever. They had cut Germany's undersea telegraph cables. Germany could still send messages overseas, but only through routes that passed through places the British could listen to. Every message was in code, so the Germans felt safe. They did not know how much Room 40 could read.
===
On January 16, 1917, the German foreign minister, Arthur Zimmermann, sent a secret telegram to the German ambassador in Mexico. The British caught it, and two code breakers in Room 40, Nigel de Grey and William Montgomery, began to work on it. Only part of the message came clear at first, but what they read was astonishing.

Germany was about to begin attacking all ships in the waters around Britain, including ships from neutral countries like the United States. The Germans knew this might bring America into the war. So Zimmermann had a plan. If America joined the war, Germany would offer Mexico an alliance. Germany would send money, and Mexico would attack the United States. In return, after the war, Mexico would get back Texas, New Mexico and Arizona, lands it had lost to the United States seventy years earlier.

The British code breakers knew they were holding one of the most important messages of the war. But they also faced a hard problem.
===
If Britain showed the telegram to the Americans, the Germans would find out that their codes had been broken. They would change their codes, and Room 40 might never read their messages again. And the Americans might not even believe it. They might think the British had faked the telegram to drag America into the war.

The British found a clever solution. They learned that the telegram had been sent on to Mexico from the German embassy in Washington, over ordinary telegraph lines. A British agent in Mexico obtained a copy of that version from the telegraph office. Now Britain could say the message had been found in Mexico, not decoded in London. The Germans would blame a spy or a careless clerk, not a broken code.

In late February, the British gave the decoded telegram to the American government. President Woodrow Wilson was furious. On March 1, 1917, the telegram was printed in newspapers across the United States.
===
Many Americans at first refused to believe it. Some said it must be a British trick. Then something surprising happened. On March 3, Arthur Zimmermann himself admitted in public that the telegram was real. No one could doubt it any longer.

Anger swept the country. Germany had already begun sinking American ships, and now it was plotting to help another country attack American land. On April 2, President Wilson asked Congress to declare war on Germany, and on April 6, 1917, the United States entered the First World War. Fresh American soldiers and supplies helped bring the war to an end in November 1918.

Historians still argue about how much the telegram itself changed. Some say America would have joined the war anyway because of the attacks on its ships. Others say the telegram was the spark that turned public opinion. But nearly everyone agrees on one point: the work of a few code breakers in a quiet room in London helped change the course of a world war. And for many years afterward, the full story of how they did it stayed secret.
--- words
telegraph: a system for sending messages over wires using electric signals
neutral: not taking either side in a war
alliance: an agreement between countries to help each other
astonishing: very surprising
embassy: the office of one country's officials inside another country
