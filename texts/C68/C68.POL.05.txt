From The Beacon, opinion page. By Akiva Feldman, editor.

Last week, our class finished reading about the race to defeat polio. In the discussion afterward, Eli asked a question that stuck with me. "If Dr. Salk's vaccine was ready in 1953," he said, "why did they make children wait almost two more years? Every summer they waited, thousands more got sick."

It is a fair question, and I understand why Eli asked it. Some people at the time felt the same way. Parents wrote letters begging for the vaccine. Newspapers printed stories asking what the delay was for. When you know that a child somewhere is about to catch a terrible disease, waiting for more tests feels cruel.

But after reading more about what happened, I have come to the opposite view. I believe the waiting was not cruel at all. I believe it was the most responsible choice the scientists could have made, and that the story of polio proves it.
===
Here is my first reason. A vaccine is not given to a few sick people. It is given to millions of healthy children. If a medicine for a sick patient has a small risk, the patient may decide it is worth it. But when you give a shot to a healthy child, even a tiny risk is multiplied by millions. A mistake that harms one child in ten thousand would harm hundreds of children across the country. Doctors have to be far more certain about a vaccine than about almost any other medicine.

My second reason is what happened when something did go wrong. In April 1955, just days after the vaccine was approved, a problem was found at one of the companies making it. Some of its batches had not been treated properly, and the virus in them had not been fully killed. Thousands of children who received those batches became sick, about two hundred were left with some paralysis, and ten died. That company's vaccine was recalled at once, and soon the shots were paused until every factory was checked.

Think about that. Even after the biggest test in history, a mistake in one factory hurt hundreds of children. Now imagine how much worse it might have been if the vaccine had been rushed out in 1953, before anyone knew how to make it safely in huge amounts. The people who wanted to skip the testing meant well, but I think they did not see how much could go wrong.
===
My third reason is trust. A vaccine only works if people agree to take it. After the factory mistake, many parents were afraid. What brought them back? The evidence. Scientists could point to the giant field trial, with its careful records from almost two million children, and show that the vaccine itself was safe and that the problem had come from one company's mistakes. New rules were written so that every batch would be tested before it left the factory. Within two years, cases of polio in America dropped by more than three quarters.

Without that careful testing, I do not think parents would have trusted the vaccine again. And without their trust, polio might still be with us.

I want to be fair to the other side. The people who wanted the vaccine sooner were not foolish. They had seen what polio did. Some of them had children in leg braces or in iron lungs. Their impatience came from love. I just think that love for children is exactly the reason to test carefully, not the reason to skip it.
===
When I told this to Eli, he thought for a moment and then said, "So the waiting was a kind of protection too." I think that is right.

Rabbi Rosen once told our class that a wise person thinks about where his actions will lead before he takes them. The polio scientists did that. They felt the pressure of every summer that passed, and they still insisted on proof. That is why, when the vaccine finally arrived, it could be trusted.

Next time someone says that a new medicine or a new idea is taking too long, I hope we remember the Polio Pioneers. Sometimes the fastest way to help people is to make sure that you are right first.
--- words
responsible: doing what is right and careful, and accepting the results
multiplied: made many times bigger
batches: amounts of something made at one time
impatience: not wanting to wait
insisted: said firmly that something must happen
