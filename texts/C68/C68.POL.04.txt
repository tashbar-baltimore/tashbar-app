title: The Polio Pioneers: The Biggest Test in History

On the morning of April 26, 1954, a line of second graders waited outside the nurse's office at an elementary school in McLean, Virginia. Some of them were nervous. A few were crying. One by one, they rolled up their sleeves and received a shot. When it was over, each child got a lollipop and a little button that read "Polio Pioneer."

Those children were the first of almost two million children who took part in the largest medical test in American history. They were helping to answer one question: did Jonas Salk's new vaccine really work?

Salk had already tried his vaccine on a few hundred people, including his own wife and three sons. None of them became sick, and their blood showed the defenders the body makes against polio. But a small test could not prove that the vaccine would protect children from the real disease. For that, scientists needed a huge test, and they needed to design it very carefully.
===
Here was the problem. Polio was frightening, but in any one year, only a small number of children caught the paralyzing form. If scientists gave the vaccine to a few thousand children and none of them got polio, that would prove nothing. Most of them would not have gotten polio anyway. To see a real difference, the test needed hundreds of thousands of children.

There was a second problem. Polio struck some towns hard one summer and left them almost untouched the next. If vaccinated children lived in one place and unvaccinated children lived in another, any difference could be caused by the place, not the vaccine.

The man chosen to solve these problems was Dr. Thomas Francis Jr., a respected scientist at the University of Michigan. He had once been Salk's teacher. Francis insisted that the test be run by an independent team, not by Salk himself, so that no one could say the inventor had judged his own work.
===
Francis designed the test with a powerful tool called a placebo. In many areas, half of the children received the real vaccine, and half received a shot that looked exactly the same but contained nothing that could protect them. The bottles were marked only with code numbers. Neither the children, nor their parents, nor the doctors giving the shots knew who got which. Only when the test was finished would the code be opened.

Why go to so much trouble? Because people's hopes can change what they see. A doctor who knew a child had received the real vaccine might, without meaning to, describe a mild illness differently. By keeping everyone in the dark, Francis made sure that the only difference between the two groups was the vaccine itself. This kind of test, where neither the patients nor the doctors know who got what, is called a double-blind study.

The work was enormous. About 20,000 doctors and public health workers, 40,000 nurses and about 200,000 volunteers took part. Teachers kept records, mothers drove children to clinics and the March of Dimes paid the bills with money collected one dime at a time. Each child needed three shots over several weeks, and blood samples were tested in laboratories across the country.
===
Then everyone had to wait. Polio season came and went that summer. Francis and his team collected the records of every child who became sick, checked which code number each one had received and counted again and again. The work took almost a year.

On April 12, 1955, reporters crowded into a hall at the University of Michigan. Francis stepped to the microphone and read the results. The vaccine was safe. And it worked: it prevented most cases of paralytic polio, in the strongest results between 80 and 90 percent of them.

Across America, the news spread within minutes. Church bells rang. Store owners put signs in their windows. In classrooms, teachers stopped the lesson to tell the children. Within hours, the government approved the vaccine, and soon millions of children were lining up for their shots.

The Polio Pioneers had done something remarkable. Most of them were six, seven and eight years old. Many of them received a shot that turned out to contain nothing at all, and they never complained, because the test needed them too. Without the children who got the placebo, nobody could have known what the vaccine really did. Every one of them helped to protect the children who came after them, including, perhaps, your own grandparents.
--- words
placebo: a pretend medicine that looks real but has nothing in it that works
independent: not controlled by the person whose work is being checked
paralytic: causing a person to lose the power to move
enormous: very, very large
remarkable: worth noticing because it is so unusual or impressive
