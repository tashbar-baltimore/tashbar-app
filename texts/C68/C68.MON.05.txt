A Beacon feature by Benny Okafor-Levine.

Every Sunday night, after the dishes are done, my father takes out a yellow pad and a calculator and sits at the kitchen table. For years I thought he was doing something boring. This year, he invited me to sit with him. I learned that a budget is not boring at all. It is a plan for how a family turns its money into the life it wants to live.

A budget starts with one number: how much money comes in each month. That is called income. For most families, income is the pay from one or two jobs. My father showed me our monthly income on the first line of the pad. Then he drew a line under it and said, "Everything below this line has to fit inside this number. If it does not fit, something has to change."

The first thing below the line, before anything else, is maaser. Many Jewish families give one tenth of their income to tzedakah. "We do not give maaser from what is left over," my father said. "It comes first, so it is never forgotten." In our house, maaser goes to the yeshiva, to a family in need, and to the gemach on Linden Street.
===
Next come the needs: the things a family must pay for. Rent or the mortgage on the house. Food, including Shabbos and Yom Tov. Tuition for the children. The electric bill, the gas bill and the water bill. Health insurance. The car payment, and gas for the car. My father wrote each need on its own line, with the amount next to it. When he added them up, I was surprised how much of our income was already spoken for.

Then come the wants: the things that make life more pleasant but that a family can live without. A new couch. Pizza on Motzaei Shabbos. A summer trip. My father did not cross these out. "Wants are not bad," he said. "Hashem gave us a beautiful world to enjoy. But wants come after needs, and they have to fit inside what is left." He wrote down a small amount for each want, and wrote "if there is room" next to the bigger ones.

The last line is savings. My father puts a little aside every single month, even when it is hard. Some of it is for things the family knows are coming, like a bar mitzvah or a new roof. Some of it is an emergency fund, for things nobody can plan, like a broken refrigerator or a hospital bill. "A family without savings is one surprise away from trouble," he told me. "A family with savings can handle the surprise and sleep at night."
===
I asked my father what happens when the numbers do not fit. He showed me last winter's pad. The heating bill was much higher than he had planned, because it was a cold winter. So the family moved money from the "wants" lines to cover it. The summer trip became a day trip. Nobody was happy about it, but nobody was in debt, either. "That is what a budget is for," he said. "It lets you choose what to give up, instead of having the choice made for you."

He also showed me something I did not expect. Every month, he compares the budget, what he planned, with what the family really spent. If the family spent more on food than planned three months in a row, he does not just sigh. He changes the plan, or he changes how we shop. A budget, he says, is not a promise you make once. It is a conversation you have every month.

I asked him why he does not just keep the numbers in his head. He laughed. "Everybody thinks he remembers where his money goes," he said. "Almost nobody does. Coffee here, a gift there, a pizza on a busy night. Each one is small. Together they can be the size of the electric bill. When it is written down, I cannot fool myself."

Here is our family's budget, in round numbers, with my father's permission. It shows what share of each dollar goes to each part.

Maaser: 10 cents of every dollar.
Housing: 30 cents.
Tuition: 20 cents.
Food: 15 cents.
Bills, insurance and the car: 15 cents.
Savings: 5 cents.
Wants: 5 cents.

When I looked at that list, I understood why my father says, "Every dollar needs a job." Before Sunday nights at the kitchen table, I thought money just disappeared. Now I know where it goes, and why. Next month, my father says, I am going to make a budget of my own, for my allowance. I already know what my first line will be. It is the same line my father writes first, every Sunday night, before anything else.
--- words
income: the money that comes in, such as pay from a job
mortgage: a loan to buy a house, paid back each month for many years
emergency: something serious that happens suddenly and needs action at once
budget: a plan for how money will be spent and saved
