For hundreds of years, the people who lived around the Bay of Naples, in the south of Italy, thought of Mount Vesuvius as a green and friendly mountain. Vineyards climbed its sides. Its soil, made rich by old ash, grew some of the best grapes and vegetables in the Roman world. Towns like Pompeii and Herculaneum spread out below it, with busy markets, paved streets and houses with gardens. Nobody alive had ever seen the mountain erupt. Most people did not know it was a volcano at all.

There were warnings. In the year 62, a strong earthquake shook the area and damaged many buildings. Small earthquakes came again and again over the next seventeen years. Springs dried up. People repaired their houses and went on with their lives. They did not know that deep under the mountain, magma was rising and pressure was building, the way it does in a bottle of soda that has been shaken with the cap still on.
===
In the year 79, on a day in late summer or early fall, the cap came off. Around noon, Vesuvius exploded. A huge column of gas, ash and broken rock shot up into the sky, perhaps twenty miles high. Across the bay, a teenager named Pliny watched it from his uncle's house. Years later, he wrote that the cloud was shaped like a tall pine tree, with a trunk that rose straight up and then spread out into branches at the top. His letter is the only eyewitness account that has survived, and scientists still use his name for this kind of eruption.

For hours, the cloud rained pumice, light, bubbly rock full of holes, and ash down on Pompeii. Roofs collapsed under the weight. Many people fled. Others stayed, hoping it would pass, or hid in their houses to wait it out.

Then, during the night and the next morning, came the deadliest part. The tall column could not hold itself up any longer, and it collapsed. Avalanches of burning gas, ash and rock raced down the sides of the mountain faster than a car on a highway. These flows are called pyroclastic flows, and nothing can outrun them. They reached Herculaneum first, then Pompeii. Within a day, both towns were buried under many feet of ash and rock.
===
For more than 1,600 years, the towns lay hidden. Farmers planted on top of them. People forgot that they had ever been there. Then, in the 1700s, workers digging a well struck the remains of a building. Digging went on, slowly, for the next two hundred years, and it still goes on today.

What the diggers found was a whole Roman town, frozen at one moment in time. There were bakeries with loaves of bread still in the ovens, turned to charcoal. There were shop signs, wall paintings, jars of food and the ruts of cart wheels in the stone streets. In the hardened ash, there were hollow spaces where people had been caught by the flows. Archaeologists poured plaster into those spaces and made casts that still show how people spent their last moments.

Vesuvius has erupted many times since then, most recently in 1944. Today, about three million people live near it, more than in all the old Roman towns together. Scientists watch the mountain every day with instruments that measure earthquakes, gases and even the smallest swelling of the ground. Italy has a plan to move hundreds of thousands of people out of the danger zone if the warnings come.

The people of Pompeii had warnings too. They simply did not know how to read them. That may be the most important lesson the mountain teaches: a warning only helps if someone understands what it means.
--- words
erupt: to burst out with gas, ash and melted rock
eyewitness: a person who saw something happen with his own eyes
pumice: light rock full of tiny holes, made in an eruption
pyroclastic: made of burning gas, ash and rock from a volcano
archaeologists: scientists who dig up and study things from long ago
casts: shapes made by pouring a liquid into a hollow space
