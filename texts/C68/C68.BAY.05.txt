The question: Should Maryland set strict limits on how many oysters and crabs watermen may take each year?

This week, The Beacon asked two people who care deeply about the Chesapeake Bay to answer that question. One studies the bay. The other works on it every day. Read both views carefully before you decide.
===
View A: Strict limits protect the bay and its future

By Dr. Ari Levinson, a marine scientist

Everyone agrees that the Chesapeake is less healthy than it was in 1900. Everyone also agrees that oysters help clean it, because an adult oyster can filter up to about 50 gallons of water a day. My claim is simple: if we want a cleaner bay and a fishing industry that lasts, Maryland must set strict limits on how many oysters and crabs are taken.

First, the history is clear. In their best years in the 1880s, Maryland watermen harvested about 15 million bushels of oysters a year. Few limits stopped them, and they scraped the reefs flat. Today the bay holds only about one percent of the oysters it once had. Every oyster taken out is one less filter in the water.

Second, limits work. In 2008, the crab population had fallen to a worrying low. Maryland and Virginia cut the number of female crabs that could be taken. Within a few years, scientists counted many more crabs in their winter survey. Protecting the mothers protected the next generation.

Third, sanctuaries where no harvesting is allowed are already showing results. In places like Harris Creek, rebuilt reefs are filling with young oysters.

Some watermen say pollution, not harvesting, is the real problem. Pollution is serious, and we must fight it too. But we cannot control the weather or wipe out disease overnight. We can control how many animals we take. A waterman with smaller catches today will have a bay worth working tomorrow.
===
View B: Limits hurt families and miss the real problem

By Wade Thomas, a third-generation waterman from Tilghman Island

My grandfather tonged oysters from the same creek I work today. I agree with the scientists that the bay is dirtier than it was in 1900, and I know better than anyone that oysters clean the water. But my claim is this: strict limits punish watermen for a problem we did not mainly cause. The bigger danger is pollution from farms and cities.

First, look at the water itself. Each summer, low-oxygen areas called dead zones spread across parts of the bay. They are caused by too many nutrients, like the fertilizer that washes off fields and lawns and the waste that flows from cities. The Chesapeake Bay Program reports that farms are the largest single source of that pollution. Oysters and crabs cannot thrive in water without oxygen, no matter how few we catch.

Second, disease did much of the damage. MSX and Dermo killed huge numbers of oysters, even on beds where nobody was working.

Third, limits hurt real families. Licenses, fuel and boats cost more every year. When catches are cut, some watermen have to sell their boats, and an old way of life on islands like ours slowly disappears.

Dr. Levinson points to the crab rebound after 2008. I respect the science, but crab numbers have gone up and down many times since, even with limits in place. If limits alone were the answer, the bay would be healthy by now. Clean up the water first, and the oysters and crabs will come back.
===
A note from the editor

Both writers love the Chesapeake, and they agree on more than you might expect. Both say oysters filter the water. Both say the bay is less clean than it was in 1900. Where they differ is on the main cause of the problem and the best solution.

As you compare the two views, ask yourself a few questions. What is each writer's claim? Which reasons are backed by evidence, and which are opinion? How does each writer answer the other side? Is it possible that both are partly right?

Write to us with your own view. We will print the most thoughtful letters in our next issue.

Akiva Feldman, Editor, The Beacon
--- words
marine: having to do with the sea
claim: the main idea a writer is trying to prove
generation: all the people or animals born around the same time
thrive: to grow strong and healthy
evidence: facts that show whether something is true
