Reb Feivel let them borrow ten envelopes, wrapped in a clean towel and tucked into a shoe box, on one condition: "You bring back every one, and you bring back the friend's name, or you bring back an honest 'we don't know.' Nothing in between."

Back in the closet, Akiva cleared the long table. He wiped the whiteboard as clean as it would go, which was not very clean, and drew his line down the middle again. PROVED on the left. GUESSED on the right.

Then they looked at the envelopes. For a long time, nobody spoke.

They were plain, small envelopes, the kind you could buy in any store in 1953. On the front of each, in pencil, someone had written a number and nothing else. 18. Inside each, Reb Feivel had said, there had once been a ten, a five and three ones.

"No name, no stamp, no address," said Yoni. "He walked it over himself."

"Which means he lived close to the bakery," said Gavriel. He wrote it on the GUESSED side.

Eli was flipping through the envelopes fast. "This is impossible. There is nothing here. A number. We should just write a story about the mystery and leave it. 'The Unknown Friend.' People love that."
===
"Reb Feivel said a name or an honest 'we don't know,'" said Akiva. "We are not at 'we don't know' yet. We have been looking for five minutes."

Benny had taken one envelope to the window. In the gray afternoon light, he was looking not at the front, but at the corners. Most of the envelopes were plain white. But this one, and two others, had something along the inside of the flap: a thin line of printing, mostly torn away where the envelope had been opened.

"Gavriel," he said. "Can your camera see small things?"

Gavriel put the envelope under the desk lamp and took a picture, close. Then he made it bigger on the screen. Most of the letters were gone with the torn paper. What was left was this:

...DEL'S  ...ORING  ...BARD ST.

"It is a business envelope," said Benny. "Someone used the envelopes from his work."

Eli leaned over the screen. "...ORING. Flooring? Motoring? Coloring?"

"...BARD ST," said Yoni. "There aren't many streets in Baltimore that end in bard. Lombard Street." He sat up straight. "My Zaidy grew up near Lombard Street. Corned beef on every corner, he says. The old Jewish neighborhood."
===
Gavriel was looking at the fronts again. "There is one more thing. Look at the eights." He lined up five envelopes in a row. "And this one." He pointed to an envelope where the writer had pressed hard and the pencil had smeared. Under the 18, very small, someone had added a date: 7/5/53. The seven had a short line across its middle, the way some people write a seven in Europe.

"My grandmother writes her sevens like that," said Benny quietly. "She learned to write in Nigeria, in a British school. My father says lots of people from Europe write them that way too."

Akiva wrote on the PROVED side: The envelopes came from a business on a street ending in -bard. And: The writer crosses his sevens.

Then, under GUESSED: Lombard Street? A man who learned to write in Europe?

Eli was drumming his fingers on the table. "Okay. I have it. ...DEL'S. Mandel's? Handel's? Rosendel's? And ...ORING. Tailoring. A tailor! Tailors use envelopes for their bills. Let's call it: the friend was a tailor named Mandel on Lombard Street. We put it on the front page. Done."

"That is three guesses glued together," said Akiva.

"Good guesses!"

"Maybe. Maybe they are all right. But if one is wrong, we print a stranger's name on the front page as the man who saved the yeshiva, and his real family reads it and knows it was not him. Or the real friend's family reads it and knows it was not him either."
===
Eli opened his mouth to argue. Then he closed it, and Benny saw something change in his face, the same thing he had seen the day Eli learned that Mr. Dahan's keys did not fit the lock.

"Fine," said Eli. "Then how do we turn a guess into a proof?"

Akiva smiled. It was the first time all afternoon. "That," he said, "is the best question anyone has asked in this closet."

They made a list. Yoni would ask his Zaidy about the old businesses on Lombard Street. Gavriel would go to the big library downtown, where Mr. Gross said they kept old city directories, thick books that listed every business on every street, year by year. Akiva would write to Reb Feivel to ask if his father had ever mentioned a tailor. And Benny would do the hardest job of all.

"Find me one more piece of writing by the same hand," said Akiva. "Not on these envelopes. Somewhere else. With a crossed seven."

Benny nodded. He had no idea where to look. But for the first time since September, he felt as if the Beacon was his newspaper too.
