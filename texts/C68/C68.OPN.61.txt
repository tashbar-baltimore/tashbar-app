To the newest reporter at The Beacon,

Welcome to the newsroom. It is a supply closet with a window, and it is the best room in the building.

Here is the first thing every Beacon reporter learns. A good reader is like a good detective. He notices. He wonders. And then, before he says one word out loud, he proves it.

Anyone can say, "I think the old man is lonely." A reader says, "I think the old man is lonely, and here is the line that shows it." That line is called evidence. When the author says something outright, you can point to it. When the author only hints, you put the clue together with what you already know about people. That is called an inference, and you have to explain it.

In this unit you will learn five small skills, one at a time:
how to back up an answer with the words of the text;
how to tell what is said outright from what is only hinted;
how to explain the steps of your thinking;
how to pick the quote that proves it best;
and how to reread one paragraph and catch what you missed.

You will use them on a real mystery, The Beacon and the Locked Archive, and on a famous old story, "After Twenty Years," where one small match changes everything.

Take your time. Detectives are patient. At the end there is a check on a story you have never seen, and you will be ready.

Your editor,
Akiva Feldman
