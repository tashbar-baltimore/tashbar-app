title: How a Bank Lends
grade: 8
kind: article
topic: Money and trust
source: original
---
When a family puts money in a bank, where does the money go? Many people picture a giant vault, with every family's dollars stacked on their own shelf. That picture is wrong. Most of the money in a bank is not sitting in the bank at all. It has been lent to other people.

Here is how it works. Suppose a hundred families each keep one thousand dollars in a bank. That is one hundred thousand dollars. The bank knows that on a normal day, only a few families will come to take money out. Most of the money will sit untouched for months. So the bank keeps a part of it ready, in cash and in its own account, and lends much of the rest to people who need it: a young couple buying a house, a baker buying a new oven, a town building a school.

The people who borrow must pay the money back, and they must pay extra for the use of it. That extra is called interest. If the baker borrows ten thousand dollars at six percent interest for one year, he must pay back ten thousand six hundred dollars. The bank uses part of that interest to pay its workers and its bills, and it pays a smaller part to the families who keep their money there. That is why a savings account grows a little each year.
===
Lending helps a town grow. Without loans, the young couple might have to save for twenty years before they could buy a house. The baker might never buy the oven that lets him hire two more workers. Money that would sit still in a drawer is put to work. The borrower gets what he needs now, and he pays for it over time.

But lending has a risk. What if many families come to take out their money on the same day? The bank does not have it all on hand, because it lent much of it out. If people hear a rumor that a bank is in trouble, they may rush to take their money out before it is gone. This is called a bank run. Each frightened family that runs to the bank makes the trouble worse, because the bank has even less cash for the next family. A bank that was healthy on Monday can fail by Friday, only because people stopped trusting it.

That is what happened to thousands of American banks in the early 1930s, during the Great Depression. Families lost their savings, and many lost them forever. To stop this from happening again, Congress created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation in 1933. Today, it promises that if an insured bank fails, each depositor will get back his money, up to two hundred fifty thousand dollars at each bank. Because families know their savings are safe, they have no reason to run. The promise itself keeps the panic away.
===
A bank also has to decide whom to trust. Before it lends, it asks questions. Does this person have a steady job? Has he paid back loans before? Is he asking for an amount he can repay? A bank that lends to people who cannot pay will lose its depositors' money. So a careful bank checks first and lends second.

The bank also charges more interest to a borrower it trusts less. A borrower who has always paid on time may get a loan at five percent. A borrower who has missed payments may be charged ten percent, or may be turned away. In this way, the interest rate is a kind of price tag on trust.

Borrowers must be careful too. A loan is a promise about the future. A family that borrows too much may spend years paying interest instead of saving. The best borrowers ask the same questions a careful bank asks: Do I really need this now? Can I pay it back on time? What will it cost me in the end?

The Torah adds another layer to all of this. A Jew may not lend to another Jew with interest, or borrow from him with interest. Instead, the Torah tells us to lend to a fellow Jew in need freely, as a kindness. When Jewish businesses need loans to grow, they follow special rules, set out by poskim, so that the loan becomes a kind of partnership instead of a loan with interest. A rav guides a business owner in how to arrange it. These rules remind us that money is not only a matter of numbers. It is also a matter of how we treat each other.

In the end, a bank is a machine built out of trust. Families trust the bank to keep their money safe. The bank trusts borrowers to pay it back. Borrowers trust that the bank will not suddenly demand all of its money tomorrow. When that trust holds, a whole town can build houses, open shops and grow. When the trust breaks, even a strong bank can fall in a week.
--- words
interest: extra money paid for the use of borrowed money
depositors: people who keep their money in a bank
rumor: a story that spreads from person to person, which may not be true
insured: protected by a promise to pay back what is lost
poskim: rabbis who decide how halachah applies to real questions
