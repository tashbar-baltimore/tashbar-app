title: The Code That Was Never Broken: The Navajo Code Talkers

During the Second World War, American forces fought Japan across the islands of the Pacific Ocean. Battles moved fast, and orders had to be passed by radio: where to send supplies, where enemy guns were hidden, when to attack. The Japanese listened to every word. Their soldiers included men who had studied in America and spoke excellent English. Many American codes were broken, and messages that took hours to encode and decode could arrive too late to help.

The United States Marine Corps found an answer in a place few people expected: the language of the Navajo people of the American Southwest.

In 1942, the Navajo language was spoken by only a few tens of thousands of people, almost all of them living on or near the Navajo Nation in Arizona, New Mexico and Utah. It had no written alphabet in common use. Its sounds and grammar were very different from English, Japanese or any European language. A single change in the tone of a vowel could change the meaning of a word. Outside the Navajo Nation, almost no one in the world could understand it.
===
In the spring of 1942, the Marines recruited twenty-nine young Navajo men. Some were still teenagers. Their first job was not fighting. It was building a code.

The code had two parts. The first part gave Navajo words to military terms that did not exist in the Navajo language. A submarine became besh-lo, "iron fish". A tank became chay-da-gahi, "tortoise". A fighter plane became da-he-tih-hi, "hummingbird", and a bomber became jay-sho, "buzzard". The pictures were easy for a Navajo speaker to remember and impossible for an outsider to guess.

The second part was an alphabet for spelling words that had no code word, such as the names of islands. Each English letter was given a Navajo word for an animal or object. For example, wol-la-chee, "ant", stood for A. To make the code harder to break, each common letter was later given several different Navajo words, so the same letter would not keep showing up in the same way. This defeated the frequency analysis that code breakers had used for centuries.

The code talkers memorized everything. Nothing was carried in writing on the battlefield.
===
The code talkers proved their worth in some of the hardest battles of the war. The most famous was the battle of Iwo Jima in February 1945. In the first two days, six code talkers sent and received more than eight hundred messages, all without a single mistake. A Marine signal officer, Major Howard Connor, later said, "Were it not for the Navajos, the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima."

The code was also fast. A message that took a machine half an hour to encode and decode could be passed by two code talkers in a few minutes. And the Japanese never broke it. Japanese code breakers, who had cracked other American codes, could make nothing of the strange sounds on the radio.

By the end of the war, about four hundred Navajo men had served as code talkers. Many of them had grown up facing unfair treatment from the same government they now served. Some had been punished in boarding schools as children for speaking Navajo. Now that same language helped protect thousands of American lives.
===
When the code talkers came home, they could not tell anyone what they had done. The government kept the code secret in case it was needed again. Their families, their neighbors and even their own children did not know. Some veterans went for decades without being thanked.

The secret was finally lifted in 1968. Slowly, the code talkers began to receive the honor they deserved. In 2001, the original twenty-nine received the Congressional Gold Medal, one of the highest awards the United States gives to civilians. By then, only a few of them were still alive to receive it.

The story of the Navajo code talkers shows that the strongest code is not always a clever machine. Sometimes it is a living language, carried in the minds of people who are proud of it. It also raises a hard question: when a secret helps win a war, how long should it be kept, and what does it cost the people who must keep it?
