From The Beacon, debate page. This week's question: After a war is over, should a government keep its code-breaking secrets, or tell the public? Two writers answer.

KEEP THEM, BUT NOT FOREVER. By Eli Mizrahi.

I believe a government should keep its code-breaking secrets while they still protect people, and then it should tell the full story. A secret has a job to do. When the job is done, keeping the secret can do more harm than good.

My first reason is that some secrets really do keep working after a war ends. The Navajo code was kept secret after the Second World War because the military thought it might need the code again. That was a fair worry at the time. A code that the enemy has never heard of is a valuable tool, and announcing it to the world would have thrown that tool away.
===
My second reason is that secrets kept too long hurt the people who earned the credit. The Bletchley Park code breakers stayed silent for about thirty years. Many grew old, and some died, without anyone knowing what they had done. The Navajo code talkers came home to no parades. Some of them waited more than fifty years for a medal. A country that asks people to serve should not leave them forgotten once their secret no longer protects anyone.

My third reason is that the public learns from the truth. The history of the Second World War was taught for thirty years without Enigma in it. Students and historians had the wrong picture of why some battles were won and lost. When the secret came out in the 1970s, whole chapters of history books had to be rewritten.

Telling the story can also be done wisely. When the Enigma secret was finally told, the machines it described were long out of use, and no living soldier was put at risk. When the code talkers were honored, the Navajo code was no longer needed. In both cases, the truth came out safely. The only problem was that it came out years later than it needed to.

Some people say that any secret, once told, might help a future enemy. I understand that worry. But a code that has already been replaced cannot help anyone. Governments should ask a simple question every few years: is this secret still doing its job? If not, it is time to tell the story, and to thank the people in it.
===
TELL EVERYTHING, RIGHT AWAY. By a reader, Mordechai Stern.

Eli's article is far too soft on governments. Every government that hides its code-breaking is simply covering up its own sneaky tricks. These shameful secrets are kept for one reason only: the people in charge do not want us to know what they are really doing.

Just look at what happened with Enigma. The British hid it for thirty years. That proves that every government will hide everything it can for as long as it can.

Some people say a secret code might be needed again. That is ridiculous. Nobody uses codes anymore. Today everything is done by computers, so old secrets cannot possibly matter to anyone.

And think about how much money is spent on secret code-breaking offices. Millions of dollars go to people who sit in locked rooms, and the public never even finds out what they do. If the work were done in the open, everyone could see whether it was worth paying for.

The code talkers deserved their thanks, and they had to wait for decades. Any honest person can see that the only fair rule is to tell everything the day a war ends. Anyone who disagrees just does not care about the heroes.
