title: The Last Daf
grade: 6
kind: story
topic: What the story is really about
source: original
---
On the night after his grandfather's levayah, Rafi Toledano made a promise. Saba had learned Masechta Berachos with him every Thursday night for two years, one daf at a time, at the kitchen table, with a glass of mint tea that Saba always forgot to drink. They had stopped at daf forty. On Saba's shloshim, Rafi decided, there would be a siyum. He would finish the masechta himself, all twenty-four dapim that were left, in thirty days.

"That is a lot of Gemara," said his father carefully.

"Less than one daf a day," said Rafi. "I did the math."

For the first week, the math worked. Rafi learned every night after supper, and on the eighth night he was on daf forty-eight, one daf ahead of his plan. He wrote the numbers on a card and taped it inside his Gemara. He felt strong and grown up and very close to Saba.

Then he got the flu.
===
For six days, Rafi lay in bed with a fever, too dizzy to read a line. When he came back to his Gemara, the card inside it was a scold. He was now three dapim behind, and the shloshim was sixteen days away.

So he started to skim. He read the Gemara fast, skipped the Rashi, and moved on whenever a piece got hard. In three nights he covered six dapim. On the fourth night, he closed the Gemara and realized that he could not remember a single thing he had learned. Not one question, not one answer. He sat at the kitchen table, where Saba used to sit, and felt worse than he had with the fever. The next night, he went back to daf forty-eight and started those six dapim again, slowly this time.

The next morning, in the beis medrash, a fourth-grader named Ezra Halabi came over with his Gemara open. Ezra was small for his age and had just started learning Gemara that year. His rebbe had told the boys they could ask an older boy for help.

"Rafi, can you help me with this line?" Ezra asked. "I've read it ten times and it still doesn't make sense."

Rafi almost said no. He did not have time. Every minute he gave Ezra was a minute he was not catching up. But Ezra was standing there holding his Gemara the way Rafi used to hold his, with both hands, a little too tight. So Rafi sat down.
===
It took twenty minutes to work through three lines. Ezra asked a question about every word. Twice, Rafi did not know the answer, and they had to look in the Rashi together, the Rashi Rafi had been skipping all week. When they finally understood it, Ezra laughed out loud, and the boy at the next shtender told them to be quiet.

"Can we do more tomorrow?" Ezra asked.

Rafi meant to say, "Maybe." Instead he said, "Same time."

They met every morning before davening. Ezra was slow, and Rafi had to explain each piece twice, sometimes three times. But something strange happened. Explaining a piece to Ezra made Rafi understand it better than he ever had alone. And at night, when he opened his own Gemara, he no longer felt like skimming. He went slower. He read the Rashi. He even wrote a question in the margin, the way Saba used to.

He also fell further behind. By the week of the shloshim, Rafi had learned twelve of the twenty-four dapim. There was no way to finish.
===
The night before the shloshim, Rafi told his father the truth. He expected his father to be disappointed. Instead, his father listened, and then asked a question.

"How many of those twelve dapim can you explain?"

Rafi thought about it. "All of them," he said slowly. "Really explain. And Ezra's three lines too."

His father was quiet for a moment. "Saba learned with you for two years," he said, "and the two of you learned thirty-eight dapim. Did he ever once tell you to go faster?"

Rafi shook his head. Saba never hurried. Saba would spend a whole Thursday night on one Tosafos and call it a good night.

There was no siyum at the shloshim. Instead, when the men had davened and eaten and the room was quiet, Rafi stood up and said over a short piece from daf fifty-one, the first piece he had ever really understood all by himself, Rashi and all. His voice shook at the start, and then it did not. When he finished, an old man from Saba's shul came over and shook his hand for a long time.

"Your grandfather," the man said, "used to say the same thing about that line. Almost the same words."

Rafi finished the masechta three months later, on a Thursday night, at the kitchen table. Ezra came for the siyum. So did a glass of mint tea, which Rafi, for once, remembered to drink.
--- words
levayah: a funeral
shloshim: the thirtieth day after a person passes away
siyum: a celebration when a whole masechta is finished
daf: one page of the Gemara, front and back
scold: words that blame or criticize
margin: the empty space at the edge of a page
