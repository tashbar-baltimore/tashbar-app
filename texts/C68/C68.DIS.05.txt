A REPORT FOR STUDENTS, written in the style of an engineers' report. It tells the true story of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in the state of Washington, which fell into the water in 1940, and what engineers learned from it.

SECTION 1: SUMMARY. On November 7, 1940, the main span of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge twisted itself apart in a steady wind of about 40 miles per hour and fell into the water below. The bridge had been open for only four months. No person was killed. The failure was caused by the shape of the bridge's deck, which was too thin and too flexible for the wind at that place. The lessons from this failure changed the way bridges are designed all over the world.

SECTION 2: THE BRIDGE. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge was a suspension bridge: its road hung from steel cables strung between two tall towers. When it opened on July 1, 1940, its main span was the third longest in the world. To save money, its designer chose a deck that was very narrow and very shallow, with solid steel girders along its sides only eight feet deep. Most long suspension bridges of that time had much deeper, open frameworks of steel, called trusses, under the road. The thin deck looked graceful, and it cost far less to build. Some engineers questioned the design before it was built, but their concerns were set aside.
===
SECTION 3: WHAT WITNESSES SAW. Even while the bridge was being built, workers noticed that its deck rose and fell in waves when the wind blew, even in a gentle breeze. Drivers said that cars ahead of them disappeared and reappeared as the road rippled. People began to call the bridge "Galloping Gertie," and some drove across it just for the thrill. The engineers tried several fixes, including extra cables to hold the deck down, but the motion continued.

On the morning of November 7, the wind was steady but not unusual. The deck began to move in a new way. Instead of rising and falling, it began to twist, one side going up while the other went down, like a ribbon being turned. The twisting grew larger and larger. The bridge was closed to traffic. One driver who was caught on the bridge had to leave his car and crawl back to the shore on his hands and knees, as the road tilted under him. A professor of engineering who had been studying the bridge was there, along with the owners of a nearby camera shop, and they filmed the twisting from the shore. A little after eleven o'clock, a large section of the deck broke away and crashed into the water. The film became one of the most famous pieces of engineering film ever made, and it is still shown to engineering students today.
===
SECTION 4: THE CAUSE. A board of leading engineers studied the failure. They found that the thin, solid sides of the deck caught the wind like a wall. As the wind flowed past, it formed swirls of air that pushed on the deck again and again. Once the deck began to twist, the wind pushed each twist a little further, adding energy faster than the bridge could lose it. Engineers call this kind of motion flutter. A deeper, open truss would have let the wind pass through and would have resisted twisting.

SECTION 5: WHAT CHANGED. Before 1940, engineers designed bridges mainly to carry the weight of the bridge itself and the traffic on it. After 1940, they also had to study how the wind moves around a bridge's shape. Engineers began to test models of new bridges in wind tunnels, machines that blow steady wind over a model so that its motion can be measured. A new Tacoma Narrows Bridge, with a deep, open truss under its road, was tested this way and opened in 1950. It still stands, and a second bridge was built beside it in 2007. Engineers also checked other suspension bridges, and some of them, including the Golden Gate Bridge in San Francisco, were later made stiffer.

SECTION 6: RECOMMENDATIONS FOR FUTURE BUILDERS.
1. Test every new design of an unusual shape in a wind tunnel before it is built.
2. Take early warning signs seriously. Strange motion is a message, not a joke.
3. Never let saving money outweigh safety in a part of a structure that people depend on.
4. Share every failure openly, so that others can learn from it.

SECTION 7: CONCLUSION. The Tacoma Narrows Bridge is remembered as a failure, but it may have saved many lives. Because its collapse was filmed and studied, and because engineers shared what they learned, the bridges built after it were safer. A failure that is hidden teaches no one. A failure that is studied can protect the next generation.
