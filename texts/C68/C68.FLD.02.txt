On Tuesday, the sky over Baltimore was the color of an old nickel.

Tuli noticed it at Shacharis, through the tall windows of the shul. By the time he walked out with his tefillin bag under his arm, the air felt thick and heavy, like a wet towel. It was not raining. It just felt as if it wanted to.

His little brother Pinny was waiting for him on the front steps of their house in his pajamas, eating a bowl of cereal.

"Take me with you," Pinny said.

"You have day camp."

"Day camp is boring. They make us do arts and crafts. I made a pencil holder out of a soup can. Yesterday I made another pencil holder out of another soup can." Pinny was nine, small for his age, with a cowlick that no amount of water could flatten and a voice that carried halfway down the block. "At the store, there are real tools."

"At the store, there are bags of cement that weigh more than you."

"I could carry a small bag."

"There are no small bags."

Mommy came to the door, wiping her hands on a dish towel, with the baby on her hip. "Pinchas Brenner, inside and get dressed. Tuli, I put an extra sandwich in your bag, and a peach for Zeidy. Make sure he eats it. He forgets."

Zeidy's horn tooted at the corner. Tuli ran.
===
By the time they crossed the Patapsco, the first few drops were hitting the windshield. Not real rain yet. Just fat, lazy drops, spaced far apart, making dark circles the size of quarters on the dusty road.

"Agnes," Zeidy said, glancing up. "The radio says she is not a hurricane anymore. She is over Georgia and the Carolinas. Weaker." He shrugged. "Weaker storms still carry water."

The store was quiet that morning. A man came in for a gallon of white paint. A boy about Tuli's age came in with his father for a new bicycle chain. A lady wanted an umbrella, then decided against it because it was not really raining, then came back twenty minutes later and bought it because now it really was.

Between customers, Zeidy taught Tuli how to cut a key.

The key machine sat at the end of the counter, a heavy iron thing with two clamps and a small grinding wheel. You put the old key in one clamp and a blank key in the other. A thin metal guide traced the bumps of the old key, and the wheel copied them onto the new one, throwing off a fine spray of brass dust that smelled like pennies.

"Slowly," Zeidy said. "Let the machine do the work. If you push, the teeth come out crooked, and the key will not open the door."
===
Tuli cut his first key. It came out crooked. He cut a second. It was better. The third one Zeidy took out to the front door, slid into the lock, and turned. The bolt clicked open.

"There," Zeidy said. "Now a family can get into their house because of you."

Tuli found that he was smiling. He had not expected to smile at all this summer.

"Zeidy," he said, "why do you still come out here every day? Abba says you could sell the store and rest. You could learn all day in the beis medrash."

Zeidy put the key down on the counter. He was quiet for a while.

"When I came to this country," he said finally, "I had nothing. Nobody knew my name. In this town, people know my name. If Mrs. Fenton's sink breaks, she does not call a stranger. She calls Mordechai. If a farmer's tractor needs a part, I find it, even if it takes me three weeks of letters. Every person who walks through that door, I can help a little." He picked up the cloth and polished the brass top of the cash register, though it did not need polishing. "Also," he added, "I like the cash register."

Tuli laughed. Zeidy's eyes crinkled.

"And everything is in here." Zeidy tapped the green ledger on the counter. "My whole life in America is written in this book. Every customer. Every sale. Every person who owed me and paid me back. You understand? Some men keep diaries. I keep a ledger."
===
At lunchtime the rain eased off, and Mr. Whitlock appeared at the back door with Captain and an extra umbrella.

"Your granddaddy says you've never seen the signal tower," he said. "Come on. Ten minutes. I'll have you back before he misses you."

They went down the alley, across a patch of weeds, and along a gravel path beside the railroad tracks. The tracks ran right along the river, so close that in places the water was only a few yards from the rails. Mr. Whitlock walked slowly with his cane, and Captain ran ahead and came back, ran ahead and came back, as if trying to hurry them.

The old signal tower was a small brick building on tall legs, with windows on all four sides. It was locked now. The railroad did most of its signals by machine these days, Mr. Whitlock explained. But for twenty-two years, he had sat up there day and night, pulling the levers that told the engineers whether the track ahead was clear.

"You learn to watch everything," he said. "The trains, the weather, the river. Mostly the river." He pointed with his cane at the stone wall of the railroad bridge, where it rose out of the water. "See those marks?"
===
Tuli looked. On the gray stone, someone had painted a column of white lines, with numbers beside them. The water this afternoon was lapping at the bottom line.

"That's a gauge. Tells you how high the water is standing. Right now she's normal. Lazy." Mr. Whitlock moved the tip of his cane up, and up, past all the white lines, to a place on the stone far above Tuli's head. "In 1868, my granddaddy said, the water came up to about there. Maybe higher. Nobody painted a line for it, because nobody was standing around with a paintbrush that day."

Tuli looked at the high place on the wall, and then at the brown water, and then up the hill at the backs of the buildings on Main Street. He tried to imagine the river rising that high. He could not.

"That couldn't happen now, though," he said. "Could it?"

Mr. Whitlock did not answer right away. Captain came back and leaned against his good leg.

"The thing about a river," the old man said at last, "is that it don't care what year it is."
===
On the way back, Mr. Whitlock asked Tuli about the small black cap on his head.

"It's called a yarmulke," Tuli said. "We wear it all the time. To remember that there's always Someone above us."

Mr. Whitlock nodded slowly, as if this made sense to him. "My mother used to say something like that," he said. "She said a man should walk around like he knows he's being watched, and like he's glad of it." He chuckled. "I wasn't always glad of it, at your age."

"Me neither," Tuli admitted, and the old man laughed out loud, a big laugh for such a thin man.

At the back steps, Mr. Whitlock stopped to catch his breath. The stairs to his apartment ran up the outside of the building, steep and narrow, with an iron railing. Tuli watched him climb them, one step at a time, leaning hard on the rail, his bad leg dragging. Captain waited at the top, his tail going.

"Every day he does that?" Tuli asked Zeidy later.

"Every day. Twice, three times." Zeidy shook his head. "I have told him, Amos, find an apartment on the ground floor. He says, Mordechai, from the ground floor you cannot see the river."

The rain came back at three o'clock, steady now, drumming on the green awning. The street outside emptied. Water ran down the gutters of Main Street in two shining streams. Tuli swept the floor, and dusted the drawers, and reorganized the box of loose screws that nobody had reorganized since 1960, and still no customers came.

At a quarter to five, Zeidy counted the cash box. Eleven dollars and fifty cents. He wrote the number in the ledger. Then, before he locked the safe, he stood for a long moment at the back window, looking down the hill at the river.

"Higher," he said quietly.

Tuli came to look. It was hard to tell through the rain. The water did look a little wider. A little browner. A little faster.

"A little," Zeidy said. "Only a little."
===
That night at supper, the rain hammered on the roof of the house in Baltimore so loudly that Abba had to raise his voice to say the bracha on the bread.

They ate Mommy's chicken and rice and potato kugel, with the radio murmuring on the kitchen counter. Pinny told everyone about his third pencil holder. The baby threw a piece of challah on the floor. Abba asked Tuli what he had learned at the store, and Tuli told him about the key machine and the signal tower and the white lines on the bridge.

Then the news came on, and everyone stopped talking.

"...the remains of Tropical Storm Agnes are moving north tonight, bringing heavy rain to Virginia, Maryland and Pennsylvania. Forecasters warn that rainfall could be extremely heavy tomorrow, Wednesday, especially west of Baltimore, and that rivers and streams may rise rapidly. Residents of low-lying areas should..."

Abba reached over and turned the radio up. He listened with his fork in the air. When the report ended, he looked at Mommy, and Mommy looked at him, the way parents look at each other when they are deciding something without words.

The phone rang. Abba answered it in the hallway. Tuli could hear only his side.

"Yes, Tatty. Yes, I heard. You want to go tomorrow anyway? Tatty, maybe this one time... I know. I know. Yes, he's here." Abba held out the phone. "Zeidy wants you."

Tuli took it. Zeidy's voice crackled on the line.

"Naftali. Tomorrow we go early. Seven o'clock, not eight. Daven at the early minyan." A pause. "Tomorrow we move the stock up off the floor. Everything. Just in case."

"Just in case of what, Zeidy?"

There was a long silence on the line. Outside, the rain kept falling.

"In case the river remembers," Zeidy said.
--- words
cowlick: a bit of hair that sticks up and will not lie flat
grinding: rubbing hard against something to shape it or wear it down
gauge: marks or a tool that measure how much or how high something is
yarmulke: the small cap a Jewish man or boy wears on his head
rapidly: very fast
