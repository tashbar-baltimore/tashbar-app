title: The Day the Dam Broke

On the afternoon of May 31, 1889, a wall of water as tall as a three-story building rushed down a narrow valley in western Pennsylvania. Within an hour, it had destroyed most of the city of Johnstown. More than 2,200 people lost their lives. It remains one of the deadliest disasters in American history, and one of the most important lessons in why the work of engineers matters.

Johnstown was a busy steel town, built where two rivers meet at the bottom of a steep valley. About 30,000 people lived in and around it. Floods were not new there. Almost every spring, heavy rain sent the rivers over their banks, and families carried their furniture upstairs and waited for the water to go down. What made 1889 different was a lake fourteen miles up the valley.

The lake was held back by the South Fork Dam, a long wall of earth and rock built years earlier to store water for a canal. When the canal closed, the dam was sold. In time, it was bought by a club of wealthy businessmen from Pittsburgh, who turned the lake into a private summer resort. They lowered the top of the dam so that a road for carriages could run across it. They put screens over the spillway, the channel that lets extra water flow out safely, to keep their fish from escaping. Their repairs were done cheaply, with mud, rocks and straw instead of careful engineering. People in the valley worried about the dam for years. Some joked that one day it would break. Few believed it really would.
===
At the end of May 1889, a huge storm dropped six to ten inches of rain on the region in one day. The lake rose fast. Workers at the dam dug frantically to cut a new spillway and pull out the fish screens, but the screens were clogged with branches. By the early afternoon, water was pouring over the top of the dam.

That day, messages were sent down the telegraph line to warn Johnstown that the dam might fail. But warnings about the dam had come before, almost every spring, and nothing had happened. Many people did not take this one seriously. Some were already on the upper floors of their homes because of the ordinary flood in the streets, and they thought they were safe. At about three o'clock, the dam gave way.

Twenty million tons of water rushed down the valley. It swept up trees, houses, railroad cars and barbed wire from a wire factory, and it grew into a moving wall of wreckage. People in its path heard a roar like thunder. Some climbed onto rooftops and floating pieces of houses. At a stone railroad bridge below the city, the wreckage piled up into a huge jam, and many people were trapped there.

Help came quickly from all over the country. Trains brought food, clothing, tents, doctors and nurses. Volunteers came to clear the wreckage and bury the dead. Clara Barton, the founder of a national relief group, arrived within days and stayed for five months, setting up shelters and organizing the work. Families across the nation sent money, and many towns adopted Johnstown as their own cause.
===
Rebuilding took years. The steel mills reopened within weeks, because the city needed work and wages to survive. New houses, schools and shops went up. Many families who had lost everything chose to stay, saying that Johnstown was their home and they would build it again. Neighbors who had survived took in families who had not. Children went back to school in tents and borrowed buildings.

The disaster also changed the law. Survivors sued the wealthy club, but they could not win, because the courts ruled that the dam's failure was an act of nature that nobody could be blamed for. Many Americans were angry. In the years that followed, courts across the country began to hold owners responsible for harm caused by things on their land, even if they had not meant any harm. People began to demand that dams be inspected by trained engineers.

More than a hundred years later, engineers studied the South Fork Dam again, using modern computer models. They found that the changes made by the club, lowering the dam and blocking the spillway, made a terrible failure far more likely. With its original height and an open spillway, the dam would probably have overflowed only gently, or held.

Today, a national memorial stands on the hillside where the dam once was. Visitors can stand where the water broke through and look down the valley toward Johnstown. The lesson of that day is not only that nature is powerful. It is that people who build something that others depend on have a responsibility to build it well, to check it carefully, and to listen when others warn that something is wrong.
--- words
spillway: a channel that lets extra water flow out of a lake safely
resort: a place where people go to rest and have fun
frantically: in a hurried, desperate way
wreckage: the broken pieces left after something is destroyed
responsible: having the duty to take care of something, and to answer for it
