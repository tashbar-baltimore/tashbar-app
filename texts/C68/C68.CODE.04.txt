It looked like a typewriter in a wooden box. A German operator pressed a key, a letter lit up on a panel of small lamps, and he wrote it down. Press A, and perhaps G lit up. Press A again, and this time it might be K. The machine was called Enigma, and the German armed forces trusted it with their most important secrets during the Second World War.

Inside the box were wheels called rotors, each with the alphabet around its edge and a tangle of wires inside. Every time a key was pressed, the first rotor turned one step, so the wiring changed. After a while the next rotor turned too, like the wheels of a car's distance counter. A board of plugs at the front swapped pairs of letters before and after they passed through the rotors. Each day, operators set the machine to a new starting position from a secret list.

The number of possible settings was so enormous that the Germans believed Enigma could never be broken. Even if an enemy captured a machine, he would still need the day's settings, and there were billions upon billions of possibilities. Trying them one by one would take longer than the war.
===
The first people to break Enigma were not British or American. They were Polish. In the early 1930s, Poland feared an attack from Germany, and its cipher office hired young mathematicians to attack the new machine. One of them, Marian Rejewski, used advanced mathematics to work out how the rotors were wired, without ever seeing the inside of a military Enigma. By 1933, the Polish team could read many German messages.

The Germans kept making Enigma harder, and the Poles struggled to keep up. In July 1939, just weeks before Germany invaded Poland, the Polish code breakers met with British and French experts in a forest near Warsaw. They handed over everything they knew, including copies of the machine they had built from their own calculations. It was one of the most generous gifts of the war.

The British carried the work forward at a country estate called Bletchley Park, north of London. By the end of the war, about nine thousand people worked there: mathematicians, chess champions, language experts, clerks and machine operators.
===
The best known of them was a young mathematician named Alan Turing. With another mathematician, Gordon Welchman, he designed an electrical machine called the bombe. The bombe did not try every possible setting. Instead, it used a weakness in the way Enigma was used.

Here is the weakness. Many German messages contained the same predictable words. Weather reports began in a standard way. Some officers ended every message with the same phrase. Code breakers called a guessed piece of plaintext a crib. If the code breakers could guess one word in a message and where it appeared, the bombe could test the rotor settings against it very quickly and throw out every setting that could not produce it. Another weakness helped too: an Enigma machine never turned a letter into itself. Press A, and A would never light up. That small fact let code breakers rule out many guesses at once.

The work was slow, tiring and secret. Messages broken at Bletchley Park were given a code name of their own, Ultra, and only a small number of leaders were allowed to see them.
===
Ultra changed the war. In the Atlantic Ocean, German submarines were sinking the ships that carried food and supplies to Britain. When Bletchley Park could read the submarines' orders, ships could be steered around them. Some historians believe the work at Bletchley Park shortened the war by as much as two years, saving many lives, though others say no one can measure such a thing exactly.

The Allies faced a constant problem: if they acted on every message they read, the Germans might realize Enigma had been broken. So they sometimes sent a spotter plane to be seen near a target, so that the enemy would think the plane, not a broken code, had given them away.

After the war, every worker at Bletchley Park was ordered to stay silent. Most kept the secret for about thirty years, not telling even their husbands, wives or children. The story of Enigma only became public in the 1970s. By then, Alan Turing had died, and many of the code breakers had grown old without anyone knowing what they had done.
