Zeidy did not say anything for a long time. He knelt in the mud with the ledger open in his hands, and the bochurim stopped scrubbing, and Dale Fisk stopped loading his truck, and even Captain sat down and was still.

Then Zeidy stood up. He walked out of the store, onto the sidewalk, to the crate where Mr. Whitlock sat washing screws in the tub. He held out the ledger, open to the first page.

"Amos," he said. "Read."

Mr. Whitlock dried his hands on the rag. He took the ledger carefully, as if it were made of glass, and held it close to his eyes. Tuli came and looked over his shoulder. At the top of the brown, wrinkled page, in Zeidy's young handwriting, it said:

Monday, June 4, 1934. Opened the store.

Below that was a long empty space, as if the writer had waited a long time for something to write. Then, near the bottom of the page:

A. Whitlock, B&O signal tower. 1 lantern wick, 1 box matches. 15 cents. On credit until Friday.

And under it, in different ink:

Paid Friday.

Mr. Whitlock read it twice. Then he lowered the book and looked up at Zeidy, and his pale blue eyes were wet.

"I'll be," he said softly. "I'll be. I forgot all about that. I was thirty years old. Came up from the tower on my break because the lantern was smoking. There was a new store with a fellow in it who had a black beard and hardly any English."

"Eleven o'clock in the morning, you came," Zeidy said. "I opened at seven. For four hours, nobody. I sat behind the counter and I thought, Mordechai, you are a fool. You borrowed money from everyone you know, and you came to a town where nobody knows you, and nobody will ever walk through that door." He put his hand on the ledger. "Then you walked through the door."
===
"Thirty-eight years ago, you were my first customer," Zeidy said. "And one week ago, in the middle of the night, I stood in the water holding this book, and I almost let you go for it." He shook his head slowly. "And the book came back anyway. The river gave it back. As if to say, Mordechai, you made the right choice, now here is your book, stop crying."

"You weren't crying," said Mr. Whitlock.

"I am crying now," said Zeidy, and he was, a little, and he did not seem to mind who saw.

He took the ledger back and held it against his chest. He looked up at the empty doorway of his store, and at the scrubbed walls inside, and at the boxes stacked along the back, and at all the people standing in the street, holding their shovels and brushes, waiting.

"Yaakov," he said to Abba. "I was wrong on Motzei Shabbos. I said it was finished." He took a deep breath. "It is not finished. We will open again. Smaller. Slower. But we will open."

Pinny cheered. Dale Fisk whistled through his fingers. One of the bochurim started to clap, and then they were all clapping, the barber and the man from the shoe repair and the grocery family across the street, all along Main Street, in the mud and the sun.

Zeidy held up his hand, and they quieted.

"But tonight," he said, "when the fast is over, everybody eats. Chana sent enough food with Yaakov this morning for an army."
===
It took the rest of the summer.

The Three Weeks were not a time for celebrations, and Zeidy said that was fine, because rebuilding was not a celebration. It was work. Every morning he picked Tuli up on the corner at twenty to eight, the way he had on the first day of vacation, in the old tan station wagon, which had been safe in the lot at the top of the hill all through the flood. They drove the long way around, past fields and woods, because the bridge was gone. Every evening they drove home.

A carpenter came and put in new floorboards. Dale Fisk brought lumber from a mill near his father's farm and would not let Zeidy pay more than it cost. Zeidy and Tuli built new drawers together, one at a time, in the back of the store. Zeidy showed him how to measure twice and cut once, how to fit the corners so they were square, how to sand the edges until they were smooth under your thumb.

"Slowly," Zeidy said, again and again. "You cannot rush a drawer."

"You say that about everything," Tuli said. "You can't rush brass. You can't rush mud. You can't rush a board."

"Because it is true about everything," said Zeidy. "Almost everything worth having is built one piece at a time."

Leonard came back two days a week, on his days off from the shopping center, to carry the heavy things. He said the air conditioning there was nice, but he missed the bell over the door.

On Tisha B'Av they did not go to the store at all. They sat on the floor of the shul in Baltimore, in the dim light, in their socks, with their leather shoes put away, and listened to Eichah, the long, sad song about a city that was destroyed. Tuli had heard it every year of his life. This year, for the first time, he thought he understood a little of what it was about. When the reader came to the end, and the whole shul said the last line together, asking to be brought back and made new as in days of old, Tuli saw Zeidy's lips move, and his eyes were closed.
===
On the day before the store reopened, Zeidy did a strange thing.

He took a small can of white paint and a narrow brush, and he climbed up on a stepladder by the wall where the long counter stood again. He found the dark, wavy line that the river had left on the plaster, the one that no scrubbing could take away. Very carefully, he painted a straight white line right along it. Beside the line he painted the words:

THE RIVER, JUNE 22, 1972.

Tuli stood at the bottom of the ladder and watched. "Zeidy, why? Everybody wants to forget about it. Why do you want to see it every day?"

Zeidy climbed down slowly. He wiped the brush on a rag. He looked up at the white line, high over both their heads.

"Everything under that line," he said, "the river can take. Nails. Cloth. Paint. A cash register. A building, even." He reached into his coat pocket and took out a folded, crumpled piece of cardboard, and Tuli saw that it was his own chart, the one he had made the night after they first shoveled the mud. Zeidy unfolded it and pointed with one big scarred finger at the very last line, the one Tuli had added at the bottom.

People: 4 safe. Dog: 1 safe.

"That," said Zeidy, "is above the line."

He folded the chart and put it back in his pocket, where Tuli suspected it had been all summer.
===
Brenner's Dry Goods and Hardware opened again on Monday, the fourteenth of August, a few days after Rosh Chodesh Elul.

There was a new door, painted green, with the old bell above it. The bell had been found in the mud by one of the bochurim and cleaned until it shone. There was a new front window with the same hand-lettered card in it: WE HAVE IT. IF WE DON'T, WE'LL GET IT. Tuli had spent four evenings with a screwdriver and a can of oil on the brass cash register, straightening every bent key, and it stood on the counter again, ringing just a little off-key, which Zeidy said gave it character. Mr. Whitlock's screws and bolts and washers filled the new drawers, each one washed and dried and sorted by hand.

Zeidy unlocked the door at seven o'clock. He opened a brand-new ledger, green like the old one, to its first page, and wrote the date at the top in his small, careful handwriting.

At five minutes after seven, the bell rang.

Mr. Whitlock came in slowly, leaning on a new cane, with Captain trotting after him. He lived on a side street up the hill now, in a ground-floor apartment that Dale Fisk's cousin rented to him. From its back window, he said, you could still see the river, if you leaned a little to the left.

"Mordechai," he said, "I need a washer for a kitchen faucet. This new place has been dripping since I moved in, and it's driving Captain crazy."

Zeidy found the washer. He held it out. Mr. Whitlock took a coin from his pocket and put it on the counter.

"Amos, please. From you I do not take money today."

"Mordechai," said Mr. Whitlock, "a man pays for what he buys. Honest weight. You taught me that." He pushed the coin across the counter. "Besides. You need to write something in that book."

So Zeidy wrote it, the first line in the new ledger, and Tuli read it over his shoulder:

Amos Whitlock. 1 faucet washer. Paid.
===
At the end of August, on the last day before yeshiva began again, Tuli finished sweeping the store floor at a quarter to five. Zeidy was counting the cash box. Through the window, Main Street was busy again. Not every shop had opened. Some windows still had plywood over them, and some had FOR RENT signs. But the grocery was open, and the barbershop, and the shoe repair next door, and a family from out of town was walking up the hill, licking ice cream cones and pointing at the old stone buildings.

Tuli went out the back door and down to the bottom of the alley and stood looking at the river.

The Patapsco was low again. It was brown and lazy and quiet, sliding along between its rocky banks, wide enough to throw a stone across if you had a strong arm. The railroad tracks had been repaired. Up in the trees along the bank, far above the water, Tuli could still see branches and scraps of plastic caught in the leaves, higher than his head, where the river had left them.

On a rock near the far bank, a heron was standing on one leg, perfectly still.

Tomorrow his friends would come back from camp, sunburned and full of stories about the lake and the trees and staying up too late. They would ask him what he did all summer. He thought about what he would say. Maybe he would tell them about the store. Maybe about the board over the water in the dark, and the roof, and the mud, and the minyan on the bare floorboards. Maybe he would just say he had helped his grandfather, which was true, and leave the rest for later.

Behind him, up the hill, the bell over the green door rang.

"Naftali," Zeidy called from the back step. "A customer."

Tuli took one more look at the river. It looked small again. He knew better now.

He turned and went up the hill to help.
