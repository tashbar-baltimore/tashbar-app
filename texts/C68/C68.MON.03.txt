A Beacon feature by Yoni Kaplan.

There is no sign on the house at the corner of Linden Street. There is no office, no computer screen and no line of customers. But every week, three or four people ring the side doorbell, sit at Mr. Yosef Abergel's kitchen table, and leave with a loan. None of them will ever pay a cent of interest.

Mr. Abergel runs a gemach, a free-loan fund. The word comes from the Hebrew words gemilus chassadim, acts of kindness. "A loan can be a bigger chessed than a gift," he told me, pouring me a glass of tea. "When you give a man money, he may feel ashamed. When you lend him money, you tell him: I trust you. You will stand on your own feet again."

His gemach began twenty-two years ago with three thousand dollars that his father left him. "My father lent money to anyone in shul who asked," Mr. Abergel said. "He kept the records in a little notebook in his pocket. When he passed away, I found the notebook. Every loan in it had been paid back. Every single one." He decided to keep his father's fund going. Today it has grown to about forty thousand dollars, mostly from neighbors who heard about it and added to it.
===
How does a fund with no interest keep going? The answer, Mr. Abergel says, is trust and careful rules. Each borrower signs a simple note that says how much he borrowed and when he will pay it back. Each borrower also brings a guarantor: a friend or relative who promises to pay if the borrower cannot. "It is not because I do not trust the borrower," he explained. "It is because I am taking care of other people's money. The fund belongs to the whole community."

The loans are small, usually between five hundred and three thousand dollars. A young couple borrowed money to pay the first month's rent on an apartment. A father who lost his job borrowed enough to pay his bills while he looked for a new one. A widow borrowed money to fix her roof before the winter. Most borrowers pay back in small amounts each month, often for a year or more.

The gemach keeps no list of who borrowed, except in Mr. Abergel's own records. Nobody in shul knows who sat at his kitchen table. "That is part of the chessed," he said. "A person who needs help should not also have to feel embarrassed. His secret is safe with me." Even his own children do not know the names in his notebook.

I asked whether anyone ever fails to pay. Mr. Abergel was quiet for a moment. "Twice in twenty-two years," he said. "Both times the guarantor paid. Both times, later, the borrower came to me and paid back the guarantor himself. People want to be honest. You only have to give them the chance."

I asked him one more question: if no borrower ever pays interest, how did the fund grow from three thousand dollars to forty thousand? "Two ways," he said, counting on his fingers. "First, every dollar that comes back goes right out again to the next family. The same dollar has been lent maybe fifty times. Second, people who borrowed from us, years later, when things are good, add to the fund. They remember the day they sat at this table. That is the interest we collect," he smiled. "Not money. Gratitude."
===
Gemachs are not only for money. In our own neighborhood, there are gemachs that lend wedding gowns, baby strollers, folding tables and chairs, medical equipment, and even pacifiers for babies who lose theirs on Shabbos. One family on Ridge Avenue runs a gemach that lends tools. Another lends seforim to bochurim who are traveling. Each one began when someone noticed that people around them had a need, and decided to fill it.

The idea is very old. The Torah commands us to lend money to a fellow Jew who needs it, and it forbids charging him interest. The Rambam writes that the highest level of tzedakah is helping a person before he falls, with a loan, or a job, or a partnership, so that he will not need to ask for help at all. A gemach does exactly that. It catches people before they fall.

Before I left, I asked Mr. Abergel what he would say to a boy my age. He thought about it. "Start small," he said. "Lend your pencil. Lend your time. Pay back what you borrow, on the day you promised, even if nobody reminds you. When you are older, you will have something better than money. You will have a good name, and people will trust you."

Then he took out his father's little notebook, and showed me the last page. In neat handwriting, the last line said: "Paid in full."
