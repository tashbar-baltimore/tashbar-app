They told Reb Feivel on Tuesday, after Minchah, in the archive room, because Benny said that was where the story had started and it was where it should end.

Reb Feivel sat in the one chair. The five boys stood around the long table. Mr. Gross stood by the door. Benny laid out the envelope, the receipt, Gavriel's page from the city directory, and a copy of the ledger page for the first week of May 1953.

Reb Feivel did not say anything while Akiva explained. He looked at each paper for a long time. When Akiva finished, the old man picked up the receipt and held it close to the lamp, and his lips moved, and Benny realized he was reading the name over and over.

"Mandel," he said at last. "My father bought his coat from Mandel. I remember the shop. I remember a little man with a cough who never smiled." He put the receipt down very gently. "He never smiled, and he saved us. And my father went to his levayah and did not know."

Nobody knew what to say to that. So nobody said anything, and that was right.
===
The next six weeks were the busiest in the history of the closet.

Reb Feivel finished typing the last folder. Gavriel photographed every page of the ledger and the old pictures in the front hallway, and found that, with the right light, you could still read the faded names of all sixty boys under the 1951 photograph. Yoni interviewed Zaidy, Mr. Dahan, Mrs. Brandt, and Reb Feivel, twice. Eli wrote a column called "Proved and Guessed," about how a reporter tells the difference, and he put himself in it as the bad example. Akiva edited all of it and argued with everyone about commas.

And Benny wrote the lead story.

He wrote it four times. The first time, it was all about the mystery, the footprints and the peppermint and the stakeout. Akiva read it and said, "Exciting. But who is it about?" The second time, it was all about Mr. Mandel, and it was so sad that Yoni said he could not finish it. The third time was better. The fourth time, Benny started with one sentence, and everything else followed.

He came to Baltimore with a needle, a thimble and nothing else, and every Thursday night for two years, he walked four blocks in the dark to save a school that was not his.
===
The Melava Malka was in the big hall, with long white tables, hot soup, and more families than Benny had ever seen in one place. At every seat lay a copy of The Beacon, special anniversary issue, twelve pages, with the 1951 photograph on the front and the headline THE FRIEND WHO SAVED OUR YESHIVA.

Benny sat with his father and his little brothers. He watched a man two tables away open the paper, read the first line, and stop eating. He watched a grandfather put on his glasses. He watched a boy from the fourth grade read the whole front page out loud to his father, getting every word right.

When the soup was finished, Rabbi Rosen stood up. He spoke about seventy-five years. He spoke about two rented rooms over a bakery. Then he held up a copy of The Beacon.

"Five boys in a supply closet," he said, "taught me something about my own yeshiva this month. We have a friend we never knew. He gave and did not want to be thanked. So we will thank him the way he would have wanted." He paused. "Starting this week, every Thursday, the boys of this yeshiva will put eighteen dollars into a box in the front hall, from all of us together, for a family in our city that needs it. We will call it the Mandel Fund. And we will never put a name on it."

The hall was very quiet, and then everyone began to clap at once.
===
After bentching, Reb Feivel came over to the Beacon's table, slowly, with his cane. He reached into his coat pocket. Benny expected a peppermint. Instead, the old man held out a brass key on a faded red string.

"My father's," he said. "The second key. I am too old to go down those stairs twice a week, and the papers are dry now." He put it in Akiva's hand, and then, after a moment, he moved it and put it in Benny's. "Rabbi Rosen has the other one. The archive needs reporters. Real ones, who knock on the door and ask."

Benny closed his hand around the key. It was warm from the old man's pocket.

Later, walking home, Eli fell into step beside him. "You know," Eli said, "I was sure it was Mr. Dahan. For about an hour. I was completely sure."

"I know," said Benny.

"I thought being a good reporter meant being fast." Eli kicked at a pile of snow. "It means being right. Fast is just for sports scores."

Benny laughed. Then Eli laughed too.

The next morning, in the closet, someone had written a new line at the top of the whiteboard, above PROVED and GUESSED, in big letters. Nobody admitted who wrote it, but it was in Eli's handwriting.

A guess is a question. Treat it like one.
