A speech by Akiva Feldman, editor of The Beacon, at the eighth grade graduation.

Rabbi Rosen, Mr. Gross, our rebbeim, our parents, and my friends.

Three years ago, five boys sat in a small room next to the library with one broken stapler and a question. Could a class of boys write a newspaper that people would actually read?

We did not know how. Our first issue had four spelling mistakes on the front page. Our second issue came out two weeks late. Our third issue had a headline about the wrong basketball team. Mr. Gross read every one of them, and every time, he said the same thing: "Good. Now do it better."

So we did it better. Not all at once. One issue at a time.

We learned that a story is not true because it is exciting. It is true because you checked it. We learned that a quote belongs to the person who said it, not to the reporter who wishes he had said it. We learned to listen to the other side, even when the other side was Eli, at a very late hour, arguing about a comma.
===
And we learned something that no one taught us in a lesson. We learned that every person in this building has a story. The custodian who has fixed every broken desk for thirty years. The cook who knows which boys skip breakfast and saves them a roll. The first grader who lost his tooth in the lunchroom and wanted it on the front page. Each one of them mattered, and each one of them was worth a careful sentence.

Tonight, The Beacon prints its last edition with the five of us as its editors. Next year, new boys will sit in that room. They will make spelling mistakes. They will argue about commas. They will be late. And one day, if they keep going, they will be good.

To them I want to say one thing. A newspaper is a promise. It is a promise to the reader that you looked carefully, that you were fair, and that you told the truth, even when the truth was not exciting.

That promise is not only for newspapers. It is a good promise for a whole life.

So to my friends Eli, Gavriel, Yoni and Benny: thank you for three years of late nights and early mornings. To our parents: thank you for reading every issue, even the one with the wrong team.

And to all of us, as we go on to the next chapter: do not just write the story. Check it. Be fair. Tell the truth.

Good. Now do it better.
--- words
edition: one printing of a newspaper or book
custodian: the person who cleans and takes care of a building
promise: a word given that you will surely do something
