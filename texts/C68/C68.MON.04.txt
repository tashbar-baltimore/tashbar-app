The Beacon debate page. This month's question: should a young adult get a credit card? Two editors argue opposite sides. You decide.

A Useful Tool, by Eli Mizrahi.

A credit card is not a trap. It is a tool, and like any tool, it helps the person who uses it well.

First, a credit card builds a credit history. When a young man pays his card on time every month, the credit companies keep a record of it. Years later, when he wants to rent an apartment or borrow money for a house, the bank will look at that record. A person with no history is a stranger to the bank. A person who has paid on time for five years has proved that he can be trusted.

Second, a credit card is safer than cash. If a wallet full of cash is lost, the money is gone. If a credit card is lost or stolen, the owner calls the company, the card is shut off, and by law he usually owes nothing for charges he did not make.

Third, a card is convenient. You can pay for a train ticket by phone, order seforim from a store in another city, or rent a car on a trip. My older brother says his card saved his trip when his bag was lost at the airport. He bought clothes and a toothbrush, and paid it all off the next month.

Finally, many cards give rewards, like points toward airplane tickets. My cousin flew to Eretz Yisrael for half price with points from his card.

Credit cards also have an interesting history. The first card of this kind was made in 1950. Its makers liked to tell how it began: a businessman in New York finished dinner at a restaurant and found that he had forgotten his wallet.

I will admit one thing. A card that is not paid in full costs a lot of interest. But the answer is simple: pay the whole bill every month, and the interest is zero. My brother has done this for four years and has never paid a cent of interest. A rule that simple can be taught in one afternoon.

A card is dangerous only for a person who spends money he does not have. The answer is to teach people to use it wisely, not to keep it from them.
===
A Trap With a Smile, by Gavriel Shirazi.

Eli is right that a credit card can be a useful tool. But for most young people, it is a tool with a sharp edge pointed at the user.

The danger is interest. If you do not pay the whole bill each month, the company charges interest on what is left. According to the Federal Reserve, the average interest rate on credit cards in 2024 was more than twenty percent a year. That is far higher than almost any other kind of loan. A boy who owes one thousand dollars and pays only the small "minimum payment" each month can take years to pay it off, and can end up paying hundreds of dollars in interest alone.

The card companies know this. That is why the minimum payment is printed in large, friendly letters, and the interest rate is in small print. A credit card makes spending feel easy, because you do not see the money leave your hand. Studies have found that people tend to spend more when they pay with a card than when they pay with cash.

Eli says a card is safer than cash. That is true about theft. But the biggest danger to most young people is not a thief. It is their own spending.

Eli also says that cards give rewards. But the rewards are paid for by the same companies that earn billions in interest. A free ticket is not free if you paid for it in interest first.

Some will say that I am against all borrowing. I am not. A loan for a house or a business can be wise. But a credit card in the hands of an eighteen-year-old with no budget is like a car in the hands of a boy who has not learned to drive. Teach him first. Give him the keys later.

Here is my plan. Before any boy gets a credit card, he should keep a budget for six months, using cash or a debit card that spends only money he already has. If he can stay inside his budget for six months, he is ready for a card. If he cannot, the card would only make the problem bigger.
===
Editor's note, by Akiva Feldman.

Both writers used facts, and both used stories about their own families. Before you decide, check each piece of evidence. Which facts come from careful sources, and which are only one person's experience? Which reasons speak to the question, and which only sound good? A good reader does not choose the side with the louder headline. He chooses the side with the stronger proof.
--- words
convenient: easy to use, and saving time and effort
minimum: the smallest amount that is allowed
history: a record of what happened over time
rewards: gifts or points given to keep a customer using something
