The firemen led them across the rooftops, one wall at a time, up the hill. One of them carried Captain under his arm like a football. The other walked beside Mr. Whitlock with an arm around his waist, and Zeidy walked on his other side, and they went very slowly. At the fifth building, where the street below was dry, there was an open hatch and a wooden stairway going down, and at the bottom of it was a door, and then they were standing on upper Main Street in the gray morning light.

It was full of people. Men in rubber boots. Policemen. Families in raincoats, holding blankets around their shoulders, who had come up the hill in the night with nothing. A woman was handing out paper cups of hot coffee from a big metal pot. Someone put a blanket around Pinny. Someone else put a blanket around Tuli. He had not known how cold he was until he felt it.

An ambulance man knelt down and looked at Mr. Whitlock's leg. "Badly bruised," he said. "Not broken, I don't think. You're a lucky man, sir."

"Not lucky," said Mr. Whitlock. He looked over at Tuli. "I had help."

The telephone lines were down all over town. But a lady in a house near the top of the hill had a phone that still worked, and she let a long line of people use it, one at a time. When it was Zeidy's turn, Tuli stood beside him and heard Mommy's voice come out of the receiver, high and thin. Then she began to cry, and Zeidy held the phone a little away from his ear, and his own eyes filled.

"Everyone is safe," he kept saying. "Chana, everyone is safe. Both boys. Not one scratch. Tell Yaakov."
===
Abba got there at two o'clock in the afternoon.

The bridge was gone, so he had driven the long way around, far to the west and back again on little country roads, turning around every time he came to a stream that had swallowed the road. It took him almost six hours. When the car finally came over the hill, Pinny saw it first and ran, and Abba jumped out without even closing the door and caught him up in his arms. Then he grabbed Tuli, and held him so hard that Tuli could not breathe, and did not want to.

Then Abba turned to Zeidy. They looked at each other for a moment, father and son. Then Abba put his arms around his father, and Zeidy, who never hugged anyone in public, let him.

"Tatty," was all Abba said. "Tatty."

When they were ready to go, Tuli noticed Mr. Whitlock standing a little apart, leaning on a borrowed broomstick, with Captain at his feet. The ambulance man had asked him where he would stay. He had said he would figure something out.

Abba noticed too. He walked over. "Mr. Whitlock," he said, "my son has told me about you. You'll come home with us."

"That's kind of you, Mr. Brenner. But I couldn't put your family out."

"You won't be putting anyone out. We have a sofa that folds into a bed, and my younger son has been asking for a dog for three years." Abba smiled. "Please. Really."

Mr. Whitlock looked at Zeidy.

"Amos," Zeidy said. "Thirty-eight years you walked into my store. Now you walk into my son's house. It is the same thing. Get in the car."
===
On Friday morning, Zeidy insisted on going back.

"Erev Shabbos," Mommy said, when he announced it at the breakfast table. "Tatty, you have been through enough."

"Exactly because it is Erev Shabbos," said Zeidy. "I need to see it before Shabbos. Otherwise for twenty-five hours I will see nothing else."

So Abba drove, and Zeidy sat beside him, and Tuli sat in the back. Mr. Whitlock stayed behind on the sofa with his leg up on two pillows, and Pinny sat on the floor beside him, explaining the rules of checkers to Captain.

The police had set up a roadblock at the edge of town. They were letting in only the people who owned homes and stores. A young officer looked at Zeidy's driver's license, and then at his face, and waved them through. "Watch your step, sir," he said. "It's bad down there."

It was bad.

The water had gone down in the night as fast as it had come up. In its place, it had left mud. Mud everywhere, thick and gray-brown and slippery, a foot deep in places, covering the street, the sidewalks, the steps of the buildings. It smelled like a swamp, and like oil, and like something rotting. Cars sat at crazy angles where the water had dropped them. A refrigerator lay in the middle of the street. Tree branches and boards and pieces of furniture were piled against every lamppost and doorway. People were standing in front of their shops in boots, not doing anything, just looking, the way you look at something you cannot believe.

The front door of Brenner's was gone. The big window was gone. Zeidy stepped through the empty frame into his store.
===
Tuli followed him, and stopped.

He had known it would be bad. He had not known it would look like this. The long wooden counter had been lifted and turned sideways and dropped across the floor. The wooden drawers along the wall had been torn out and were lying everywhere, half buried in mud. The shelves on the right had collapsed. The tall brass cash register lay on its side in a corner, packed with mud, its round keys bent. The key machine was gone. Above the place where the counter had stood, on the plaster wall, ran a dark, wavy line, higher than Abba's hat. That was where the water had stood.

In the back office, the old black safe sat in a puddle with its door hanging open. It was empty.

Zeidy stood in the middle of it all and said nothing. He did not cry. He did not speak. He only looked, slowly, from one side of the store to the other, as if he were reading something written on the walls.

Upstairs, it was a little better. The water had come up over the storeroom floor, but only a few inches. The bottom row of boxes was soaked through. But the boxes above them, the ones Tuli and Pinny had carried up all day Wednesday, piled three and four high, were damp at the corners and dry inside.

"Zeidy," Tuli called down the stairs. "Some of it's okay! The screws, the hinges, the thread. A lot of it's okay!"

Zeidy did not answer.

A policeman put his head in through the empty window frame. "Sir, I'd board this up before you leave," he said. "We've got men out all night, but we can't watch every door."

So Tuli and Abba found three sheets of plywood in the cellar, the only things down there that had not floated away, and carried them up through the mud, and nailed them over the front of the store with a hammer and a coffee can of nails from upstairs. It was hard, slippery, filthy work. By the time they finished, it was after one o'clock.

"Tatty," Abba said gently, "it's time. It's Erev Shabbos. Shabbos doesn't wait for the river."

Zeidy looked at the plywood for a long time. Then he turned and walked to the car.
===
On the drive home, Abba turned on the radio. A man was reading the news in a slow, careful voice. Tropical Storm Agnes had flooded towns from Virginia to New York. Along the Patapsco River, seven people had drowned. Across the state of Maryland, nineteen people had died.

Abba turned the radio off. Nobody said anything for a long time. Tuli looked out the window at the green fields going by and thought about the porch with the rocking chair, and the man with the burlap sacks, and the lights moving in the trees on the Oella side of the river. He said a perek of Tehillim in his heart, slowly, for people he would never know.

They got home with three hours to spare. The house smelled of chicken soup and fresh challah. Tuli stood under the shower for a long time and watched the gray water run off him and swirl down the drain, and then he put on his Shabbos clothes, and it felt like putting on a different life.

Mommy lit the candles. Abba and the boys and Zeidy walked to shul. And when they walked back home in the long June evening, Mr. Whitlock was sitting at the dining room table in one of Abba's white shirts, which was much too short in the sleeves, with his hair combed and his hands folded, looking at the two challos under their embroidered cover as if he were not sure he was allowed to be there.

Abba made Kiddush, and everyone washed, and Abba made the bracha over the challos. Then he cut a slice, dipped it in salt, and passed it across the table to Mr. Whitlock.

"Mr. Whitlock," he said, "in our house we say that on Shabbos nobody at the table is a stranger."

Mr. Whitlock took the slice of challah and held it in his big, creased hands.

"You know, Mordechai," he said slowly, "I've lived next door to you for thirty years. I've walked into your store maybe ten thousand times. And I never once sat at your table." He shook his head. "Took a river to get me here."

"Then the river did one good thing," said Zeidy.
===
It was the best Shabbos meal Tuli could remember. Mommy's soup was hot and golden. Zeidy sang the zemiros in his deep, cracked voice, and Mr. Whitlock hummed along with the tunes he could catch. Pinny told the story of the stairs and the flashlight three times, each time a little more heroic. And when Mr. Whitlock told about the boy who crawled across a board over the river in the dark to find him, Mommy put down her spoon and looked at Tuli, and Tuli looked at his plate and felt his face go hot.

All through Shabbos, Zeidy was quiet. He davened. He learned. He ate. He answered when he was spoken to. But Tuli saw that his eyes kept going to the window, toward the west, toward Ellicott City.

On Motzei Shabbos, when three stars had come out, Abba made Havdalah. The braided candle hissed in the wine, and the smell of the spices filled the room, sweet and sharp. Then the week began again.

Zeidy sat down heavily in the armchair. He took off his hat and held it in his lap. For the first time Tuli could remember, he looked old.

"Yaakov," he said. "Naftali. I have decided."

Everyone turned.

"I am seventy-one years old. Thirty-eight years I built that store. I do not have another thirty-eight years." He looked down at the hat in his hands. "I will not open again. It is finished."
