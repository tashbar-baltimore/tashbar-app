Stand on a dock in Annapolis and dip your finger in the water. Then touch it to your tongue. It tastes a little salty, but not nearly as salty as the ocean at Ocean City. That gentle saltiness is a clue. The Chesapeake Bay is not quite a river and not quite a sea. It is something in between, called an estuary.

An estuary is a place where fresh water from rivers meets and mixes with salt water from the ocean. Estuaries are found all over the world, but the Chesapeake is the largest one in the United States. It stretches about 200 miles from its northern tip near Havre de Grace, Maryland, down to its mouth at the Atlantic Ocean in Virginia.

Long ago, at the end of the last Ice Age, melting glaciers made the ocean rise. The sea slowly flooded the valley of the Susquehanna River. The drowned river valley became the bay we know today, with dozens of smaller rivers branching off it like the limbs of a tree.
===
Fresh water and salt water do not mix all at once. Salt water is heavier, so it tends to slide along the bottom while lighter fresh water flows on top. Tides push salt water up the bay twice a day, and rivers push fresh water down. The result is a slow, swirling blend.

The amount of salt changes from place to place. Near the top of the bay, where the Susquehanna pours in, the water is almost fresh. In the middle of the bay, it is brackish, meaning partly salty. Near the mouth, it is close to ocean water. The salt level even changes with the seasons. After a rainy spring, a flood of river water pushes the salty water farther south.

The Susquehanna is the most important of all the rivers. It flows about 440 miles from New York State through Pennsylvania and into Maryland. It supplies about half of all the fresh water that enters the bay. The Potomac and the James add much of the rest, and dozens of smaller rivers add the remainder.
===
For such a big body of water, the Chesapeake is surprisingly shallow. Its average depth is only about 21 feet. A tall man could wade across large parts of it, and in many coves the water would barely reach his knees. There are a few deep channels, some more than 100 feet down, but they are the exception.

Shallow water lets sunlight reach the bottom. Where the water is clear, underwater grasses grow in thick meadows. These grasses give off oxygen and offer hiding places for young fish and crabs.

The land that drains into the bay is called its watershed. Every stream, creek and storm drain in that area eventually sends its water toward the Chesapeake. The watershed is enormous, about 64,000 square miles. It covers parts of six states, Maryland, Virginia, Pennsylvania, Delaware, New York and West Virginia, plus all of Washington, D.C. More than 18 million people live in it. That means a family washing its car in Pennsylvania is, in a small way, part of the bay's story.
===
Why is an estuary so full of life? The answer is in the mixing. Rivers carry nutrients, the natural food for tiny plants, washed from the land. The tiny plants feed tiny animals, and those feed fish, oysters and crabs. Calm, shallow water warms up quickly and gives young creatures a safe place to grow.

Because the salt level changes from top to bottom, the bay offers a home for many different creatures. Some fish, like striped bass, swim up into nearly fresh water to lay their eggs. Blue crabs travel the other way, to the salty mouth of the bay, to release theirs. Scientists count more than 3,600 kinds of plants and animals living in and around the Chesapeake.

So the next time you taste that faint salt on a Baltimore dock, remember what it means. You are touching the meeting place of mountains and sea, the waters of six states and the Atlantic Ocean, all mixing together in one great, living bay.
