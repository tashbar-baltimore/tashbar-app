title: The Button on Dovi's Jacket
grade: 7

Spring 1954.

The note from Dovi's school came home folded inside his reader. Tatty read it twice at the supper table, then passed it to Mama without a word. Shmuel, who was twelve and thought he understood everything, read it over her shoulder.

Dear Parents, it said. Your child's class has been chosen to take part in a national test of a new vaccine against polio. Please sign below if you agree.

"A test," Mama said quietly. "They want to test it on the children."

"They already tried it on thousands of people," Tatty said. "Dr. Salk gave it to his own sons."

Dovi, who was seven, looked from one face to the other. "Is it a shot?" he asked.

"Three shots," Shmuel said, reading the bottom of the page. "Over a few weeks."

Dovi put down his fork. He did not say anything, but Shmuel saw his lip begin to shake.
===
That night, Shmuel could not sleep. He kept thinking about Avromi Klein from shul. Two summers ago, Avromi had gone to day camp on a Monday with a headache. By Thursday he was in the hospital, and now he walked to shul with metal braces on both legs, swinging himself forward on crutches. He still came every Shabbos. He still sang louder than anyone. But he would never play ball again.

Shmuel got up and went to the kitchen for a drink. The light was on. Mama and Tatty were sitting at the table with the note between them.

"What if it is not safe?" Mama was saying. "What if something goes wrong?"

"And what if it is safe, and we say no?" Tatty answered. "What if this summer is like the summer of Avromi Klein?"

Neither of them noticed Shmuel in the doorway. He stood very still. He had never seen his parents afraid before.

After a long time, Mama picked up the pen. "They will have doctors watching every child," she said, as if she were telling herself. "And if it works, it works for everyone, not only for us." She signed her name.
===
On the morning of the first shot, Dovi would not eat breakfast. Shmuel walked him to school, holding his hand the whole way, which he had not done since Dovi was in kindergarten.

"Will it hurt?" Dovi asked at the corner.

"For a second," Shmuel said. "Then you get a lollipop. And a button."

"What button?"

"A button that says Polio Pioneer. It means you are helping. Like a soldier, except nobody has to fight."

Dovi thought about this. "Do I get to keep it?"

"Forever," Shmuel promised.

The line outside the nurse's office was long. Some of the boys were crying. A doctor with gray hair and kind eyes knelt down in front of each one before the shot and asked his name. When it was Dovi's turn, the doctor asked, "And what do you want to be when you grow up?"

"A rebbe," said Dovi.

"Then you will need two strong legs to walk to your yeshiva," the doctor said, and before Dovi could answer, the shot was over.

Dovi looked down at his arm in surprise. Then he looked at the lollipop. Then he looked at the button, and he smiled for the first time all day.
===
The summer of 1954 was long. Shmuel noticed things he had never noticed before: which boys were missing from shul, which houses had a doctor's car outside. Every time Dovi said he was tired, Mama put her hand on his forehead.

One evening in August, Shmuel found Mama staring at the button, which Dovi had pinned to his jacket on the hook by the door.

"Mama," Shmuel asked, "did Dovi get the real vaccine?"

"Nobody knows," she said. "That is the strange part. Half the children got the real one, and half got a shot of nothing. Not even the doctor knew. That is how they will find out if it truly works."

"So Dovi might not be protected at all?"

"He might not." Mama touched the button gently. "But even if he got nothing, he is helping. Without the children who got nothing, nobody could ever prove that the real one works."

Shmuel was quiet for a long moment. He thought about Avromi Klein swinging forward on his crutches, singing louder than anyone. He thought about his little brother crying in line and still rolling up his sleeve.

"Then he really is a pioneer," Shmuel said.

"He really is," said Mama.

Dovi did not get polio that summer. Neither did anyone else in his class. And the next April, when the radio announced that the vaccine was safe and that it worked, Dovi ran through the house shouting, holding his button high above his head, and Shmuel ran right behind him.
--- words
pioneer: one of the first people to try something new, which helps others later
vaccine: a medicine that teaches the body to fight a germ before it strikes
braces: metal supports worn on the legs to help a person stand and walk
announced: told everyone, out loud or in the news
