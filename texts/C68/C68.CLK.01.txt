Lombard Street was not the street it used to be. Zeidy said so almost every day. When he opened his shop in 1961, the block smelled of pickles and fresh rye bread, and men argued in Yiddish outside the butcher. Now there was a glass apartment tower on one corner, a phone repair store on the other, and a parking garage where the bakery had been.

In the middle of it all stood a narrow brick building with a faded sign: M. ADLER, WATCHES AND CLOCKS. Above the door, on an iron arm, hung a great round clock with a white face and black hands. The hands pointed to 4:17. They had pointed to 4:17 for eleven years.

Yehoshua Adler, whom everyone called Shuey, came to the shop every afternoon after yeshiva. He was thirteen, and he liked the shop better than anywhere except the beis medrash. It ticked. Fifty clocks on the walls, all ticking a little differently, like a room full of people learning out loud.

That Monday a boy of about six came in with his father, holding out a stopped plastic watch with a cartoon on it.

Zeidy bent over it with his loupe in his eye. He worked on that watch for forty minutes. He cleaned it, oiled it, and fixed a tiny bent pin. Then he charged two dollars.

"Zeidy, that watch costs eight dollars new," Shuey said after they left.

"To you it costs eight dollars," Zeidy said. "To him it is his watch."

Then Zeidy handed him an envelope. It was from Raymond Tavares, the man who had bought the building. The lease would end on March 31. On the morning of April 1, a crew would come to take down the old clock.

Shuey looked up through the window at the frozen hands. "Zeidy, why did the big clock stop?"

Zeidy took the loupe out of his eye. "It did not stop by itself," he said. "I stopped it."
===
That was all he would say. When Shuey asked again, Zeidy said, "Not yet," and his voice made it clear that the conversation was finished.

But Shuey noticed something he had never noticed before. Every time the clock came up, Zeidy's eyes went to the bottom drawer of his workbench. It was the only drawer in the shop that had a lock.

On Wednesday, Mr. Tavares came to see the shop. He was a tall, polite man in a gray coat, and he brought his foreman, Kenny Doyle. Mr. Tavares shook Zeidy's hand warmly.

"I'm sorry about the timing, Mr. Adler," he said. "I really am. But the new building needs a modern front, and that clock has been dead for years. Nobody will miss it."

Zeidy said nothing.

While the two men talked, Kenny wandered over to Shuey and looked up at the clock through the window. "You know what I'd do?" he said quietly. He reached into his truck by the curb and came back with a small cardboard box. Inside was a black plastic motor, the kind that runs on a battery. "Pull out the old guts, stick this behind the face, and those hands will move in ten minutes. Keeps perfect time. If the clock works, maybe the boss lets it stay up as decoration." He winked. "The old man never has to know."

Shuey stared at the box. Eleven years of stopped hands, fixed in ten minutes. Zeidy could keep his clock. Maybe Mr. Tavares would even feel generous about the shop.

"Keep it," Kenny said. "No charge."

When Shuey walked home that evening, the box was in his backpack.
===
He did not sleep well. At supper his little sister Chaya Leah told a long story about her class play, and his brother Dovi spilled the soup, and Bubby laughed at both of them. Shuey hardly heard any of it. The box sat in his backpack by the door like a guest nobody had invited.

On Thursday he carried it to the shop. He meant to tell Zeidy everything. He set the box on the workbench, and before he could speak, Zeidy picked it up and read the label.

Zeidy did not get angry. He looked at the motor for a long moment. Then he took a small key from his vest pocket and unlocked the bottom drawer.

Inside, wrapped in a soft gray cloth, was a brass wheel about the size of a saucer. Its edge was cut into teeth, sharp and even, like a crown. Shuey counted them. Fifty-seven. Then he saw the gap where three teeth were still missing.

Under the wheel lay an old notebook. Each line had a date and a few words in Zeidy's careful handwriting. "Tooth 12. Motzei Shabbos." "Tooth 13. Motzei Shabbos." The dates went back years.

"The escapement wheel," Zeidy said. "The heart of the clock. One tooth broke, so I am making a new wheel by hand. One tooth a week. You cannot rush brass."

"But you stopped at fifty-seven," Shuey said.

Zeidy held out his hands. They were trembling, just slightly. Shuey had seen it before and pretended he hadn't. "The last teeth are the finest," Zeidy said. "These hands are not steady enough anymore."

Shuey looked at the motor. Ten minutes. Then he looked at the notebook. Years.

He picked up the box and walked out to Kenny's truck, which was parked across the street. He handed it back. "Thank you," he said. "We're going to fix the real one."

When he came back inside, Zeidy was holding out a fine steel file.

"Eighteen days," Zeidy said. "Three teeth. Can you do it?"

"I don't know," said Shuey.
===
For the first four days, Zeidy did not let him touch the wheel at all. He made Shuey practice on scraps of brass clamped in the vise. Stroke, look, stroke, look. Hold the file flat. Let the file do the work. Check with the gauge every few strokes, because a tooth that is too short cannot be made longer.

Shuey's arm ached. His friend Ezra Benayoun came by one afternoon and watched for twenty minutes. "That's it?" Ezra said finally. "You just rub it?"

"That's it," said Shuey, and kept rubbing.

On the fifth day Zeidy put the real wheel in the vise. Shuey cut tooth fifty-eight over three evenings. When it was finished, Zeidy checked it under the loupe for a long time. Then he wrote in the notebook, "Tooth 58. Shuey." Shuey felt like he had just been given a medal.

Tooth fifty-nine went more slowly. Yeshiva had a big Gemara test that week, and Shuey learned until late every night. By the time he got to the shop on Monday, there were only six days left.

Six days, two teeth. He did the math in his head, again and again. The numbers would not change.

So he hurried. Zeidy had stepped out to daven Mincha at the shul on the corner, and Shuey thought he could get far ahead before he came back. He filed faster. He checked the gauge less. Stroke, stroke, stroke, look. Stroke, stroke, stroke, stroke.

When he finally held the gauge against the tooth, his stomach dropped.

The tooth was too short. Only by a hair, but a hair was everything. A clock with one short tooth would slip, gain time, and lose its way.

He had ruined the wheel.
===
Zeidy came back, took one look at Shuey's face, and set down his hat. He studied the tooth under the loupe. He did not sigh, and he did not scold. That was almost worse.

"It is not ruined," he said at last. "It is set back. That is different."

He showed Shuey how to cut out the bad tooth completely, fit a tiny new block of brass into the gap, and fasten it in place with silver solder. It took all of Tuesday and most of Wednesday. Then Shuey had to cut tooth fifty-nine again, from the very beginning.

This time he did not hurry. Stroke, look. Stroke, look. When he wanted to go faster, he made himself go slower. Zeidy sat beside him, saying nothing, which Shuey slowly understood was a way of saying a great deal.

"Fast," Zeidy said once, very quietly, "is usually the slowest way."

Tooth fifty-nine was finished on Thursday night. Zeidy wrote it in the notebook.

That left Friday. One tooth. Shuey ran to the shop the minute yeshiva let out at noon. He filed steadily all afternoon, and the sixtieth tooth grew slowly out of the brass. At five o'clock it was close. At six it was closer.

Then Zeidy put his hand gently on Shuey's arm. "Candle lighting is in twenty minutes."

"Zeidy, I just need one more hour!"

"You need one more hour," Zeidy agreed. "And Shabbos needs you now." He wrapped the wheel in its gray cloth and locked it in the drawer.

The crew would come at eight o'clock Sunday morning. Between now and then were twenty-five hours of Shabbos, when no one would touch a file. Shuey walked home under the dead clock, its hands still stuck at 4:17, and wondered how he would ever wait that long.
===
It turned out to be a strange, good Shabbos. At first Shuey kept glancing at the clock on the dining room wall. But somewhere between the fish and the soup, he stopped counting hours. Tatty asked him to say a dvar Torah, and he said one about the mon in the desert, how it fell each day and not all at once. Bubby gave him the end piece of the challah, which she only did when she was proud of him.

At shalosh seudos, Zeidy finally told him why he had stopped the clock.

"When the tooth broke, the clock did not stop. It kept running, but wrong. Two minutes fast one day, five minutes slow the next." He shook his head. "People on Lombard Street had set their watches by that clock for fifty years. A man missed his bus. A boy came late to his bar mitzvah lesson. So I climbed up and stopped the pendulum myself. A clock that tells no time is better than a clock that tells the wrong time."

The moment Tatty finished Havdalah, Shuey grabbed his coat. Zeidy and Tatty came with him. By ten o'clock he was at the bench, and by midnight the last tooth was done. Zeidy checked it under the loupe, all sixty teeth, one by one. Then he wrote in the notebook: "Tooth 60. Shuey. Motzei Shabbos."

Tatty held the ladder while Zeidy talked Shuey through every step behind the great white face. Shuey fit the new wheel into place, tightened the last screw, and gave the pendulum a gentle push.

Tick. Tock. Tick. Tock.

It was the most beautiful sound he had ever heard. Down on the sidewalk, Tatty clapped.

Then, at 2:40 in the morning, the ticking grew slower and fainter. The pendulum swung shorter and shorter.

And then it stopped.
===
Shuey wanted to cry. Instead he climbed down and sat on the curb, and Zeidy sat beside him in the cold.

"Listen," Zeidy said. "Before it stopped. Did you hear it? Tick-tock. Tick... tock. Uneven. It is out of beat. The wheel is perfect. The clock just needs to be leaned, a little."

So they climbed up again. Zeidy could not hold the tools steady, but his ears worked perfectly. "A hair to the left," he said. "Now push." Tick, tock. "Another hair." Tick, tock. They did it eleven times. On the twelfth, the ticks came even, like a heartbeat, and they did not fade.

At four in the morning, Shuey set the hands to the right time. Then everyone went home and slept for three hours.

At eight o'clock sharp, a white truck pulled up. Kenny climbed out with a ladder. Mr. Tavares stepped out after him, holding a coffee.

They both stopped and looked up.

The hands said 8:00. As they watched, the long hand clicked forward to 8:01.

People had begun to gather. Mr. Haddad from the phone store came out. Rabbi Hirsch, Shuey's rebbe, walking home from Shacharis, stopped with his tallis bag under his arm. An old man Shuey didn't know took out a pocket watch, looked at it, looked up at the clock, and smiled.

Mr. Tavares was quiet for a long time. "My grandfather used to set his watch by this clock," he said finally. "I forgot that." He turned to Zeidy. "Mr. Adler, I can't keep your shop. But a clock like this needs someone to wind it every week. If someone will do that, it stays."

Zeidy did not answer. He looked at Shuey.

"Someone will," said Shuey.

The shop closed on March 31, and Zeidy moved his bench into the basement at home, where little Chaya Leah brings him broken watches from all her friends. But every Friday afternoon, before Shabbos, Shuey climbs the ladder on Lombard Street with a brass key in his pocket. He winds the clock slowly, exactly forty turns. He never hurries.
--- words
loupe: a small magnifying glass a watchmaker holds in one eye
lease: an agreement to rent a place until a certain date
escapement: the part of a clock that lets the gears move forward one small step at a time
gauge: a tool used to measure something exactly
pendulum: a weight that swings back and forth to keep a clock's time
