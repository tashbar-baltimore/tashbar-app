If the Chesapeake Bay had a mascot, it would be the blue crab. Its picture is on T-shirts, road signs and restaurant menus all over Maryland. It is even Maryland's official state crustacean. Yet many people who see its picture every day know very little about the real animal and its remarkable life.

Its scientific name is Callinectes sapidus. The first part, Callinectes, comes from Greek words that mean "beautiful swimmer." The name fits. A blue crab's claws are tipped with bright blue, and the claws of the females also have red tips. Its shell, called a carapace, is olive green or brownish and can grow to about nine inches across.

Like all crabs, the blue crab has ten legs. The front two are claws, used for grabbing food and for defense. Anyone who has been pinched by one remembers it. The middle six legs are for walking. But the last pair is special. Those legs are flat, like the paddles of a canoe. A blue crab can turn them like little propellers and swim quickly, even sideways, through the water. Many crabs can only crawl. The blue crab is a true swimmer.
===
A blue crab's life begins far from where most people see it. After mating, usually in summer or fall, the female travels down to the salty water near the mouth of the bay. There, often the next spring or summer, she releases her eggs, usually a few million at a time.

The newly hatched larvae look nothing like crabs. They are tiny, almost clear, and drift with the currents out toward the ocean. Most are eaten by fish and other animals. The few that survive change shape several times and ride the tides back into the bay. By then they look like very small crabs.

A crab's hard shell does not grow. To get bigger, the crab must molt, which means it climbs out of its old shell. First a new, soft shell forms underneath. Then the old shell splits open at the back, and the crab slowly backs out. A blue crab molts about twenty times or more before it is fully grown, usually in a little over a year.

For a few hours after molting, the crab is completely soft and helpless. It hides in underwater grass while its new shell hardens. A crab caught at this stage is sold as a soft-shell crab, and some people pay high prices for it.
===
Blue crabs are a big part of Maryland life and its economy. Thousands of watermen catch them each year, and crab houses, packing plants and seafood markets depend on them. In a typical recent year, Maryland watermen bring in more than 20 million pounds of blue crabs. Tourists come from far away to visit crab festivals on the Eastern Shore.

Scientists watch the crab closely. Every winter, while crabs are buried in the mud, researchers pull small dredges across the bottom at about 1,500 spots and count them. The count helps Maryland and Virginia decide how many crabs watermen may take, especially how many females, so enough remain to lay eggs.
===
In a frum family in Baltimore, though, the blue crab never comes to the dinner table. The reason is in the Torah. The Torah teaches that a creature from the water may be eaten only if it has both fins and scales. A salmon or a tuna has both. A crab has neither. It has legs, claws and a hard shell instead. So crabs, along with lobsters, shrimp and clams, are not kosher.

This does not mean a Jewish boy cannot enjoy the blue crab. He can watch one paddle through the shallows at a nature center. He can study how it molts, admire its blue claws and learn why it matters to the bay. Many Jewish families visit the Chesapeake, go boating and fishing, and appreciate its creatures, while keeping their own way of eating.

There is even something to learn from the crab's molting. To grow, it must leave behind a shell that has become too small. Growing is not always comfortable, but the crab does it again and again. In its own quiet way, the blue crab is a reminder that every creature in the bay has its place and its purpose.

carapace: the hard shell that covers a crab's back
larvae: very young animals that look different from their parents
molt: to shed an old shell or skin so the body can grow
