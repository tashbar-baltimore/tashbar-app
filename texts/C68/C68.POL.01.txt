Imagine a summer when parents were afraid of the beach, the swimming pool and the movie theater. Imagine a summer when a boy with a stiff neck and a fever made his whole family hold its breath. For about forty years in the middle of the last century, that is what summer felt like in America. The reason was a disease called polio.

Polio is short for poliomyelitis. It is caused by a tiny germ, a virus, that spreads from person to person. Most people who caught it never knew they had it. Some felt sick for a few days, with a fever and an upset stomach, and then got better. But in a small number of cases, the virus did something terrible. It attacked the nerves in the spine that tell the muscles to move. When those nerves were damaged, an arm or a leg could become weak or stop working altogether. This is called paralysis.

Doctors could not predict who would be hurt. That was part of what made polio so frightening. Two brothers could catch the virus in the same week. One might have a mild fever, while the other might never walk again.
===
Polio was not a new disease. Pictures carved in stone in ancient Egypt seem to show a man with a withered leg, the kind of damage polio leaves behind. But for most of history, polio did not come in huge waves. Then, around 1900, that changed.

In 1916, a terrible epidemic struck the northeastern United States. New York City was hit the hardest. Across the country that year, about 27,000 people became sick, and about 6,000 died. Most of them were young children. Families who could afford it fled the city. Signs went up on the doors of homes where someone was sick. Some towns stopped travelers at the edge of town and turned children away.

After 1916, polio came back again and again, usually in the summer. Nobody yet knew exactly how it spread, so people guessed. They blamed flies, dirty water, crowds and even cats. Parents kept children away from pools and lakes. Camps closed. Summer, the happiest time of the year for children, became the season of fear.
===
Here is a strange fact about polio: it often struck harder in clean, comfortable neighborhoods than in poor, crowded ones. Scientists later worked out a likely reason. In earlier times, almost every baby caught the virus very early, while he was still protected by his mother's antibodies, the body's natural defenders. He would fight it off without harm and be safe for life. As homes got cleaner, with indoor plumbing and filtered water, many children did not meet the virus until they were older. By then, their bodies had less protection, and the virus was more likely to do damage.

The disease reached the most powerful home in the country. In 1921, a rising young politician named Franklin Delano Roosevelt fell ill with polio at age 39. He never walked on his own again. Twelve years later, he became President of the United States. He usually hid his braces and his wheelchair from cameras, but he never forgot what the disease had done.

In 1938, President Roosevelt helped start a group to fight polio. It became famous for a simple idea: ask everyone to give just a dime. A popular entertainer named the campaign the March of Dimes. Envelopes full of coins poured into the White House, millions of them. The money paid for hospital care, for braces and for research into a cure.
===
Polio reached its worst year in 1952. Nearly 58,000 Americans caught it. About 21,000 of them were left with some paralysis, and more than 3,000 died. Every hospital ward seemed to hold children in leg braces, learning to walk on crutches. In some wards, there were rows of large metal tanks, each one breathing for a child who could no longer breathe alone.

Families and neighbors did what they could. They visited the sick, brought meals and raised money. Children who recovered went back to school in braces. Many of them grew up to become doctors, lawyers and teachers, and some later said that polio taught them patience and courage. But nobody wanted another summer like 1952.

The answer, everyone hoped, would be a vaccine: a way to teach the body to fight the virus before it ever struck. By the early 1950s, scientists were racing to make one. The race would become one of the most famous stories in the history of medicine. And it would end with a test so large that more than a million children took part.
--- words
paralysis: losing the power to move a part of the body
epidemic: a time when a disease spreads to many people in one area at once
antibodies: the body's natural defenders that fight a germ
virus: a tiny germ that can make people sick
ward: a large room in a hospital for many patients
