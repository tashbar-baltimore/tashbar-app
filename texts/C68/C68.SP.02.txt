A speech by Akiva Feldman, editor of The Beacon, at the end-of-year Beacon dinner.

Good evening, rebbeim, parents, and my fellow reporters. Thank you to Mr. Gross for giving us a room, a deadline, and a lot of patience. Thank you to our parents for driving us to interviews, reading our first drafts, and pretending not to notice when we took over the kitchen table. And thank you to our rebbeim, who taught us that words matter, long before we ever wrote one for a newspaper.

When we started this year, I thought a newspaper was about writing. I was wrong. A newspaper is about listening.

We learned that in our very first story. We wrote about polio. At first it was just a word in a history book. Then Eli interviewed his great-uncle, who was in a hospital bed at nine years old. He told us about the iron lungs, the empty swimming pools, and the day the vaccine finally came. Suddenly polio was not a word anymore. It was a boy. It was a family. It was a whole city holding its breath.
===
Then came the code breakers. Benny studied the code breakers of the war, and he showed us how one smart guess about a single word could unlock a whole page. We learned that a puzzle is not solved by the fastest person in the room. It is solved by the most patient one.

Then came the Chesapeake Bay. We rode on a boat with a waterman and sat in an office with a scientist. They disagreed about almost everything, but they both loved the same water. We learned that two people can both be honest, and both be partly right, and still argue. Our job was to be fair to both.

And then, in a box in the school archive, Gavriel found a letter. It was tucked inside a dinner program from 1952, written in a code by Reb Yisroel Goldberg, the old shamash of this school. He made the code simple enough for a seventh grader to break, because he hoped that someday a seventh grader would. It led us to a tin box under a stone step, holding a key, a photograph of the first graduating class, and a short note. He asked us to learn well and to take care of this place. He understood that a school is not its building. A school is its boys.
===
So what did The Beacon teach us this year?

It taught us to ask a question, and then to really listen to the answer.

It taught us to check every fact, and then check it again, because people trust what we print.

It taught us that a great-uncle is a library, that a waterman is a scientist, that a shamash is a historian, and that a seventh grader can be a reporter, if he is willing to work.

It taught us that every story starts small. A word in a book. A message nobody could read. A letter at the bottom of a box.

We wrote about polio, and we found a boy. We wrote about codes, and we found patience. We wrote about the bay, and we found fairness. We opened an old box, and we found a man who cared about boys he would never meet.

More than seventy years ago, Reb Yisroel wrote a letter and trusted that someone would read it. This year, we read it. Next year, someone will read what we wrote. So let us write it well, let us write it honestly, and let us always remember why we write.

At the start of this year, I said that a paper is about listening. Now I know why. The best story is never the one we were clever enough to write. It is the one we were quiet enough to hear.
--- words
vaccine: a medicine that teaches the body to fight off a disease
archive: a place where old papers and records are kept
shamash: the man who takes care of a shul or beis medrash
historian: a person who studies and records what happened in the past
honestly: in a truthful way, without hiding or changing facts
