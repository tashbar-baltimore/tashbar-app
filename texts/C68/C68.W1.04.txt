On Thursday, Reb Feivel was not in the beis medrash before Minchah.

Benny noticed first, because he had been looking for him. The chair by the window was empty. The Gemara was there, closed, with a pencil lying across it. Benny felt a strange mix of things: a little thrill, and a little sadness, as if he were about to find out a secret he did not really want to know.

After Minchah, the five boys went to the small library at the top of the basement stairs. It had two tables, a radiator that knocked, and a clear view through its glass door of the stairs going down. Akiva had asked Mr. Gross for permission to learn there. Mr. Gross had said yes, and had said he would be in his office down the hall, "in case anybody needs a grown-up."

They opened their Gemaras. Akiva and Eli learned together, the way they had since third grade, Eli arguing every line and Akiva answering. Yoni and Gavriel learned together at the other table. Benny sat with Yoni and Gavriel, which made them three, and nobody minded.

It was the strangest stakeout in the history of newspapers. Five boys, two tables, a knocking radiator, and the sound of Gemara.
===
For twenty minutes nothing happened. Then Gavriel, who was facing the door, stopped in the middle of a word.

Footsteps. Slow ones, on the stairs from the beis medrash, coming down. Between the footsteps came another sound, a soft tap, tap, like a stick on stone.

"A cane," whispered Yoni.

Through the glass door, they saw a thin figure in a long black coat go past the library and down into the dark. A minute later, far off at the end of the basement hall, they heard a key turn in an old, stiff lock. A door groaned.

Eli was out of his chair before anyone could stop him. Akiva caught his sleeve.

"Where are you going?"

"Down there! We have him. We open the door and we see."

"And then what? We burst in on an old man in the dark and shout, 'We caught you'?" Akiva shook his head. "He is eighty-eight, Eli. And we still don't know what he is doing. If we want the truth, we ask for it. Like menschen."

Eli stood there, breathing hard. Then he sat back down.

"So what do we do?" asked Gavriel.

Akiva closed his Gemara and stood up. "We go get Mr. Gross. We walk down like people, not like police. And we knock."
===
They went down together, Mr. Gross in front with his hands in his pockets, the five boys behind him. The hall was dark except for one thin yellow line under the archive door, exactly where Mr. Dahan had said it would be.

Mr. Gross nodded at Akiva. It was his story. Akiva stepped forward and knocked, three times, politely.

Inside, a chair scraped. The tapping came toward the door. The old brass lock turned, and the door opened.

Reb Feivel Brandt stood there in his long coat, holding a cane in one hand and a cardboard folder in the other. A small desk lamp glowed on the long table behind him. The folder was gray and soft with age, and its edges were stained a dark brown, the way paper looks when it has been wet and dried. From his coat pocket hung a faded red string.

He looked at Mr. Gross. He looked at the five boys. His eyes stopped on Benny, and he smiled.

"Nu," said Reb Feivel. "The reporters. I wondered how long it would take you." He reached into his pocket and held out a peppermint. "You found my wrapper, didn't you, new boy? I dropped it the last time. My wife says I leave a trail like a child in a forest."
===
Benny's ears went hot. He took the peppermint.

"Reb Feivel," said Akiva carefully, "the drawer for 1951 to 1955 is empty. Do you have the papers?"

"Every page," said Reb Feivel. "Some here. Most at my house, on my dining room table, where my wife is very tired of looking at them."

Eli could not hold it in. "But why? Why take them in secret?"

The old man did not look offended. He looked at Eli the way a rebbe looks at a good question.

"Come in," he said. "Sit. There is only one chair, so I will sit, and you will stand, because I am eighty-eight and you are twelve." He sat down slowly and laid the brown-edged folder on the table, under the lamp. "You want to know why. That is the right question. It is a long answer, and it is not my secret only. It belongs to this whole yeshiva."

He opened the folder. Inside lay a thin book with a cracked cloth cover, and on the cover, in faded ink, was one word in English and the same word in Hebrew.

LEDGER.

"My father kept the money of this yeshiva in this book," said Reb Feivel. "In the spring of 1953, this yeshiva almost closed its doors forever. And it did not close, because of a man whose name nobody knows." He tapped the cover with one finger. "Not even me."
--- words
stakeout: watching a place in secret to see who comes
radiator: a metal heater that warms a room
menschen: good, decent people
politely: in a kind and respectful way
stained: marked with a color that will not wash away
ledger: a book where money in and money out is written down
