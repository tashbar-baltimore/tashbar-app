Yoni's Zaidy lived in an apartment with a view of the parking lot and a closet full of things he refused to throw away. He listened to the whole story from his armchair, with his eyes closed. When Yoni said the words "a tailor on Lombard Street," his eyes opened.

"Mandel," he said.

The boys looked at each other. Eli's face went red, then very still.

"Yisroel Mandel," said Zaidy. "A small man with a big cough. He had a shop the size of this kitchen, under a sign that said Mandel's Tailoring. He came after the war, in 1949, with a needle, a thimble and the clothes he was wearing. He had lost everybody. Wife, children, everybody." Zaidy was quiet for a moment. "He sewed from six in the morning until late at night. My father took me there for my bar mitzvah suit in 1954. I remember because I wanted a blue suit, and Mr. Mandel said, 'Gray. Blue is for chasunas. Gray, you can wear for ten years.' He was right. I wore it for ten years."

"Zaidy," said Yoni carefully, "do you think a man like that could have given eighteen dollars a week? For two years?"

Zaidy laughed, a short, surprised laugh. "Mandel? He lived on bread and tea. He had one coat." Then he stopped laughing. "But he never missed a minyan. And he never charged a rebbe for a repair."
===
Gavriel came back from the downtown library with a copy of one page from the 1953 city directory. He put it on the long table in the closet. Halfway down the page, between a shoe store and a bakery, was one line: Mandel, Israel, tailoring, 1017 Lombard St.

"The street matches. The DEL'S matches. The ORING matches," said Gavriel. "The bakery is a different bakery from the yeshiva's, but it is only four blocks away. A man could walk it on a Thursday night."

Eli stood up and went to the whiteboard. Everyone watched him. He picked up the marker. Then, instead of writing anything, he turned around.

"It fits," he said. "It all fits. Every part of my guess from last week was right. Mandel, tailoring, Lombard Street." He took a breath. "And we still can't print it."

Akiva raised his eyebrows.

"There could be two tailors who crossed their sevens," said Eli. "There could have been a nephew who used his envelopes. Everything we have says it was a man who worked in that shop. Nothing says it was Mr. Mandel himself." He capped the marker. "We need his handwriting. Benny's job."

Benny had never heard Eli argue against himself before. From the look on Akiva's face, neither had Akiva.
===
Benny had spent a week looking. He had asked at the shul near Lombard Street, which had closed long ago. He had asked Mr. Gross. He had even asked his own father, who was very good at finding old papers, and who had only said, "Ask the people who wore the clothes."

So, on Sunday, Benny asked Yoni's Zaidy one more question. "When you got your bar mitzvah suit, did Mr. Mandel give your father a receipt?"

Zaidy looked at him for a long moment. Then he got up from his armchair, very slowly, and went to the closet full of things he refused to throw away. He came back with a flat cardboard box that said, in faded letters, Bar Mitzvah, 1954. Inside lay a small black siddur, a picture of a serious boy in a gray suit, a cufflink, and a folded square of yellowed paper.

At the top of the paper, printed in small letters, it said: MANDEL'S TAILORING, 1017 LOMBARD ST. Below, in pencil, in a neat, small hand: One boy's suit, gray wool, with two fittings. And the date: 7/9/54.

The seven had a short line across its middle.

Benny did not say anything. He just laid the receipt next to the envelope on Zaidy's coffee table. The paper was the same color. The printing was the same size. The handwriting was the same: the same round eights, the same crossed sevens, the same pencil pressed hard.
===
"A tailor writes his own receipts," said Zaidy softly. "Nobody else in that little shop. There was nobody else."

For a while, nobody said anything. Yoni's eyes were wet. Gavriel took a picture of the two papers side by side, very carefully, without touching either one.

Eli was the one who spoke. "That's not a guess anymore," he said. "That's proof."

Back in the closet the next morning, Akiva walked to the whiteboard. He did not write anything new on the PROVED side. Instead, he took the eraser and, one line at a time, wiped out everything on the GUESSED side. Lombard Street. A man who learned to write in Europe. A tailor? A man named Mandel? Then he wrote, on the left side, in big letters:

The friend was Yisroel Mandel, a tailor on Lombard Street, who had almost nothing and gave eighteen dollars every week.

He stepped back. "Now," he said, "we can tell Reb Feivel."

Benny thought about a man who came to Baltimore with a needle and a thimble, who had lost everyone, and who walked four blocks every Thursday night in the dark to slide an envelope under the door of a school for other people's children. He thought about what it means to be new somewhere. And he thought that he finally knew what the lead story of the anniversary issue should be.
--- words
thimble: a small cap worn on a finger to push a sewing needle
minyan: a group of ten men who daven together
receipt: a paper that shows what was paid for
fittings: visits to try on clothes while they are being made
yellowed: turned yellow with age
cufflink: a small button that closes a shirt sleeve
