If you could dig a hole straight down from Baltimore, you would have to dig about 3,950 miles before you reached the center of the earth. No one has ever come close. The deepest hole people have ever drilled, in Russia, goes down a little more than seven miles, and the rock at the bottom was so hot that the drill could go no further. So how do scientists know what is down there? And why should a reader in Baltimore care? Because almost every earthquake and every volcano on the planet begins with what is happening deep under our feet.

The earth is built in layers, a little like a peach. On the outside is the crust, the thin, rocky skin we live on. Under the oceans, the crust is only about four miles thick. Under the continents, it is thicker, about twenty to thirty miles. Compared with the whole earth, that is thinner than the skin on a peach.
===
Under the crust is the mantle, the largest layer, almost 1,800 miles deep. The mantle is made of hot rock. Most of it is solid, but it is so hot and under so much pressure that, over millions of years, it moves very slowly, the way a thick block of tar slowly sags on a hot day. Hot rock deep in the mantle rises, cools near the top, and sinks again, in giant, slow circles.

At the very middle is the core. The outer core is liquid metal, mostly iron and nickel, and as it swirls it makes the earth's magnetic field, the force that turns a compass needle north. The inner core is a ball of solid metal about as hot as the surface of the sun. It stays solid only because the weight of the whole planet presses down on it.

How do scientists know any of this, if no one has ever been there? They listen. When a large earthquake happens, it sends waves of energy through the whole planet. Some waves can travel through liquid and solid rock. Others can travel only through solids. By setting up instruments all over the world and timing when each kind of wave arrives, scientists found that one kind of wave never reaches the far side of the earth. Something liquid in the middle must be stopping it. That is how they discovered the liquid outer core, without ever seeing it.
===
Now comes the part that matters most for earthquakes and volcanoes. The crust and the very top of the mantle are not one solid shell. They are broken into about fifteen huge pieces called tectonic plates. The plates float on the softer mantle below and move a few inches a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. That may not sound like much, but over millions of years it is enough to open oceans and push up mountains.

Where plates meet, things happen. In some places, plates pull apart, and hot rock rises to fill the gap. In other places, one plate slides under another and sinks back into the mantle, where it melts. That melted rock, called magma, is lighter than the rock around it, so it rises. When it reaches the surface, it becomes a volcano. In still other places, two plates grind past each other side by side. They stick, the pressure builds up for years, and then they slip all at once. That sudden slip is an earthquake.

This is why most volcanoes and earthquakes are not spread evenly over the earth. They gather along the edges of the plates. The largest chain of them circles the Pacific Ocean and is called the Ring of Fire.

Baltimore sits in the middle of a plate, far from any edge. That is why the ground under our yeshivos and shuls is so quiet. But even here, the earth is never truly still. Deep below, the mantle is turning, the plates are drifting, and the planet we stand on is slowly, quietly changing shape.
--- words
crust: the thin, rocky outer layer of the earth
mantle: the thick layer of hot rock under the crust
core: the metal center of the earth
pressure: a strong push from weight pressing down
tectonic: about the huge moving pieces of the earth's crust
magma: melted rock under the ground
