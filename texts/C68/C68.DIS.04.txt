Earthquakes do not kill people. Buildings do. That saying, common among engineers, sounds harsh, but it points to an important truth. When the ground shakes, a person standing in an open field may fall down, but he is rarely badly hurt. The danger comes when walls crack, floors fall and roofs come down. So for more than a hundred years, engineers have worked on one question: how can a building survive the shaking?

To understand their answer, picture what an earthquake does. Deep underground, huge slabs of rock that make up the earth's crust slowly push against each other. When the strain becomes too great, the rock slips suddenly, and waves of energy race outward through the ground. At the surface, the ground jerks back and forth, and sometimes up and down, for a few seconds or for more than a minute. A building sitting on that ground is shaken from the bottom up, like a stack of blocks on a table that someone is bumping.

The first idea that most people have is to make the building as strong and stiff as possible. But a building that is very stiff can crack, like a dry stick that snaps when you bend it. Engineers learned that a better goal is a building that can bend and sway without breaking, like a green branch, and that can soak up the earthquake's energy so that less of it reaches the people inside.
===
Engineers use several tools to do this. The first is called base isolation. Instead of fixing the building tightly to the ground, they set it on thick pads made of layers of rubber and steel. When the ground shakes sideways, the pads flex, and the building above moves much more slowly and gently than the ground below. It is a little like a waiter carrying a tray of glasses who bends his knees as he walks, so that the glasses do not rattle.

The second tool is bracing. Engineers add strong steel beams in an X shape between the columns of a building, or they build thick walls of concrete with steel bars inside, called shear walls. These keep each floor from sliding sideways too far over the one below it, which is what makes many buildings collapse.

The third tool is called a damper. Some dampers work like the shock absorbers in a car: they turn the energy of motion into heat, so the swaying dies down faster. The most famous damper is in the Taipei 101 tower in Taiwan, which stood as the tallest building in the world when it opened in 2004. Hanging high inside the tower, between its 87th and 92nd floors, is a giant steel ball that weighs about 660 metric tons. When strong winds or an earthquake push the tower one way, the heavy ball swings the other way, and it slows the tower's sway. Visitors can watch it move.
===
Each of these ideas was learned the hard way, from buildings that failed. After a great earthquake destroyed much of San Francisco in 1906, engineers began to study which buildings stood and which fell. After an earthquake in Mexico City in 1985, they learned that buildings of a certain height swayed in step with the shaking of the soft ground under the city, which made their motion grow larger and larger. Countries such as Japan and Chile, which have many strong earthquakes, have written strict building codes based on these lessons. A building code is a set of rules that every new building must follow.

Engineers also test their ideas before an earthquake does. They build models, and sometimes full-size rooms or small buildings, on huge platforms called shake tables. The table copies the motion of a real earthquake, and cameras and sensors record exactly where the walls bend and where they crack.

The codes work. In 2010, a very strong earthquake struck Chile. Many older buildings were damaged, but most buildings built under the newer rules stood. Engineers say that the difference between a deadly earthquake and a survivable one is often not the strength of the shaking, but the quality of the buildings.

Of course, old buildings are the hardest problem. Many cities have thousands of buildings put up before the modern codes. Strengthening them, by adding braces, walls or new foundations, is expensive. Cities must decide how to pay for it, and which buildings to fix first. Schools and hospitals usually come first, because they hold the people who most need protection, and because a hospital must keep working after the shaking stops.

The next time you walk into a tall building, look for the clues: a thick concrete wall, a steel X between the windows, a gap around the base. Someone you will never meet studied the last earthquake, so that you would be safe in the next one.
