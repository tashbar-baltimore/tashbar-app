For one long second, nobody moved.

Down in the dark store, Zeidy stood by the counter with the water swirling around his waist. In one arm he held the green ledger. In his other hand he held the cash box, high over his head. Tuli could see his face in the beam of the flashlight. It was the face of a man doing arithmetic, very fast.

Next door, Captain barked and barked. Through the wall, Mr. Whitlock called again, weaker this time.

Zeidy looked at the ledger. He looked at the wall. Then he did something Tuli would think about for the rest of his life.

He opened his arms.

The cash box dropped into the water with a heavy splash and was gone. The green ledger slid off his arm, floated for a moment on the brown surface, and began to drift slowly away into the dark, turning as it went, like the porch with the rocking chair.

"Zeidy! The ledger!"

"Paper," Zeidy said. He was already pushing through the water toward the stairs. "It is only paper, Naftali. Amos is a person."

He reached the stairs, grabbed the railing, and pulled himself up, step by step, the water pouring off his clothes. When he reached the top, he was shaking with cold, and his breath came in short, hard gasps. But his eyes were clear.

"Step eight," whispered Pinny.

"Good boy," said Zeidy. "Now we stop counting. Now we work."
===
They went to the back window of the storeroom. Tuli pushed it open, and the rain blew in, cold and stinging.

Across the gap, so close that he could have tossed a ball onto it, was the top landing of Mr. Whitlock's outside stairs. Below it, the stairs went down into black water and disappeared. The landing itself was still dry, but the river was sliding past just a few feet beneath it, fast and loud and full of branches. Through the little window in Mr. Whitlock's door, Tuli could see the faint, wavering glow of a candle.

"His door is right there," Tuli said. "If I could get across..."

Zeidy was already looking around the storeroom. His eyes stopped on a long, heavy plank leaning against the wall. It was a shelf board, ten feet long and a foot wide, that he had been meaning to put up for two years.

"This," he said.

Together they slid the plank out through the window. It was heavier than it looked. Tuli's arms burned. The far end wobbled out over the rushing water, dipped, rose, and finally came down with a thump on the iron railing of the landing. Zeidy leaned his whole weight on the near end, against the windowsill, to hold it steady.

"I will go," Zeidy said.

"Zeidy, you're soaking wet, and you can't even feel your hands. And the board won't hold you as well as me." Tuli's voice came out steadier than he felt. "I'm lighter. I'll go."

Zeidy looked at him for a long moment in the dark.

"Do not look down," he said at last. "Look only at the door. Go slowly. You cannot rush a board."
===
Tuli climbed onto the windowsill. He put one knee on the plank, then the other. It was wet and cold and narrow, and it moved under him like something alive. He did not stand up. He crawled.

He did not look down. But he could hear the river under him. It was the loudest thing he had ever heard, a roaring and grinding and crashing, as if the whole valley were being torn up and carried away. Something heavy hit the stairs below the landing with a crack, and the plank shivered.

He kept his eyes on the candle in the door. One hand. One knee. One hand. One knee.

Then the iron railing was under his fingers, and he was pulling himself over it onto the landing, and his feet were on something solid. He turned around. Across the gap, in the storeroom window, he could see the pale shape of Zeidy's face and the small round light of Pinny's flashlight.

"I'm over!" he shouted.

He tried the door. It was not locked. He pushed it open.

Inside, the little apartment was a different place from the warm room where they had drunk tea that evening. Water covered the whole floor, brown and ankle-deep, and the braided rug floated in the middle of it. The candle was standing on top of the bookshelf, beside the model steam engine. Captain was on the kitchen table, barking at the water as if he could scare it away.

Mr. Whitlock was on the floor at the bottom of a wooden ladder that went up to a square hatch in the ceiling. He was sitting in the water with his back against the wall, holding his bad leg with both hands. His cane was gone.

"Naftali," he said, and his voice cracked. "Well. Look at you. The twelve tribes came after all."

"Are you hurt, Mr. Whitlock?"

"Tried to get up the ladder. Got halfway. Leg gave out." He shook his head. "I'm a foolish old man, son. Should have gone up the hill this afternoon, when they told us to. I thought I knew this river."
===
Tuli looked up at the hatch, and then at the water around his ankles. It had come up a little, even while they talked. He thought of the joined roofs and the low brick walls between them.

He ran back to the door and shouted across the gap. "Zeidy! He can't climb! I need help pulling him up through his roof hatch! Go up yours and come across!"

Zeidy understood at once. Tuli saw him pull the plank back into the storeroom, so that it would not fall. Then the window was empty. A minute later, Tuli heard footsteps on the roof above him, and the scrape of the hatch being pulled open from outside. Zeidy's face appeared in the square of black sky, with rain falling past it. Behind him, Pinny was holding the flashlight with both hands and pointing it down.

"Amos," Zeidy called. "We are going to take you up to the roof. Do not argue."

"Mordechai, I wouldn't dream of it," said Mr. Whitlock.

It took them a long time. Tuli would never be able to remember all of it. He remembered getting his shoulder under Mr. Whitlock's arm and lifting him to his feet. He remembered the old man's whole weight leaning on him, rung after rung, and his own legs shaking. He remembered Zeidy lying flat on the roof and reaching down through the hatch with both arms, grabbing Mr. Whitlock under the shoulders and pulling, and saying the same few words in Yiddish over and over through his teeth: "One more. One more. One more." He remembered the moment when Mr. Whitlock's weight suddenly went up and away from him, and he was standing alone on the ladder, gasping.

Then Captain. Tuli lifted him off the table, wet and wriggling, and handed him up through the hatch. Then Tuli climbed up himself and lay down on the flat tar roof in the pouring rain and could not, for a little while, do anything at all.

Pinny crawled over and lay down next to him. "You were very brave," Pinny said seriously. "I was holding the flashlight the whole time."

"I know," said Tuli. "I saw it."
===
They waited on the roof for the rest of the night.

There was nowhere else to go. Zeidy found a big sheet of tar paper that the roofers had left behind years ago, and they huddled under it against the low brick wall, the four of them and the dog, pressed close together for warmth. Mr. Whitlock sat with his leg out straight. Pinny fell asleep against Zeidy's shoulder. Captain lay across Tuli's feet. Zeidy said Tehillim from memory, softly, one chapter after another, and after a while Tuli found that he was saying the words with him.

Once, Tuli crawled to the edge and looked over. The water had come up past the top of the first floor. The green awning was gone. Below him, the storeroom window that he had climbed out of was only a few feet above the river now. Everything they had carried up those fourteen steps all day was sitting just above the water, waiting to see how high it would go.

Slowly, slowly, the sky in the east began to turn from black to gray.
===
The rain was thinner now. In the first light, Tuli sat up and looked at Ellicott City, and for a moment he could not understand what he was seeing.

The bottom of Main Street was gone. In its place was a wide brown lake, moving fast toward the river, with the tops of buildings standing up out of it. Water was pouring through the upper windows of the shops across the street. Caplan's big front windows were underwater. A delivery truck lay on its side against a wall. Further down, the railroad bridge stood up out of the flood like a dam, with a tangle of trees and lumber piled against it, and the old stone station stood in the brown water, solid and gray, as if it had decided to wait.

"Fourteen and a half feet," Mr. Whitlock said quietly beside him. "If I had to guess. Higher than any line I ever painted. Not as high as my granddaddy's, but close enough."

From somewhere up the hill came a shout. Tuli turned. Two men in yellow firemen's coats were coming toward them across the rooftops from the higher end of the street, stepping over the low brick walls, carrying a coil of rope.

"Anybody up here?" one of them called.

"Four people and one dog!" Pinny shouted, wide awake now, waving his flashlight.

Tuli started to laugh, and then found that he was crying instead, and then laughing again. Zeidy put a hand on his shoulder and kept it there.

Then Tuli looked past the firemen, past the roofs, down toward the river, toward the place where he had crossed every morning with Zeidy in the tan station wagon. He looked for a long time, to be sure.

Where the bridge had been, there was only river.
--- words
arithmetic: working with numbers, adding and subtracting
plank: a long, flat, heavy piece of wood
wavering: moving unsteadily, flickering back and forth
rung: one of the steps of a ladder
huddled: crowded close together to stay warm or safe
