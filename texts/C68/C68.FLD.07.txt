"If it is finished," Zeidy said on Sunday morning, "then we must clean it, so that someone can buy it. A man does not sell a building full of mud."

That was what he said. But Tuli noticed that he was wearing his old gray work coat when he got into the car, and that he had brought his own shovel.

Abba drove them, as he had on Friday, and Mr. Whitlock came too, because he said that if he spent one more day on the sofa he would turn into a cushion. Pinny came because nobody could stop him. Captain came because Pinny came.

Main Street on Sunday was different from Main Street on Friday. On Friday, people had stood in front of their stores and stared. On Sunday, they were working. Up and down the street, men in boots were shoveling mud out of doorways into the gutter. Women were carrying out armloads of soaked clothing and dumping them in piles. A big county truck with a scoop on the front was crawling down the middle of the street, pushing the mud into heaps, and another truck was hauling the heaps away. Somebody had set up a table with a pot of coffee and a box of doughnuts on the sidewalk near the top of the hill, with a cardboard sign that said FREE. HELP YOURSELF.

The man who owned the shoe repair next door was there already, scraping mud off his workbench with a putty knife. The barber from across the street was there. A family who ran the little grocery was there, three generations of them, passing ruined cans out of the door from hand to hand like a fire brigade.

Zeidy took down the plywood. Tuli looked inside at the mud, the broken drawers, the tipped-over counter.

"Where do we even start?" he said.

"At the door," said Zeidy. "One shovel at a time. You cannot rush mud."
===
They shoveled all morning. The mud was heavy and wet and full of things. Every shovelful had something in it: a hinge, a spool of thread, a broken jar, a soggy work glove, a dozen screws. Tuli learned to look before he threw.

Mr. Whitlock could not shovel with his bad leg. So Zeidy set an upturned crate for him on the sidewalk, in the sun, and put a washtub of clean water beside him and a row of empty coffee cans in front of him. Whenever Tuli found hardware in the mud, he carried it out to Mr. Whitlock, who washed it in the tub, dried it on a rag, and sorted it into the cans. Screws in one can. Bolts in another. Washers. Nails. Hinges. He worked slowly and carefully, with his hat pushed back, like a man who had all the time in the world.

"Forty-one years on the railroad," he said, "and I never sorted so many screws. Turns out I've got a gift for it."

At about eleven o'clock a dusty pickup truck pulled up in front of the store, and a big young man with a red face and a farmer's tan climbed out. He looked at the sign that used to hang over Brenner's door, which was lying in the mud now, and then at Zeidy.

"Mr. Brenner?" he said. "I'm Dale Fisk. Harlan Fisk's boy. You remember my dad?"

Zeidy straightened up slowly from his shovel. "Tar paper," he said. "1951. Paid in 1953, with honey."

Dale grinned. "That's him. He's got bad lungs now, can't come down himself. But he heard on the radio about Ellicott City, and he said to me, Dale, you take the truck and go help Mordechai Brenner. He said, that man trusted me when nobody else would." Dale pulled a shovel out of the back of the truck. "Where do you want the mud, sir?"
===
Dale Fisk's truck hauled away the mud that afternoon, and the ruined shelves, and the broken drawers. By evening the front half of the store was down to the floorboards, which were warped and gray and covered in a thin slime, but which were floorboards.

That night at home, Tuli could not sleep. So he took a piece of cardboard from a box in the basement, and a fat black crayon, and he made a chart. He had seen Zeidy do something like it once, when the store took inventory at the end of the year. He listed every kind of thing they had carried up the fourteen steps on Wednesday, and how many boxes of it they had, and how many were dry and how many were ruined. He counted from memory and from the labels he had written himself.

BRENNER'S: WHAT THE RIVER LEFT

Screws, bolts and nails: 22 boxes dry, 4 ruined.

Hinges and hardware: 9 boxes dry, 2 ruined.

Thread and buttons: 6 boxes dry, 0 ruined.

Work shirts and overalls: 5 boxes dry, 3 ruined.

Bolts of cloth: 4 dry, 7 ruined.

Paint: 18 cans good, 6 cans dented.

Seed: 0 dry, 5 ruined.

Below it, he wrote: Total: 64 dry or good, 27 ruined or dented.

He looked at it for a long time. Then he added one more line at the bottom, because it was true and he did not know where else to write it.

People: 4 safe. Dog: 1 safe.

In the morning, he put the chart on the dashboard of Abba's car, where Zeidy would see it. Zeidy picked it up and read it all the way through, twice, without saying anything. Then he folded it carefully and put it in his coat pocket.
===
On Monday, the boys came.

There were nine of them, crammed into two cars: older bochurim from the yeshiva and a few men from shul, with a young rebbe named Rabbi Lerner, who had sleeves rolled up past his elbows and a shovel over his shoulder. They had heard about Reb Mordechai's store from Abba at Shacharis on Sunday. Nobody had asked them to come. They had simply shown up.

"Reb Mordechai," said Rabbi Lerner, "we are at your service until the store is clean. Tell us where to put our hands."

Zeidy stood in the doorway with his mouth open. Then he closed it. Then he pointed at the cellar.

They came on Monday, and on Tuesday, and on Wednesday. Each day there were different faces and the same shovels. They carried the mud up out of the cellar in buckets, passed from hand to hand up the stairs, and dumped it into Dale Fisk's truck. They scrubbed the walls with brushes and buckets of soapy water until the plaster showed white again, except for the dark wavy line that no amount of scrubbing would take away. They pulled up the worst of the warped floorboards. They carried the boxes down from the storeroom, the dry ones, and stacked them along the cleaned back wall.

At lunchtime they all sat on the curb together with their sandwiches: the bochurim in their white shirts gone gray with mud, Dale Fisk with his peanut butter and jelly, the barber from across the street, the man from the shoe repair, and Mr. Whitlock on his crate in the middle of it all, telling railroad stories. Pinny sat as close to Dale's truck as he could get.

Tuli looked up and down the curb at all of them, and thought that he had never seen a stranger group of people eating lunch together in his life. Then he thought that he had never seen a better one.
===
Thursday was the seventeenth of Tammuz.

Zeidy fasted, and Abba fasted, and Tuli fasted for the first time since his bar mitzvah, a whole day without food or water, in the June heat, in the mud. By the middle of the afternoon his head was pounding and his mouth felt like sandpaper. But Zeidy worked, slowly, and so Tuli worked too.

Late in the afternoon, Rabbi Lerner looked at his watch and counted the men in the store. With the bochurim, and Abba, and Zeidy, and Tuli, there were eleven. "Reb Mordechai," he said, "we have a minyan. Shall we daven Mincha here?"

So they davened Mincha in the store, standing on the bare floorboards, among the stacked boxes, under the dark line on the wall. Mr. Whitlock sat quietly on his crate in the doorway with his hat in his lap, the way he had in the back office the day before the flood. When they finished, Rabbi Lerner did not go back to work right away. He stood for a moment and looked around the store.

"Today begins the Three Weeks," he said quietly. "Every year we remember that the Beis HaMikdash was destroyed, and we are sad. But a Jew is not sad the way other people are sad when something is broken. We are sad the way builders are sad. We mourn a house so that we never forget to build it again."

Nobody said anything. Then the bochurim picked up their brushes and went back to the walls.

Zeidy stood still in the middle of the floor. He looked at the boys scrubbing, and at Dale Fisk outside loading his truck, and at the barber from across the street, who had come over with a broom without being asked, and at Mr. Whitlock on his crate, washing screws one at a time. He looked for a long time. Then he put his hand on Tuli's shoulder.

"Naftali," he said, and his voice was rough. "Look. The water took the store. It did not take the town."
===
It was Tuli who found it, an hour later.

He was helping two of the bochurim move the long wooden counter, which still lay sideways across the back of the store. It took four of them to lift it. As they tipped it up onto its feet, something came loose from underneath, where the mud had packed hardest against the back wall, and fell with a soft, wet thud.

It was a book. It was swollen to twice its thickness and caked with gray mud, and its cover had gone from green to almost black. But there was no mistaking it.

"Zeidy!" Tuli shouted. "Zeidy, come here!"

Zeidy came. He looked down at the thing on the floor. For a moment he did not move at all. Then he knelt down in the mud, slowly, the way an old man kneels, and picked it up in both hands.

The ledger.

The water had carried it all the way across the store and jammed it under the counter, and the mud had covered it there, and kept it. Most of its pages were stuck together into a single solid brick. Zeidy wiped the mud from the cover with his sleeve. Very gently, with one finger, he worked at the edge of the front cover until it peeled back. The first page came with it, brown and wrinkled and stained. But the ink was still there, in a small, careful handwriting that was younger than Zeidy's handwriting now.

Zeidy read it. Then he read it again.

And Tuli, standing beside him, saw his grandfather's hand begin to shake.
