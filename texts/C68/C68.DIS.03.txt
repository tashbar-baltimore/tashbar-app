HURRICANE READY: A FAMILY GUIDE. Prepared by a coastal town's emergency office for the families it serves. Read this guide before hurricane season begins on June 1, and keep it where everyone in your home can find it.

PART 1: KNOW THE WORDS. Two words on the radio and on your phone can sound alike, but they mean very different things. A hurricane WATCH means hurricane conditions are POSSIBLE in your area, usually within 48 hours. A watch is your signal to get ready: check your kit, fill your car's gas tank, and follow the news. A hurricane WARNING means hurricane conditions are EXPECTED in your area, usually within 36 hours. A warning is your signal to finish your preparations and to leave at once if you are told to evacuate. Remember it this way: a watch means watch out; a warning means act now.

Hurricanes are rated by their steady wind speed, in five categories. A Category 1 storm has winds of 74 to 95 miles per hour. It can tear off shingles and bring down tree branches and power lines. A Category 3 storm, with winds of 111 to 129 miles per hour, is called a major hurricane. It can rip off roofs and leave a town without power or water for days or weeks. A Category 5 storm has winds of 157 miles per hour or more and can flatten homes. But the category tells you only about the wind. Do not judge a storm by its category alone.
===
PART 2: THE WATER IS THE GREATEST DANGER. In most hurricanes, more people are hurt by water than by wind. The deadliest part of a hurricane is usually the storm surge: ocean water pushed onto the land by the storm's winds. A surge can rise many feet in minutes and flood streets, homes and roads far from the beach. Heavy rain can also flood rivers and low streets, many miles inland. Never walk or drive through flood water. Just six inches of moving water can knock a grown man off his feet, and about a foot of moving water can sweep away a car. Turn around; do not drown.

Our emergency office has divided the town into evacuation zones, marked A, B and C on the map on the back page of this guide. Zone A is closest to the water and is told to leave first. Find your zone before the season begins. If you are told to evacuate, go. Do not wait to see how bad the storm becomes. The roads out of town fill quickly, and once the winds pass 40 miles per hour, rescue crews cannot come for you.

PART 3: MAKE A KIT. Pack a kit now, before any storm appears, and keep it in a waterproof bin. It should hold:
1. Water: one gallon for each person for each day, for at least three days.
2. Food that will not spoil: canned and packaged food, with a manual can opener. Families who keep kosher should pack kosher food, since a shelter may not have any.
3. A flashlight and a battery radio, with extra batteries.
4. A first aid kit and a week of every medicine that a family member needs.
5. Copies of important papers, such as identification and insurance papers, in a waterproof bag.
6. Phone chargers, and a list of family phone numbers written on paper.
===
PART 4: MAKE A PLAN. Every family should agree on a plan before the season begins. Decide where you will go if you must leave: a relative's home inland, a hotel, or a public shelter. Choose one friend or relative who lives out of town, so that everyone in the family can call that person to say they are safe. Decide who will help older neighbors who may need a ride. Practice your plan once with your children, so that no one is surprised. Write the plan on one page and tape it inside a kitchen cabinet, next to this guide.

Families who keep Shabbos should ask their rav, before the season begins, what to do if an order to evacuate comes on Shabbos or Yom Tov. Saving a life comes before almost everything else, and the time to learn the halachos is before a storm, not during one.

PART 5: AFTER THE STORM. Stay where you are until officials say it is safe to return. Stay away from fallen power lines, and treat every wire as if it were live. Do not use a generator or a grill indoors, because its fumes can kill. Take photos of any damage to your home before you clean up, for your insurance company. Check on your neighbors, especially those who live alone.

Remember: a storm can damage houses, but people who are ready can rebuild them. The most important thing in your house is the people in it.
--- words
evacuate: to leave a dangerous place to get to a safe one
surge: a sudden rise of water, pushed onto the land by a storm
category: a group in a system that sorts things by size or strength
generator: a machine that makes electricity, often with gasoline
