More than two thousand years ago, the Roman general Julius Caesar had a problem every leader has faced. He needed to send orders to his officers far away, and the messages had to travel by hand. A messenger could be captured. A letter could be stolen. If an enemy read Caesar's plans, a whole army could be lost.

Caesar's answer was simple. According to the Roman historian Suetonius, he wrote his secret letters with every letter of the alphabet moved three places down. A became D. B became E. C became F. At the end of the alphabet, the letters wrapped around to the beginning, so X became A, Y became B and Z became C. The word ATTACK, written this way, became DWWDFN.

To anyone who did not know the trick, the message looked like nonsense. To an officer who knew the rule, it was easy to read: he simply moved every letter three places back.

A secret way of writing like this is called a cipher. The ordinary message is called the plaintext, and the scrambled message is the ciphertext. The rule that turns one into the other, in this case "move three letters", is called the key.
===
For a long time, a cipher like Caesar's was good enough. Few people could read at all, and fewer still had ever seen a secret message. But a cipher that shifts every letter by the same amount has a serious weakness. There are only twenty-five possible shifts. An enemy with patience could simply try them all: shift by one, shift by two, shift by three, until a real word appeared. This way of breaking a code, by trying every possible key, is called brute force.

Later cipher makers tried to fix the weakness. Instead of shifting every letter by the same number, they mixed up the whole alphabet in a secret order. A might become Q, B might become W, C might become M, and so on. Now there were not twenty-five possible keys but an enormous number, far too many to try one by one. Surely, they thought, such a cipher could never be broken.

They were wrong. The method that broke it came from a scholar named al-Kindi, who lived in Baghdad more than eleven hundred years ago. He wrote the oldest known description of a powerful idea: frequency analysis.
===
Frequency analysis begins with something every reader knows without thinking about it. In any language, some letters are used far more often than others. In English, the most common letter is E. T, A, O, I and N come close behind. Letters like Q, X and Z are rare.

Now imagine a long secret message written with a mixed-up alphabet. The code breaker counts how often each letter appears. Suppose the letter K appears more than any other. The code breaker makes a guess: K probably stands for E. Then he looks for other clues. A three-letter word that appears again and again is likely to be THE. A single letter standing alone is probably A or I. Step by step, guess by guess, checking each guess against the message, the code breaker rebuilds the secret alphabet.

The method is slow and needs patience, but it works, and it works no matter how the alphabet was scrambled. It has one important limit: it needs a long enough message. A short message of ten letters does not show which letters are common. A message of several hundred letters almost always gives itself away.
===
The story of Caesar's cipher teaches a lesson that code makers and code breakers have learned again and again. Every cipher has a weakness, and the people trying to break it will search for that weakness with great determination. The cipher makers then invent something stronger, and the code breakers search again. This contest has gone on for thousands of years.

In the twentieth century, the contest became a matter of life and death for whole nations. Armies sent thousands of secret messages by radio, and the enemy could hear every one of them. A government that could read its enemy's messages knew where ships would sail, where armies would attack and what leaders were planning. The code breakers who succeeded, often working in secret for years, changed the course of world wars.

The tools grew far more complicated: machines with spinning wheels, codes based on rare languages, and finally computers. But the basic ideas stayed the same as in Caesar's day. A message, a key and a weakness to find. Every modern code breaker, in a way, is still asking the question that faced the enemies of Caesar: what does this pattern of strange letters really say?
