title: The Long Way Home
grade: 6
kind: story
topic: Prove it
source: original
---
For as long as Natan Abergel could remember, Mr. Pinchas Weiss had sat in the same seat at Shalosh Seudos: the third chair from the window, under the clock, next to the big plate of herring that nobody else liked. He sang every zemer a little too slowly and a little too loud, and when the boys hurried ahead, he did not hurry with them. He was the oldest man in the shul. He had a small white beard and a gray hat with a brim that had gone soft from forty years of Shabbos.

So when Mr. Weiss stopped coming to Shalosh Seudos in the middle of the winter, Natan noticed.

The first week, his chair was empty. Natan thought he might be sick. The second week, Natan saw him at Minchah, standing near the back, in his soft gray hat, davening with his eyes closed. He looked fine. But when Minchah ended and the men moved into the small room for Shalosh Seudos, Mr. Weiss buttoned his coat and went straight out the side door. Outside, it was already beginning to get dark.

"Maybe he doesn't like herring anymore," said Natan's younger brother, Avi.

"Nobody likes herring," said Natan. "That never stopped him before."
===
The third week, Natan watched more carefully. He did not want to be a spy. He just wanted to understand.

He noticed that Mr. Weiss did not sit in his own seat during Minchah. He stood by the side door, the one closest to the street. He noticed that when the gabbai called out the page number, Mr. Weiss leaned close to the man next to him and asked him to repeat it. He noticed that the siddur Mr. Weiss held had very big letters, much bigger than the ones in Natan's siddur. And he noticed that, as Mr. Weiss went out the side door, he stopped on the top step and looked down for a long moment, holding the cold metal rail with both hands, before he took the first step.

Then he walked home, very slowly, close to the houses, while the sky turned from blue to gray to black.

That night, after Havdalah, Natan asked his father what time it got dark in January.

"Early," said his father. "By the end of Shalosh Seudos, it's night. Why?"

Natan did not answer right away. He was thinking about the big letters in the siddur, and the page number Mr. Weiss had asked for twice, and the long look down the dark steps.
===
"Abba," he said, "I think Mr. Weiss can't see very well anymore. Not in the dark, anyway. I think he leaves before Shalosh Seudos so he can get home while there is still a little light."

His father put down the Havdalah candle and looked at him for a while. "What makes you think so?"

So Natan told him: the seat by the side door, the page number, the siddur with the big letters, the rail, the walk close to the houses. His father listened to every piece.

"You might be right," his father said finally. "You might be wrong, too. It could be something else. Maybe his wife isn't well, and he wants to get home to her." He was quiet for a moment. "There is only one way to know, and it isn't guessing. But you have to ask in a way that doesn't embarrass him."

Natan thought about that all week. How do you ask an old man if he is afraid of the dark without making him feel old?
===
The next Shabbos, after Minchah, Natan was waiting by the side door before Mr. Weiss got there. He had his coat on already.

"Good Shabbos, Mr. Weiss," he said. "My mother baked a cake for the Benarochs, on your street. Can I walk with you? I would like the company."

All of it was true. Natan had asked his mother that morning if there was anything to bring to that street, and she had laughed and given him the cake. He had not said one word about eyes, or the dark, or steps. He did not need to.

Mr. Weiss looked at him for a long moment, the same way he had looked down the dark steps. Then something in his face relaxed.

"The company," he said, "I would like too. Yes, of course, walk with me. You will hold the cake, and I will hold your arm. That way neither of us falls."

They walked slowly. Mr. Weiss told him about the shul fifty years ago, when it was in a basement with a leaky roof. At his door, he squeezed Natan's arm.

"Next week," he said, "I think I will stay for Shalosh Seudos. If someone could walk me home after, I would sing better. Even the herring would taste better."

"I'll be there," said Natan. And he was, every week, all winter long.
--- words
zemer: a song sung at a Shabbos meal
gabbai: the man who helps run the davening in a shul
rail: a bar to hold on to beside steps
relaxed: became calm and less worried
