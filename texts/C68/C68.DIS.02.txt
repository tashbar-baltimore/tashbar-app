In the fall of 1918, as the First World War was ending, a new and deadly kind of flu swept around the world. It spread from army camps to cities, from cities to farms, and across oceans on crowded ships. By the time it faded in 1920, it had killed tens of millions of people around the world, and about 675,000 in the United States alone. Unlike most kinds of flu, which are most dangerous for babies and the very old, this one often struck healthy young adults.

There were no flu vaccines in 1918, and no medicines that could kill the virus. Doctors did not even know yet that the flu was caused by a virus. What cities could do was decide how people would gather. The choices made by two American cities, Philadelphia and St. Louis, have been studied ever since.

In September 1918, Philadelphia was planning a giant parade to sell war bonds, a way for citizens to lend money to the government to pay for the war. A few days before the parade, sailors in the city began to fall sick with the flu. Some doctors warned that a crowd of hundreds of thousands of people, packed shoulder to shoulder, would spread the disease. City leaders went ahead. On September 28, about 200,000 people lined the streets to watch the marching bands and soldiers.
===
Within three days, every hospital bed in the city was full. Within a week, thousands of people were sick. The city closed its schools, houses of worship, theaters and other gathering places on October 3, but by then the flu was everywhere. Doctors and nurses were sick themselves. There were not enough coffins, and not enough people to dig graves. In the worst week of October, more than 4,500 people in Philadelphia died.

St. Louis acted differently. The flu reached the city a little later, and by then its leaders had read the frightening reports from the cities in the east. When the first cases appeared, the city's health commissioner, Dr. Max Starkloff, moved quickly. Within days, St. Louis closed its schools, theaters, movie houses and pool halls, and it stopped large public gatherings. Business owners complained. Some newspapers called the rules too strict. Dr. Starkloff held firm. He believed that keeping people apart for a few weeks was the best tool the city had, and he kept the rules in place even when the number of new cases began to fall, until he was sure the danger had passed.

St. Louis did not escape the flu. Thousands of its people died too. But the deaths came more slowly, and the hospitals were not overwhelmed in the same way. In 2007, two teams of scientists studied the records of many American cities from 1918. They found that at the worst point of the flu, the death rate in Philadelphia was about eight times as high as in St. Louis. Cities that closed gathering places early, and kept them closed longer, tended to have lower peak death rates.
===
Should we conclude, then, that the parade alone caused Philadelphia's disaster, and that closing gathering places alone saved St. Louis? Careful historians say it is not quite so simple. Philadelphia was a crowded port city where the flu arrived early, carried by sailors, before anyone knew how deadly it would be. St. Louis had a few extra weeks to watch what was happening in eastern cities and prepare. The two cities also differed in how crowded their neighborhoods were and in how many doctors and hospital beds they had. Some cities that closed early still suffered badly, and some that closed late were luckier. A single comparison of two cities can show a pattern, but it cannot prove what caused it. For that, scientists need many cases, and careful numbers from each one.

Still, when scientists compared many cities, not just two, the pattern held: on the whole, acting early made a real difference. The story of Philadelphia and St. Louis is a famous example of that pattern. It is not, by itself, the proof.

The flu of 1918 also showed how communities rebuild. In city after city, volunteers cooked soup for families whose parents were too sick to stand. Neighbors cared for orphaned children. Shuls and community groups organized help. When the flu finally passed, cities studied what had happened, and public health departments grew stronger. Many of the rules that cities use today in an outbreak, such as closing schools for a time, came from what was learned in 1918.

The lesson for a careful reader is a double one. First, the choices that leaders make in a crisis can save or cost many lives. Second, when someone tells a dramatic story with a clear lesson, a careful reader asks: is that the whole story, and what do the other sources say?
