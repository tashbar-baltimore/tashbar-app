Earthquakes do not kill people. Falling buildings do. Engineers say this often, and it explains their whole job. No one can stop an earthquake or even predict the day it will come. But people can build houses, schools, shuls and hospitals that stay standing when the ground starts to shake. Around the world, the way a country builds is one of the biggest differences between an earthquake that causes a few injuries and one that causes a disaster.

To understand how engineers do it, think about what an earthquake does to a building. The ground moves quickly back and forth, mostly sideways. The bottom of the building moves with the ground at once. The top, because of inertia, tries to stay where it was, so it lags behind. The building bends, first one way and then the other. A building that cannot bend will crack. A building that is too heavy at the top will sway even more. And a building whose parts are not tied together can come apart, with walls falling outward and floors dropping down.
===
Engineers have three main answers.

The first answer is to make buildings flexible and tied together. Steel and wood can bend without breaking, and they bounce back. Plain brick and stone cannot. That is why, in places with many earthquakes, most houses are made of wood, and tall buildings have frames of steel or of concrete filled with steel bars. The walls, floors and roof are all bolted and strapped to one another, so the whole building moves as one piece instead of falling apart. Many older brick buildings in California have been strengthened with steel frames added inside them.

The second answer is bracing. A tall box made of four sticks falls over easily if you push its top sideways. Add one diagonal stick from corner to corner, making triangles, and it suddenly becomes stiff. Engineers do the same thing with steel braces shaped like an X or a V, and with strong "shear walls" that keep a building from leaning too far.

The third answer is the cleverest: separate the building from the ground. In base isolation, a building sits on large pads made of layers of rubber and steel. When the earthquake comes, the ground moves, and the pads stretch sideways, but the building above moves much more slowly and gently. It is like standing on a skateboard when someone shakes the floor: your feet stay almost still while the floor slides under you.
===
Some very tall buildings also use a tuned mass damper. Near the top of the building hangs a gigantic weight, sometimes as heavy as several hundred cars. When the building sways one way, the weight swings the other way and pulls it back toward the middle. Taipei 101, a famous skyscraper in Taiwan, has a golden steel ball weighing about 660 tons hanging inside its upper floors, where visitors can see it.

Good engineering only works if people actually use it. That is why many countries have building codes, laws that say how a building must be built in an area where earthquakes happen. Japan and Chile, which have many large earthquakes, have strict codes and check that builders follow them. In 2010, a huge earthquake of about magnitude 8.8 struck Chile, one of the strongest ever measured, and most modern buildings stood. Only weeks earlier, a much smaller earthquake in Haiti, where few buildings were built to resist shaking, caused one of the worst disasters of our time.

The difference was not the earthquake. It was the buildings.

Engineers keep learning. After every large earthquake, they study which buildings stood and which fell, and they change the codes to do better. Every school that stands after the shaking stops, with every boy safe inside, is a lesson that someone learned and remembered.
--- words
predict: to say what will happen before it happens
flexible: able to bend without breaking
diagonal: a straight line from one corner to the opposite corner
isolation: being kept apart from something
damper: something that makes shaking or swaying smaller
codes: written rules or laws
