title: Two Doctors, Two Answers: Salk and Sabin
grade: 7

---
Two scientists did more than anyone else to defeat polio. Both were children of Jewish families who came to America with little money. Both worked for years in their laboratories, often late into the night. Both wanted, more than anything, to protect children. And for much of their lives, they disagreed sharply about the right way to do it.

Jonas Salk was born in New York City in 1914. His parents had not gone far in school, but they pushed their sons to study. Salk became a doctor and then a researcher. In 1947 he moved to the University of Pittsburgh, where he set up a laboratory to study the polio virus.

Albert Sabin was born in 1906 in Bialystok, a city then in the Russian Empire and now in Poland. His family came to America when he was a teenager, and he learned English quickly. He also became a doctor and a researcher, and he worked at a children's hospital in Cincinnati. By the early 1950s, Sabin was already one of the best-known polio scientists in the world.
===
To understand how the two men disagreed, it helps to understand how a vaccine works. A vaccine shows the body's defense system what a germ looks like, without making the person sick. Then, if the real germ ever arrives, the body recognizes it at once and fights it off. The question is how to show the body the germ safely.

Salk's answer was a killed-virus vaccine. In his laboratory, he grew large amounts of polio virus and then treated it with a chemical that destroyed its power to make anyone sick. The dead virus still looked like polio to the body, so the body learned to make defenders against it. Salk's vaccine was given as a shot. It needed more than one dose, and later a booster.

Sabin's answer was a live-virus vaccine. He searched for weak forms of the polio virus, ones that could still live and grow in the body but were too weak to cause paralysis. His vaccine was swallowed, often on a sugar cube or as drops on the tongue. Because the weak virus grew in the body for a while, Sabin believed it gave stronger and longer protection, and it was cheap and easy to give to huge numbers of children.
===
Each man thought the other was making a dangerous mistake. Sabin argued that a killed virus could never teach the body as well as a living one, so Salk's protection would fade. Salk argued that using a live virus was a risk, because in very rare cases a weakened virus might grow strong again and harm someone. He believed it was wiser to use a virus that could not come back to life.

Salk finished first. His vaccine was tested on more than a million children in 1954, and in April 1955 it was declared safe and effective. Car horns honked, factory whistles blew and parents wept with relief. Salk became one of the most famous men in the world. When a television reporter asked him who owned the patent on the vaccine, Salk answered, "Well, the people, I would say. There is no patent. Could you patent the sun?"

Sabin kept working. Because Salk's vaccine was already in use across America, Sabin tested his own vaccine abroad, and millions of children in the Soviet Union received it in the late 1950s. In the early 1960s, his vaccine was approved in the United States, and for almost forty years it was the main polio vaccine in America. Sabin, too, refused to make money from his discovery.
===
So who was right? In a way, both of them were.

Sabin was right that his vaccine was cheaper, easier to give and very good at stopping the virus from spreading through a whole community. It was the main tool in the worldwide campaign to wipe out polio, and it still is in many countries.

Salk was right that a live virus carries a small risk. In about one case in a few million doses, the weakened virus caused paralysis. Once polio had disappeared from the United States, that tiny risk became the only polio danger left. So in the year 2000, America switched back to a killed-virus vaccine, an improved version of Salk's idea.

The two men never became friends. But the argument between them pushed both to work harder, test more carefully and prove their claims with evidence. Today, thanks to both vaccines, wild polio has been wiped out in almost every country on earth. Millions of people walk, run and breathe on their own because two stubborn scientists each refused to stop until they had an answer.
--- words
vaccine: a medicine that teaches the body to fight a germ before it strikes
booster: an extra dose that makes a vaccine's protection last longer
patent: a legal right that lets only the inventor make and sell something
weakened: made weaker, so it can no longer do much harm
evidence: facts that show whether something is true
