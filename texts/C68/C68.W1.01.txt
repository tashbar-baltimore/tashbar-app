The newsroom of The Beacon was really a supply closet with a window. It had one long table, five chairs that did not match, a whiteboard that never fully erased, and a printer that worked only when Gavriel Shirazi spoke to it kindly. Akiva Feldman, the editor, called it "the office." Everyone else called it "the closet."

On the first Tuesday in December, Rabbi Rosen, the menahel, knocked on the open door. He had never come to the closet before. The five boys stood up so fast that Eli Mizrahi's chair fell over.

"Sit, sit," Rabbi Rosen said, smiling. He held up a brass key tied to a faded red string. "Do you know what year this yeshiva opened?"

"Nineteen fifty-one," said Akiva at once.

"Very good. This year we are seventy-five years old. At the Melava Malka in six weeks, I want every family to take home a special issue of The Beacon. I want the whole story of how this yeshiva began." He laid the key on the table. "Everything about our first years is in the archive room in the basement. It has been locked since before any of you were born."
===
Yoni Kaplan, who wrote the interviews, leaned forward. "Who started the yeshiva, Rebbe?"

"Rabbi Aharon Brandt. He came to Baltimore with almost nothing and opened a school in two rented rooms over a bakery." Rabbi Rosen turned to go, then stopped. "Two rules. Mr. Gross goes down with you the first time. And you treat those papers as if they were your own grandfather's letters."

The minute he left, Eli grabbed the key. "A locked room nobody has opened in years. This is going to be the best issue we ever printed."

"It is going to be the most careful issue we ever printed," said Akiva, and took the key back.

After Minchah, Mr. Gross, their adviser, led them down the back stairs. They passed the open door of the beis medrash, where old Reb Feivel Brandt sat by the window with a Gemara, the way he did almost every afternoon. He was eighty-eight, thin as a pencil, and he always had a pocketful of peppermints for the boys.

"You are the new one," Reb Feivel said to Benny Okafor-Levine, and he pressed a peppermint into Benny's hand. "Welcome. A new boy should not walk down dark stairs without something sweet."

Benny, who had moved to Baltimore only in September, said thank you and put it in his pocket. It was the first time an old man in this building had spoken to him by name. Well, almost by name.
===
The basement smelled of cold stone and old paper. At the end of the hall was a wooden door with a brass lock, darker than the wall around it. Mr. Gross turned the key. The lock was stiff, and he had to use both hands. The door groaned open.

Gavriel's flashlight swept the room. There were shelves of boxes, a long wooden table, one chair by a high window, and a tall gray metal cabinet with four drawers. Everything wore a gray coat of dust, thick enough to write your name in.

Eli stepped in first. "Nobody has touched this place in fifty years."

Gavriel did not answer. He was looking at the floor. Then he crouched down and held the flashlight low, so the light ran along the ground instead of down onto it.

"Look," he said quietly.

In the low light, every bump in the dust threw a tiny shadow. The floor was gray from wall to wall, except for one strip. A clean path, about as wide as a man's shoes, ran from the door straight to the tall cabinet, and from the cabinet to the chair by the window. On that strip, the dust was thin, almost gone, as if someone had walked it many times.

"Maybe the custodian swept," said Eli.

"Then why did he sweep only a path?" said Gavriel.
===
Akiva did not say anything. He walked carefully beside the path, not on it, to the cabinet. Each drawer had a small paper label in old, neat handwriting: 1956–1960, 1961–1965, 1966–1970. The top drawer said 1951–1955. He pulled it open.

It was empty.

Not a folder, not a page, not a paper clip. The bottom of the drawer was clean and bare. The drawers below it were full and dusty.

"The first five years," Yoni whispered. "The exact years Rabbi Rosen wants."

Mr. Gross frowned and looked at the lock on the door, then at the path in the dust. "This room has been locked for years," he said slowly. "And as far as I know, the only key is the one in your hand."

Benny had wandered to the chair by the window. The seat of the chair was clean too. On the floor beside it, half hidden under the table, was a small twist of clear paper. He picked it up. It crinkled, and a sharp, cool smell rose from it, a smell he knew from twenty minutes before.

He put his hand in his pocket and felt the peppermint Reb Feivel had given him. Then he looked at the wrapper again. It was the same kind.

Benny opened his mouth to say so. Then he closed it. He was the new boy. He had been here three months. And he was not ready to tell four boys he barely knew that he had a clue nobody else had seen.

So he put the wrapper in his other pocket, and he said nothing at all.
