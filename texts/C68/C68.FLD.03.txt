Pinny came too.

It was not Tuli's idea. At six-thirty on Wednesday morning, the day camp called to say it was closed for the rain. Mommy had to take the baby to the doctor, and Abba had to be at work, and Pinny stood in the kitchen in his raincoat and boots before anyone had even asked him, as if he had been ready since the night before.

"He will be fine," Zeidy said, when he pulled up at the corner and saw them both. "The store is on the hill. What could happen in a store?"

Pinny climbed into the back seat, beaming. Tuli climbed into the front, not beaming.

It was raining hard now, a steady, gray, serious rain that bounced off the hood of the station wagon and blurred the windows faster than the wipers could clear them. Frederick Road had turned into a shallow stream. In some low places Zeidy slowed almost to walking speed, and the water hissed up against the tires.

When they came down the hill to the bridge, Tuli sat up straight.

The Patapsco did not look lazy anymore. It looked angry. It had risen in the night, and spread out, and turned the color of chocolate milk. It was moving fast, so fast that whole trees were riding along on it, roots and all, turning slowly as they went. The rocks along the bank where the heron had stood were gone, swallowed.

"Zeidy," Pinny said from the back seat, in a small voice. "Is that the same river?"

"The same river," Zeidy said. "In a bad mood."
===
They started at eight o'clock and did not stop.

First the drawers. Zeidy said the metal things on the bottom rows would rust if they got wet, so Tuli pulled out drawer after drawer of screws and bolts and hinges, dumped each one into a cardboard box, wrote the label on the side with a fat black crayon, and carried the box up the narrow back stairs to the storeroom on the second floor. Then the cloth from the bottom shelves. Then the work boots, the bags of seed, the cans of paint. Pinny carried the light things: boxes of matches, spools of thread, packets of sandpaper. He went up and down the stairs so many times that he began counting them out loud.

"Fourteen steps," he announced. "Every time. Fourteen."

"Good," Tuli said, passing him with a box of hinges. "Now you can stop counting them."

"Fourteen," said Pinny.

The storeroom upstairs had a low ceiling and two windows. The front window looked out over Main Street. The back window looked down the hill toward the river, and right across at the top landing of Mr. Whitlock's outside stairs, so close that Tuli could have tossed a ball onto it. In the ceiling was a square wooden hatch with a ladder below it. Zeidy used it twice a year to climb onto the flat roof and clean the leaves out of the gutters. The roofs of all the buildings on that side of the street were joined together, separated only by low brick walls you could step over.

Customers kept coming, but they were not the usual customers. They wanted flashlights. They wanted batteries, and rope, and plastic sheeting, and buckets. A man from one of the houses down by the river bought every one of Zeidy's burlap sacks and filled them with sand from the pile in the alley, to stack in front of his door.

"Does that work?" Tuli asked him.

The man wiped the rain off his face. "Depends how much water," he said, and heaved another sack onto his shoulder.
===
At one o'clock, the sky opened.

Tuli had never seen rain like it. It did not fall in drops. It fell in sheets, in walls, so thick that he could not see the buildings across Main Street. The noise on the roof was like a train that never finished passing. Water poured off the awning in a solid curtain. In the street, the two shining streams in the gutters grew into two rushing creeks, and then the creeks joined in the middle, and the whole of Main Street became a river of its own, racing downhill toward the Patapsco.

Under the floor, something was roaring.

"What is that?" Pinny asked, his eyes wide.

"The Tiber," Zeidy said. "A little stream. It runs down the hill under the street, under some of the buildings, in a stone channel. Usually you cannot even hear it."

They could hear it now. It sounded like something very large trying to get out.

At two o'clock the back door banged open, and Mr. Whitlock came in, dripping, his cane in one hand and Captain's leash in the other. His hat had turned to a sponge. He did not bother to take it off.

"I've been down to the gauge," he said. "She's over the top line, Mordechai. Over the top. And still coming."

"How fast?" Zeidy asked.

Mr. Whitlock shook his head. "Fast. Faster than I've ever seen. I'd say a foot an hour, maybe more. And all of that rain upstream hasn't even got here yet. Every creek between here and Westminster is pouring into her."

Zeidy was quiet. Then he looked at the clock on the wall.

"Mincha," he said.

Tuli stared at him. "Now?"

"Now is the time for Mincha. The river does not change the time for Mincha."

So the three of them stood in the back office between stacks of cardboard boxes, facing east toward Yerushalayim, and davened, while the rain thundered on the roof and the Tiber roared under their feet. Mr. Whitlock waited quietly by the door with his hat in his hands. Tuli tried to concentrate on the words. It was hard. But when he got to the part of Shemoneh Esrei where you ask for help, he found that he meant it more than he had ever meant it before.
===
At four o'clock a police car crawled slowly down Main Street with its lights flashing, sending up a wake like a boat. A voice came from a loudspeaker on its roof.

"Attention. Attention. The river is rising rapidly. Residents of low-lying areas near the river should prepare to leave their homes. Roads in the area may be closed. Attention..."

The car turned around at the bottom of the hill, near the railroad bridge, and crawled back up.

Zeidy went to the telephone in the office and called home. Tuli heard him talking to Abba at his office in Baltimore. Then he hung up.

"Your father will come for you after work. He will be here by six, six-thirty. You two will go home with him." Zeidy took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes. "I will stay a little longer. There is more to bring up."

"You're not coming home, Zeidy?"

"Later. Later I will come."

They went on carrying. By five o'clock, the floor of the store was nearly bare. The bottom drawers were empty, the bottom shelves were empty, and the storeroom upstairs was so full of boxes that you had to walk sideways to get through it. Only the heavy things remained below: the cash register, the key machine, the long wooden counter, the old safe.

===
At six-thirty, the phone rang.

It was Abba. Tuli could hear his voice from across the room, loud and tight.

"...a gas station on Frederick Road, at the top of the hill. Tatty, they've closed the road. The police won't let anyone down to the bridge. They say the water's already over the approach on the far side. I can't get to you. Is there any other way? Is there any... Tatty, are the boys all right?"

Zeidy looked at Tuli, and at Pinny, who was sitting on the stairs eating the last sandwich.

"The boys are fine," Zeidy said into the phone, very calmly. "Yaakov, listen to me. The store is up from the river. The second floor is high. We will stay here tonight, upstairs, and in the morning when the water goes down, you will come for us. Tell Chana not to worry." A pause. "Yes. Yes. I promise. Go home, Yaakov. Go home and say Tehillim."

He hung up. For a moment nobody said anything. Then Pinny swallowed the last bite of his sandwich.

"We're sleeping here?" he said. He sounded thrilled.
===
Mr. Whitlock insisted they come next door for hot tea. His apartment was small and very neat, with a braided rug, a big armchair by the back window, a photograph of a lady with a kind face on the wall, and a model of an old steam engine on the shelf. He had a tea kettle on a little gas stove. Zeidy had brought his own cups from the store, and a box of crackers, and a can of sardines that he kept in the office for emergencies, and they sat around Mr. Whitlock's table and ate by the light of a lamp while the storm beat against the window.

"I'm sorry I can't offer you more," Mr. Whitlock said.

"Amos," said Zeidy, "you have given us the most important thing. A warm room and good company. The rest is only food."

After Maariv, they went back to the storeroom. Zeidy spread two bolts of cotton cloth on the floor between the boxes for the boys to sleep on and folded a work coat into a pillow for each of them. He sat down in an old wooden chair by the back window with a small Tehillim in his lap and a flashlight beside him.
===
"Sleep," he said. "I will watch."

Pinny was asleep in five minutes. Tuli lay awake, listening. The rain had not slowed at all. The Tiber was still roaring under the street. Somewhere far away, a siren wailed and faded. He heard Zeidy's voice, very low, murmuring the words of the Tehillim, over and over.

He must have dozed off, because the next thing he knew, the room was completely dark. The little light over the stairs had gone out. The hum of the refrigerator downstairs had stopped. The whole building was silent except for the rain.

"Zeidy?" he whispered.

"I am here," Zeidy said. "The electricity is gone. Go back to sleep."

But Tuli got up and felt his way to the back window and stood beside his grandfather. Outside, everything was black. Then lightning flashed, white and sudden, and for one second Tuli could see the whole hillside behind the store as clearly as noon.

The railroad tracks were gone. The gravel path to the signal tower was gone. The patch of weeds was gone. Where they had been, there was only moving water, brown and wide and full of broken things, and it was already lapping at the edge of the alley, right behind their own back door.

wake: the trail of waves left behind something moving through water
approach: the road that leads up to a bridge
