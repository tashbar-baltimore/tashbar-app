On Sunday afternoon, Mr. Gross drove the five boys to Reb Feivel's house, a narrow brick row house on a quiet street off Park Heights Avenue. Mrs. Brandt opened the door, looked at the five of them, and said, "Finally. Maybe now the papers will leave my dining room." Then she went to bring rugelach.

Reb Feivel's dining room table had disappeared. In its place was a sea of paper. Old folders lay open in neat rows on clean white towels. Single pages were spread out to dry under sheets of glass. At the end of the table sat a heavy black typewriter, older than Mr. Gross, with a stack of fresh typed pages beside it.

"Sit," said Reb Feivel. "Touch nothing with jam on your fingers."

He told them the story slowly, the way old people tell stories they have been waiting to tell.

"Two years ago, I went down to the archive to look for a picture of my father for my grandson's bar mitzvah. When I opened the first drawer, the bottom folders were wet. A pipe behind the wall had been leaking, a little, for who knows how long. The first years of this yeshiva were turning into mush."
===
"Why didn't you tell Rabbi Rosen?" asked Akiva.

"I did. I told him the wall was damp, and he had it fixed. Mr. Dahan's work, I think. But the papers?" Reb Feivel spread his thin hands. "Rabbi Rosen has four hundred boys and a building that leaks. The papers were my father's. So I took the key I had kept in my drawer since 1979, and I made a plan. Two afternoons a week, I take one folder home. My wife and I dry the pages, one by one. Then I type every word, so that if the paper crumbles, the words will not."

"You could have asked for help," said Eli.

Reb Feivel smiled. "I could have. My father used to say, 'A gift you announce is half a gift.' I wanted to give the yeshiva its own beginning for the seventy-fifth year, typed and whole, and say nothing until the night of the Melava Malka." He looked at the boys and shrugged. "Then five reporters with a flashlight came into my basement."

Benny thought about the peppermint wrapper, and the clean path, and the light under the door. They had been so sure they were chasing a thief. They had been chasing a man who was quietly saving something.

Gavriel looked at the typewriter. "How far have you gotten?"

"To 1953," said Reb Feivel. "To the hard year."
===
He opened the ledger with the cracked cloth cover. The pages were ruled in faded blue lines. In each row, in small, careful handwriting, his father had written a date, a few words, and an amount.

"Read me the spring of 1953," said Reb Feivel. "Young eyes."

Yoni read. "March 1953. Rent, two rooms, owed. Teachers, two weeks, not paid. Coal, owed." He turned the page. "April. Rent, owed. Letter from the landlord." He looked up. "It is all owed."

"My father had sixty boys and no money," said Reb Feivel. "The landlord was a fair man, but he was not a rich one. He wrote that if the rent was not paid by the end of May, the yeshiva had to leave. I was fourteen. I remember my father sitting at our kitchen table at night with this book, not eating. I remember my mother telling us to be very quiet."

"What happened?" asked Benny.

Reb Feivel turned the page himself. "This happened."

In the row for the first week of May, in the same careful handwriting, it said: Received, in an envelope under the door. From a friend. And the amount: eighteen dollars.

Under it, the next week: From a friend. Eighteen dollars.

And the next week. And the next.
===
Yoni ran his finger down the page and onto the next one, and the one after that. "It goes on for two years," he said. "Every single week."

"Every single week," said Reb Feivel. "In 1953, eighteen dollars was a lot of money for a working man. It was not enough to save a yeshiva alone. But my father showed the ledger to the landlord, and the landlord said, 'If a stranger believes in your school this much, I can wait.' And he waited. And other people heard, and they gave too. And here we are."

The room was very quiet. Even Mrs. Brandt had stopped in the doorway with the plate of rugelach.

"Who was it?" asked Eli.

"Nobody knows. The envelope came under the door of the two rooms over the bakery, every Thursday night, before Shabbos. Never a name, never a note. My father kept every envelope. He said one day he would thank the man properly. He never found out." Reb Feivel touched a thin bundle tied with string at the edge of the table. "One hundred and four envelopes."

Akiva looked at the bundle. Then he looked at the other boys, and he could see they were all thinking the same thing.

"Reb Feivel," he said slowly, "would you let us try to find him?"
