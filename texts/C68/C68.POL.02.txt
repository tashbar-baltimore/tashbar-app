title: The Machine That Breathed: The Iron Lung
grade: 7

---
Take a deep breath. You did not have to think about it. A muscle under your lungs, called the diaphragm, pulled down, and air rushed in. Then it relaxed, and the air went out. You do this about twenty thousand times a day without ever noticing.

Now imagine that the nerves telling that muscle to move have stopped working. That is what polio did to some of its patients. The virus damaged the nerves that control breathing. These patients were awake and could think clearly, but they could not pull air into their own lungs. Without help, they would die within hours.

In the 1920s, doctors had almost nothing to offer them. Then, in 1928, two scientists at Harvard University, Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw, built a machine to do the breathing for them. Its official name was the Drinker respirator. Everyone else called it the iron lung.
===
The iron lung was a long metal tank, about the size of a small car. The patient lay inside on a flat bed, with only his head sticking out through a soft rubber collar that sealed around his neck. A pump at the end of the tank moved air in and out of the tank, over and over.

Here is the clever part. When the pump pulled air out of the tank, the pressure inside dropped. The patient's chest rose, just as it does when the diaphragm works, and fresh air rushed into his lungs through his nose and mouth. When the pump let air back into the tank, the pressure rose, the chest was pressed gently down, and the air went back out. The machine did not push air into the lungs. It changed the pressure around the body, so the body breathed the way it always had.

The first patient was an eight-year-old girl in Boston who could not breathe on her own. Placed inside the machine, she began to breathe again. News of the iron lung spread quickly, and doctors around the world wanted one.
===
The first iron lungs were very expensive, costing more than many families earned in a whole year. In 1931, an inventor named John Emerson built a cheaper, quieter model that was easier to repair. It also had small windows on the side, so nurses could reach in to care for the patient without stopping the machine. Emerson's version soon filled hospitals across the country.

During the worst polio years, hospital wards held rows and rows of iron lungs. The sound of a ward was the steady whoosh of dozens of pumps working at once. Patients could not see their own bodies, so mirrors were fixed above their heads. In the mirror, a boy could see the nurse, the door and the window, and sometimes a visitor waving.

Most patients needed the iron lung for only a week or two, until their nerves healed enough for them to breathe on their own again. But some never fully recovered. They lived in their iron lungs for years. A few lived in them for most of their lives, and some of those people finished school, ran businesses and wrote books while lying inside the tank, using mouth sticks to turn pages and type.
===
Life in an iron lung was hard in ways that are easy to miss. A patient could not scratch an itch or wipe away a tear. He depended on others for almost everything. If the electricity failed, nurses and family members had to pump the machine by hand, sometimes for hours, to keep the patient alive. During storms, hospitals kept volunteers ready to take turns at the handles.

Families learned to be close to a person they could not hug. Fathers read the newspaper aloud. Brothers held up schoolwork in front of the mirror. Visitors told stories, sang songs and simply sat nearby so the patient was not alone. Many patients later said that the kindness of the people around them was what kept their spirits up.

The iron lung saved thousands of lives. But it was a way to survive polio, not a way to stop it. Doctors knew that the real answer had to come before a child ever got sick. The goal was a world where no one would ever need an iron lung again. That goal was reached, in the United States, within a single generation. Today, iron lungs are found mostly in museums, where children stare at them and find it hard to believe that their great-grandparents lived in fear of the disease they were built to fight.
