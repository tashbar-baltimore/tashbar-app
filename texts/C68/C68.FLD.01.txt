On Monday, June 19, 1972, the first morning of summer vacation, Naftali Brenner stood on the corner of his street in Baltimore at twenty to eight, holding a paper bag with two sandwiches in it and wishing he were somewhere else.

Somewhere else was a bus. At that very moment, half his class was climbing onto a big yellow bus on Park Heights Avenue, bound for overnight camp in the Pocono Mountains. They would swim in a cold lake, learn under the trees, and come home in August with stories.

Tuli, as everyone called him, was going to Ellicott City to sell nails.

A tan station wagon came around the corner, rattling a little, and stopped at the curb. On its door, in neat black letters that Zeidy repainted himself every spring, it said BRENNER'S DRY GOODS AND HARDWARE, MAIN STREET, ELLICOTT CITY.

"Good morning, Bar Mitzvah boy," Zeidy said, leaning over to open the door. "Did you daven with a minyan?"

"Yes, Zeidy."

"Good. Then the day already has a foundation. The rest we build on top."
===
Zeidy was seventy-one years old. He had a short white beard, a gray hat that he wore at exactly the same angle every day, and big hands with scars across the knuckles from fifty years of cutting glass and wire. He had come to America from a small town in Poland at nineteen, with one suitcase. In 1934 he had opened a store in Ellicott City with borrowed money and a borrowed cash register. He had paid back the money in two years. He still had the cash register.

Tuli climbed in. The car smelled of coffee and machine oil.

"You are disappointed," Zeidy said as they pulled away. It was not a question.

"No," Tuli said. Then, because lying to Zeidy never worked, he said, "A little."

"An honest answer. Also good." Zeidy nodded slowly. "Leonard quit on Thursday. He found a job at the new shopping center. Better pay, air conditioning. I do not blame him. But a man who is seventy-one cannot carry forty-pound bags of cement up from the cellar by himself all summer."

"I know, Zeidy."

"So. You are not going to camp. You are going to the store. Maybe in the store, you will also learn something."

Tuli doubted it. But he did not say so.
===
The drive took about half an hour. They went west out of the city on Frederick Road, past the rows of brick houses, past Catonsville, until the houses thinned out and the road began to drop down into a deep green valley. Trees closed in on both sides. Then, all at once, the land fell away, and there was the river.

The Patapsco was not much to look at that morning. It was brown and lazy and low, sliding along between rocky banks, wide enough to throw a stone across if you had a strong arm. On the far side, the town of Ellicott City climbed straight up a steep hill, as if somebody had tried to build a city on the side of a staircase.

"Look," Zeidy said, as he always did at this spot. "The most beautiful town in Maryland, and nobody knows it."

The car rumbled over the bridge. Tuli looked down at the water. A broken branch floated past, turning in slow circles.

On the town side of the river, the road became Main Street, and Main Street went up. It was lined on both sides with old buildings of gray granite and red brick, pressed shoulder to shoulder, two and three stories high. Some had faded painted signs on their side walls. Just above the river, a stone railroad bridge crossed over the street, and beside it stood the old B&O station, a solid stone building with a sloping roof. Zeidy liked to say it was the oldest railroad station in the whole country.
===
Higher up was Caplan's, the department store, with dresses and suits in its big front windows. There was a drugstore, a bank, a lunch counter. Here and there a window was empty, with a FOR RENT sign taped to the glass. Ellicott City was an old mill town, and some of the mills had closed, and some of the young people had moved away to the new suburbs.

Brenner's stood on the lower part of the street, only a short walk up from the railroad bridge. It was a narrow brick building, two stories tall, with a green awning and a big front window full of things: rakes, garden hoses, a pyramid of paint cans, a bolt of blue cotton cloth, a pair of rubber boots, a kerosene lamp, and a hand-lettered card that said WE HAVE IT. IF WE DON'T, WE'LL GET IT.

Zeidy unlocked the door. A small bell rang above it, the same bell that had rung for thirty-eight years.

"Welcome," Zeidy said, "to the center of the world."
===
The store was long and narrow and dim, and it was packed from floor to ceiling. On the left were wooden drawers, hundreds of them, each with a little brass handle and a little paper label: wood screws, number 8, one inch; wood screws, number 8, inch and a half; carriage bolts; washers; wing nuts; hinges. On the right were shelves of folded cloth, work shirts, overalls, socks, aprons, and thread in every color. Down the middle ran a long counter of dark wood, worn pale in the center where customers had leaned for almost four decades. On the counter sat the cash register, a tall brass machine with round keys like typewriter keys and a little window at the top where the numbers popped up.

In the back was a small office with a desk, an old black safe, and a shelf with a few sefarim. Zeidy hung his hat on a hook, put on a long gray work coat, and opened the safe. He took out a thick green ledger and a metal cash box.

"Every penny that comes in and every penny that goes out," he said, tapping the ledger. "Thirty-eight years. Every customer, every order. If someone owes me two dollars from 1951, it is in here."

"Does anyone owe you two dollars from 1951?"

Zeidy smiled. "Mr. Harlan Fisk. He was out of work, his roof leaked, and he needed tar paper. He came back to pay me in 1953. With interest, he said. He gave me a jar of honey from his own bees." Zeidy put the ledger on the desk. "That was a good customer."

The first customer of the morning was a farmer who wanted a pound of roofing nails. Zeidy handed Tuli the metal scoop and pointed him to the scale.

"A pound," Zeidy said. "Not a pound and a little. Not a pound minus a little. A pound."

Tuli scooped the nails onto the scale. The needle swung past the line. He took out three nails. It swung under. He put back one.

"Exactly," said Zeidy. "Honest weight. It says in the Torah. Now you are a businessman."

By noon, Tuli had sold nails, cut rope, and carried two bags of cement up from the cellar. His shoulders ached. He ate his sandwiches on an upturned crate in the back while Zeidy said Tehillim at the desk.
===
In the middle of the afternoon, the bell over the door rang, and a tall, thin man came in, walking with a cane. A small brown dog with a white chest trotted in after him, as if it owned the place.

"Mordechai," the man said to Zeidy. "Have you got a washer for a kitchen faucet? Mine's been dripping since April, and it's driving Captain crazy."

"Amos," said Zeidy warmly, coming around the counter. "Of course. Tuli, this is Mr. Whitlock, my neighbor. He lives upstairs next door, over the shoe repair. Amos, my grandson Naftali."

Mr. Whitlock looked down at Tuli with pale blue eyes in a face as brown and creased as an old baseball glove. He held out a hand. Tuli shook it. It was dry and strong.

"Naftali," Mr. Whitlock repeated carefully. "That's a fine name. Biblical, isn't it?"

"Yes, sir. One of the twelve tribes."

"Well, Naftali, this here is Captain." The dog sniffed Tuli's shoe, decided it was acceptable, and sat down on it.

Zeidy found the washer. Mr. Whitlock paid, and then he did not leave. He leaned on the counter, the way people do in a store where they have been coming for almost forty years, and talked. He had worked forty-one years for the B&O railroad, he told Tuli, most of that time as a signalman in the little tower down by the river. He had retired after a freight car crushed his leg. His wife had passed away six years ago. Now it was just him and Captain upstairs, and the river outside his back window.

"I've been watching that river since I was younger than you," he said. "My granddaddy used to tell me about the big one, back in 1868, when the water came right up into town and took the mills away. He said it rose so fast that men who went down to look at it couldn't get back up the hill."

"That was a long time ago," Tuli said.

"A long time for people," said Mr. Whitlock. "Not for a river. Rivers have long memories, son."
===
At a quarter to five, Zeidy closed the ledger and counted the cash box. Forty-three dollars and eighty cents. He wrote the number in the ledger in his small, careful handwriting, locked everything in the safe, and turned off the lights. Tuli carried the garbage out to the back.

Behind the store, a narrow alley ran along the backs of the buildings. Beyond it, the ground dropped toward the railroad tracks, and beyond the tracks was the river. In the late sun it looked almost gold. A heron stood on one leg on a rock near the far bank, perfectly still.

Mr. Whitlock was sitting on the back steps of the shoe repair, with Captain at his feet and a small radio on the step beside him. A man's voice was coming out of it, crackling.

"...the storm, named Agnes, is coming ashore today on the Florida panhandle with winds near hurricane force. Forecasters expect it to weaken as it moves inland over Georgia..."

"Florida," Tuli said. "That's far."

"Far enough," Mr. Whitlock agreed. He turned the radio off. He looked at the river for a long moment, and then up at the sky, which was clear and blue and empty all the way to the hills.

"Funny thing about storms, though," he said quietly, almost to himself. "They don't always stay where they're put."

Zeidy came out, locked the back door, and tugged it twice to be sure. "Good night, Amos. Good night, Captain."

"Good night, Mordechai. Good night, Naftali of the twelve tribes." Mr. Whitlock lifted his cane in a kind of salute.

As they drove back across the bridge, Tuli looked down at the Patapsco again. It was still low and lazy and brown. The heron was gone. He thought about camp, and the lake, and his friends. He did not think about Florida at all.

He would remember, later, that this was the last time he ever saw the river looking small.
--- words
foundation: the strong bottom part that something is built on
ledger: a book where a store writes down all the money that comes in and goes out
counter: a long flat table in a store where customers pay and are helped
signalman: a railroad worker who works the signals that tell trains when to stop and go
forecasters: people who study the weather and tell what it will probably do
