Imagine trying to measure the shaking of the ground while you are standing on it. Everything around you shakes too: the table, the paper, your own hand. That was the problem scientists faced for centuries. The machine that solved it is called a seismograph, from Greek words meaning "earthquake writer". Today, thousands of them, all over the world, record every earthquake big enough to measure, and many that no person ever feels.

People tried to build earthquake detectors long ago. Almost two thousand years ago, a Chinese scholar named Zhang Heng built a large bronze jar with eight dragon heads around the top, each holding a ball in its mouth. When the ground shook, a weight hanging inside the jar swung and pushed a ball out of one dragon's mouth into the mouth of a bronze toad below. The ball showed that an earthquake had happened and, people believed, which direction it came from. It could not measure how strong the shaking was, but it was a brilliant start.
===
The modern seismograph works on a simple idea you can test at home: a heavy object does not like to move. Hang a heavy weight from a string and quickly slide the top of the string sideways. The weight stays almost where it was, at least for a moment, while everything around it moves. Scientists call this inertia.

A seismograph uses inertia. Its frame is bolted firmly to the ground, so the frame shakes along with the earth. Inside the frame, a heavy weight hangs on a spring or a hinge, so it is free to stay still. In older seismographs, a pen was attached to the weight, and a drum covered with paper turned slowly underneath it, bolted to the frame. When the ground was quiet, the pen drew a straight line. When the ground shook, the drum and paper shook with the earth, but the pen, held by the heavy weight, stayed nearly still. The result was a zigzag line. The bigger the shaking, the bigger the zigzags.

That zigzag line is called a seismogram. Today, most seismographs no longer use pens and paper. They use magnets and electronics to measure the tiny difference between the still weight and the moving ground, and they send the numbers to computers. But the idea has not changed in more than a hundred years.
===
A seismogram does much more than show that an earthquake happened. An earthquake sends out different kinds of waves, and they travel at different speeds. The fastest, called P waves, push and pull the rock like a spring. They arrive first. The slower S waves shake the rock from side to side, like a rope being snapped. They arrive second. The longer the gap between the first wave and the second, the farther away the earthquake was.

One seismograph can tell how far away an earthquake was, but not in which direction. So scientists use three. Each station draws a circle on a map, with the right distance from that station. The three circles cross at only one point, and that is where the earthquake began. Finding a spot this way is called triangulation.

Seismographs also measure how strong an earthquake is. Scientists use a scale called the magnitude scale. Each step up the scale means much more energy, about thirty-two times more. An earthquake of magnitude 7 releases about a thousand times more energy than one of magnitude 5.

Today, a network of seismographs can locate a large earthquake anywhere on earth within minutes. Near the ocean, that speed saves lives, because a big undersea earthquake can cause a tsunami, a giant wave, and every minute of warning counts. The same machines watch volcanoes like Vesuvius day and night, because swarms of small earthquakes are often the first sign that magma is on the move. From a dragon jar to a worldwide network, the goal has stayed the same: to hear what the earth is saying before it is too late.
--- words
seismograph: a machine that measures how the ground shakes
inertia: the way a heavy thing keeps still unless something pushes it
seismogram: the zigzag line a seismograph draws
triangulation: finding a spot by measuring from three places
magnitude: how big and strong an earthquake is
tsunami: a giant ocean wave caused by an earthquake under the sea
