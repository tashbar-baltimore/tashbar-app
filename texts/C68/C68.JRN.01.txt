Benny Okafor-Levine had never traveled alone before. That was the first problem.

The second problem was that his brother Ezra's chasuna was in Cleveland, five hundred miles away, on a Tuesday night, and Benny had a state math exam on Tuesday morning that could not be moved. His parents had flown out on Sunday to help with the preparations. The plan was simple. After the test, Benny would take a bus from the city, change buses once, and arrive two hours before the chuppah. His mother had printed the tickets, written the schedule on an index card, and made him promise three times to call at every stop.

"It is just a bus," Benny told Eli at lunch on Monday. "How hard can it be?"

Eli looked at him over his sandwich. "Famous last words," he said.

The math exam went well. The first bus left on time. Benny sat by the window with his tefillin bag on his lap, a bag of pretzels and two apples from home, and the index card in his shirt pocket. He watched the city turn into fields, and he felt, for the first time, almost grown up.
===
The bus broke down two hours later, on the side of a highway, in the rain.

The driver walked down the aisle and announced that a new bus would come "within a few hours." People groaned. A woman began to call her office. Benny looked at the index card. His connecting bus would leave without him. He called his mother, and his voice came out smaller than he wanted it to.

"Stay calm," she said. "Let me see what I can find. Call me back in twenty minutes."

Across the aisle sat an old man in a gray coat with a small sefer open on his knee. He had been learning quietly the whole ride. He looked up at Benny and smiled.

"Your first time traveling alone?" he asked.

Benny nodded.

"Mine was in 1961," the man said. "A train, not a bus. I was going to my sister's wedding, too. I missed it by a whole day." He closed his sefer. "I am Mr. Ashkenazi. Where are you going?"

Benny told him. Mr. Ashkenazi took out a little notebook and a pencil, and together they looked at the map on Benny's phone. There was a small town six miles ahead with a train station. A train left from there at four o'clock and reached Cleveland at nine. The chuppah was at nine thirty.

"Six miles," Benny said. "In the rain."

"You will not walk it," said Mr. Ashkenazi. "You will ask."
===
Asking was the hardest part. Benny had always let his parents ask. Now he walked up to the front of the bus and asked the driver if anyone was going toward the town. The driver shrugged. Benny asked a man in a work jacket, who said no. He asked a young couple, who said no. He wanted to sit down and wait for the new bus with everyone else.

Then he saw a woman struggling to lift a heavy suitcase down from the rack while holding a baby. Without thinking, Benny reached up and lifted it down for her. She thanked him. Her husband, who had been on the phone outside in the rain, came back in, saw the suitcase on the floor, and asked who had helped.

"He did," his wife said.

The husband's brother was coming in a van to pick them up. They were going through the town with the train station. "Come with us," the man said. "There is room."

Benny turned back to Mr. Ashkenazi. "Will you come too?"

The old man shook his head. "My bus is the next bus," he said. "But you go." He tore a page from his notebook and wrote down his phone number. "Call me when you get there. I want to know how the story ends."

In the van, the baby fell asleep on Benny's shoulder. He did not move for forty minutes.
===
The train was crowded and slow. Benny davened Minchah standing between two cars, with the fields going by in the dusk. At nine o'clock exactly, the train pulled into Cleveland. His father was waiting on the platform in his suit, holding Benny's suit on a hanger.

"Change in the car," his father said. "We have twenty-five minutes."

Benny walked into the hall just as the music began. He stood next to his brother under the chuppah, in a suit that he had put on in the back seat of a car, with his hair still damp from the rain. Ezra looked over at him and grinned. Benny grinned back.

Later that night, during the dancing, Benny stepped outside and called the number on the page from the notebook.

"I made it," he said.

"I knew you would," said Mr. Ashkenazi. "Now tell me. What did you learn?"

Benny thought about it. About the broken bus, and the rain, and the suitcase, and the baby asleep on his shoulder.

"That it is not just a bus," Benny said. "And that you cannot get anywhere without asking, and without helping."

On the way home on Wednesday, Benny took the same bus. This time he sat next to a younger boy who was traveling alone for the first time, and who looked, Benny thought, exactly the way he must have looked on Tuesday. Benny took out his last apple and cut it in half.

"Your first time traveling alone?" he asked.
--- words
chasuna: a Jewish wedding
chuppah: the canopy under which a Jewish wedding takes place
connecting: joining one trip to the next one
struggling: trying hard to do something that is difficult
dusk: the time just after the sun sets
