The school archive was a narrow room behind the library, full of boxes that nobody had opened in years. Mr. Gross had given The Beacon permission to search it for the paper's seventy-fifth anniversary issue. "Old photographs, old programs, anything with a story," he said. "And put everything back where you found it."

Gavriel found the envelope on the second afternoon. It was tucked inside a dinner program from 1952, yellow and soft at the edges. Inside was a single sheet of paper covered in neat capital letters that made no sense at all.

WKH NHB LV XQGHU WKH WKLUG VWHS

"It's gibberish," said Yoni.

"It's a code," said Gavriel. He did not say much, but he noticed everything. "Look how neat the letters are. Nobody writes gibberish that carefully."

Akiva took the paper and frowned at it. "Then we need Benny. He reads everything."
===
Benny Okafor-Levine read the letters three times, his lips moving. Then he took out a pencil.

"We just read about this in class," he said. "Caesar's cipher. Every letter is moved the same number of places. If that's what this is, we only have to find the shift."

"How?" asked Eli. "There are twenty-five shifts. We could be here all night."

"We could try them all," Benny said. "That's brute force. But there's a faster way. Look at the first word. WKH. Three letters, at the start of a sentence, and it appears twice. What's the most common three-letter word in English?"

"The," said Akiva at once.

Benny wrote it out. "So W is T. That's a shift of three. K is H, also three. H is E, three again." He looked up, and for once even Eli was quiet. "It's three letters down. Just like Caesar."

Working together, they moved every letter three places back up the alphabet. Yoni read the result aloud, slowly.

"The key is under the third step."
===
For a moment nobody spoke. Then everybody spoke at once.

"Which third step?"

"There are a hundred steps in this building!"

"Maybe it's not even this building!"

Akiva held up his hand, the way he did in editorial meetings when everyone was talking. "Stop. Before we start pulling up stairs, what do we actually know?" He counted on his fingers. "The letter was inside a dinner program from 1952. So somebody put it there around then. Who would have hidden a letter in a program?"

Gavriel was already turning the program over. On the back was a printed list of names: the dinner committee. At the bottom, in the same neat capital letters as the coded note, someone had added a small line in pencil. MR. GOLDBERG, SHAMASH.

"The shamash," said Yoni. "He took care of the old building. Zeidy told me about him. He knew every corner of the place."

"The old building," Eli said slowly. "The one that's now the gym. It still has the old stone steps at the side door."
===
Mr. Gross came with them, because no one was allowed in the gym after hours without a teacher. The side door opened onto four worn stone steps. The third step had a crack along its front edge, and when Gavriel pressed on one end, the stone shifted slightly.

Underneath, wrapped in oilcloth, was a small tin box. Inside the box were not jewels or money. There was a brass key, a short letter and a photograph of a group of boys in jackets, standing on these same steps.

The letter was written in plain English this time. Akiva read it aloud.

"To whoever finds this: this is the key to the old aron in the bais medrash, which was put away when we moved. These are the boys of the first graduating class, 1952. If you have found this, you are clever enough to be good students. Learn well, and take care of this place. Yisroel Goldberg, shamash."

They stood on the steps for a long time, looking at the faces in the photograph.

"So that's our anniversary story," said Benny at last.

Akiva nodded. "That's our front page. But first," he said, smiling, "we find out what the key opens."
--- words
archive: a place where old papers and records are kept
gibberish: words or letters that make no sense
anniversary: the same date each year after something important happened
shamash: the man who takes care of a shul or study hall
brute: plain force; trying everything without a clever shortcut
