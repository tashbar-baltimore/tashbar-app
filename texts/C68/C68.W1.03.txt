Yoni found Mr. Dahan that same afternoon, before Minchah, in the boiler room, where he was tightening a pipe with a wrench as long as his arm. Yoni did not come with a list of hard questions. He came with two cups of hot tea from the teachers' room, because that was how Yoni talked to people.

"Mr. Dahan, can I ask you about the basement?"

Mr. Dahan wiped his hands on a rag and took the tea. "Ask. I know every pipe down here by its first name."

"The archive room, at the end of the hall. Do you ever go in?"

Mr. Dahan laughed and lifted the huge ring of keys from his belt. "I would love to. Every winter I want to check that wall for damp. But look." He held up one key after another. "New locks, new keys. That door has an old brass lock, from the first days of the building. Not one of these fits it. I tried, the first week I came. Then I asked the office, and they said the room stays locked. So it stays locked."

Yoni wrote it down. Then, because he was Yoni, he asked one more question. "Have you seen anyone go in?"
===
Mr. Dahan thought about it. "Seen, no. But I will tell you something funny. On Tuesdays and Thursdays, when I come down after Minchah to check the boiler, the light is on under that door. A thin yellow line. By the time I come back from the other end of the hall, it is off." He shrugged. "I thought maybe one of the rabbeim was working down there, with permission. Old buildings have their secrets. It is not my business."

"Only Tuesdays and Thursdays?"

"Only those two. I notice, because on Mondays and Wednesdays I have a second job, and I leave right after Minchah."

When Yoni told the others, Eli was very quiet. He sat down at the long table and turned his pencil over and over. Finally he said, to nobody in particular, "His keys don't even fit."

"No," said Akiva.

"I almost wrote his name on the board." Eli looked at the eraser in his hand. "What if I had said it out loud in the lunchroom? Half the yeshiva would think he was a thief by Minchah."

"But you didn't," said Akiva.

"Only because you took the marker."

Nobody had anything to add to that. Benny, at the end of the table, thought it was the most honest thing he had heard anyone say all week.
===
Gavriel had been gone most of the afternoon. He came back with his camera and a look on his face that the others had learned meant he had seen something.

"You know the old pictures in the front hallway?" he said. "The black-and-white ones nobody looks at?"

Everyone knew them. They hung in a long row outside the office, so faded that most boys walked past them a hundred times without seeing them at all.

"The first one is from 1951. The opening day." Gavriel put his camera on the table and made the picture bigger. Two rented rooms over a bakery. A few rows of boys in caps. In the middle stood a tall man with a short beard and a black hat: Rabbi Aharon Brandt, the founder.

"Look at his hand," said Gavriel.

In Rabbi Brandt's right hand, held up a little, as if he were proud of it, was a key. Hanging from it was a string, gray in the old photograph, but there was no mistaking the knot. It was the same key Rabbi Rosen had laid on their table. Or one exactly like it.

"The founder's key," whispered Yoni. "The one that was never found."
===
"There is more," said Gavriel. He moved the picture to the side. Next to Rabbi Brandt stood a thin boy of about twelve, with big ears and a serious face, holding his father's other hand. At the bottom of the photograph, someone had written in white ink: Opening Day, 1951. Rabbi Aharon Brandt and his son Feivel, age 12.

Benny felt the back of his neck go cold.

Akiva looked at the photograph for a long time. Then he looked at the whiteboard. He did not erase anything. Instead, under PROVED, he added three lines:

Mr. Dahan's keys do not fit the lock.

The light is on under the door on Tuesdays and Thursdays after Minchah.

Rabbi Brandt had a key on a string. His son was Feivel.

Under GUESSED, he wrote only one line: Who learns in the beis medrash on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons?

Benny knew the answer. They all did. Reb Feivel sat by the window with his Gemara almost every afternoon. But almost every was not the same as every.

"Tomorrow is Thursday," said Akiva. "We are not going to accuse anyone. We are going to watch, and we are going to learn. Literally." He took a Gemara off the shelf. "Bring yours."
