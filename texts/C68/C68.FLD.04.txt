For a long time after the lightning, Tuli and Zeidy stood at the back window and did not speak.

It was hard to see anything. Every few minutes the lightning came again, and each time the picture outside had changed. The water had crossed the alley. The water was against the back wall of the building. The water was pushing at the back door, and Tuli could hear it, a low, steady thudding, like someone knocking who would not go away.

"Zeidy," he whispered, so as not to wake Pinny. "What time is it?"

Zeidy shone the flashlight on his wristwatch. "Ten minutes to one."

"When will it stop rising?"

"When it stops raining," Zeidy said. "And then a little after. A river is like a person who is angry. Even after you stop shouting, the anger takes time to go away."

Downstairs, something gave a long, groaning creak. Then came a sound Tuli would never forget: a gurgling, rushing sound, coming from under the building, as if the floor itself had begun to drink.

Zeidy stood up.

"It is inside," he said.
===
They went to the top of the stairs and pointed the flashlight down. The beam shook a little in Tuli's hand.

The store was filling with water. It was not pouring in through the door, the way Tuli had imagined a flood would come. It was rising up through the floorboards, bubbling between the cracks, spreading across the floor from every direction at once, brown and thick and smelling of mud. As they watched, it covered the bottom of the long wooden counter. A cardboard box they had forgotten floated out from behind it and turned in a slow circle.

"Fourteen steps," said a sleepy voice behind them. Pinny was sitting up on his bolt of cloth, rubbing his eyes. "What's happening?"

Tuli thought fast. He had read somewhere that the worst thing you could do to a scared little boy was tell him not to be scared. So he did something else.

"Pinny," he said, "I need you to do a job. A real job. You sit right here, at the top of the stairs, with this flashlight. You know there are fourteen steps."

"Fourteen," Pinny agreed.

"Every time the water covers one more step, you tell us. Out loud. Can you do that?"

Pinny took the flashlight in both hands. He looked down the stairs at the dark water lapping at the bottom step. He swallowed.

"The water," he said in a very serious voice, "is on step one."
===
Outside, there were new sounds now. Voices, far up the street, shouting. A siren, closer than before. A loudspeaker, crackling through the rain from somewhere up the hill, the words broken by the wind.

"...evacuate... all residents of lower Main Street... the water... still rising... repeat, evacuate..."

Zeidy went to the front window and looked down at Main Street. Tuli came beside him.

The street was a river. Not the shining stream of the afternoon, running downhill, but something worse. The Patapsco had come up from the bottom of the hill, past the railroad bridge, and was pushing its way up Main Street, the wrong way, filling it like a bathtub. In the glow of a flashlight from a window across the way, Tuli could see that the water was already over the tops of the parked cars. One car had been lifted and turned sideways. It drifted slowly against a lamppost and stuck there.

"We cannot go out the front," Zeidy said. "Not with Pinny. The current would take him like a leaf."

Something big came sliding down the middle of the street, out of the dark. At first Tuli thought it was a boat. Then the lightning flashed, and he saw that it was a wooden porch, a whole porch, railings and steps and all, torn off some house down by the river. A rocking chair was still standing on it. The porch bumped against the lamppost beside the stuck car, spun once, and slid on into the darkness, and the rocking chair rocked as it went.
===
Tuli thought of the man who had filled all of Zeidy's burlap sacks with sand that afternoon. He hoped that man had gone up the hill. He hoped everyone who lived down there had gone up the hill.

Across the river, on the Oella side, a few small lights were moving among the trees, flashlights or lanterns, bobbing and stopping and bobbing again. People up there were awake too. Somewhere in Baltimore, Tuli knew, Abba was awake, saying Tehillim at the kitchen table, not knowing anything at all.

"Then what do we do?"

Zeidy looked up at the ceiling, at the square wooden hatch.

"If we must," he said, "we go up."

"The water," announced Pinny from the stairs, "is on step three."

Zeidy turned and looked down the staircase. Then he did something Tuli did not expect. He started taking off his shoes.

"Zeidy, what are you doing?"

"The ledger," Zeidy said. "And the cash box. They are in the safe. I thought the safe would keep them dry. But that safe is fifty years old, Naftali, and the door is not tight. If the water goes inside, the ledger is finished." He set his shoes neatly side by side on the top step. "Thirty-eight years. Everything I have built in this country is in that book. Every name."

"Zeidy, you can't go down there!"

"The water is only on step three. It is up to my knees, not more. I know where the safe is with my eyes closed." He rolled up his sleeves. "Two minutes. Hold the light for me."
===
He went down. Tuli stood at the top of the stairs beside Pinny and pointed the second flashlight after him, his heart pounding so hard he could feel it in his throat.

Zeidy waded across the store with his arms held out for balance. The water was cold and brown and full of floating things: a broom, a pair of work gloves, a cardboard sign that said WE HAVE IT. He reached the back office and disappeared through the doorway. Tuli could see only the faint glow of his flashlight moving on the office wall, and hear the sound of him breathing hard.

"The combination," came Zeidy's voice. "Hold on. My hands are wet. The dial is slipping."

"Step four," said Pinny.

Tuli knelt and shone his light down the stairs. It was true. The water had crept up another step. And it was not knee-deep anymore. Down in the store, a paint can that had been sitting on a low shelf lifted off and began to float.

"Zeidy!" Tuli called. "It's coming up faster!"
===
"I have it. One more number." A clank. A grinding, rusty sound. "The door is stuck. The water is pressing on it."

"Step five," said Pinny. His voice was not steady anymore.

Tuli put his foot on the top step. Then the next one. The flashlight beam jumped around the dark store like a frightened bird. He went down three steps until his sneakers were almost touching the water, and he could feel the cold coming up off it.

"Zeidy, please! Leave it! Come back up!"

From the office came a scraping and a splash, and then Zeidy's voice, breathless but triumphant. "I have it. I have them both."

He came out of the office doorway. He was holding the thick green ledger against his chest with one arm and the metal cash box in the other hand, high above the water. But the water was no longer at his knees. It was at his waist, and it was pulling at him. He took one step, and staggered, and caught himself against the counter.

"Step six," whispered Pinny.
===
And then, from somewhere beyond the wall, there came another sound.

A dog was barking. Not the ordinary barking of a dog who hears a stranger. This was wild, high, desperate barking, on and on, without stopping. It was coming from next door. From Mr. Whitlock's apartment.

Tuli turned his head. In the noise of the rain and the water, he had forgotten all about Mr. Whitlock. He had forgotten the steep outside stairs that went up the back of the shoe repair, the stairs that ended in the alley, the alley that was now under deep, rushing brown water. He had forgotten the bad leg that dragged on every step, and the ladder to the roof that only someone with two good legs could climb.

Mr. Whitlock could not get down. And if the water kept coming, he could not get up.

"Zeidy," Tuli said. "Captain. Listen."

Zeidy had stopped by the counter, the water swirling around him. He was listening too. In the beam of the flashlight, Tuli could see his face, wet and white, and his arms still clutching the ledger and the cash box, his whole life in America, held up out of the river.

"Step seven," said Pinny. "Tuli. Step seven."

The barking next door went on and on. Then, under it, through the brick wall between the two buildings, faint and hoarse, a man's voice called out. Tuli could not make out most of the words. But he heard one of them clearly, and it went through him like the cold of the water.

"Naftali!"
--- words
gurgling: making the bubbling sound that water makes when it flows unevenly
evacuate: to leave a dangerous place to get to a safe one
current: water that is moving strongly in one direction
staggered: walked unsteadily, almost falling
desperate: so worried or afraid that you will try anything
