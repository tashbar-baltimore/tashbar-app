At 5:12 in the morning on April 18, 1906, most of San Francisco was asleep. The city was the largest on the west coast of America, a busy port of about 400,000 people, with tall new office buildings downtown and wooden houses climbing its famous hills. Then, without any warning, the ground began to roll.

The shaking lasted less than a minute, but it was one of the strongest earthquakes ever to strike the United States. Scientists today believe it was about magnitude 7.9. Along almost 300 miles of the San Andreas Fault, the crack where two of the earth's plates grind past each other, the ground had slipped as much as twenty feet in a single moment. Fences that had stood in straight lines were suddenly broken in two, one half shifted far to the side of the other.

In the city, chimneys toppled into the streets. Brick walls cracked and fell. Buildings that stood on firm rock mostly survived, but buildings on soft, filled-in land near the bay shook far worse, because loose, wet ground shakes like jelly in an earthquake. Some sank into the ground.
===
The earthquake was terrible. What came next was worse.

All over the city, the shaking had broken gas pipes and knocked over stoves and lamps. Small fires started in dozens of places at once. In an ordinary week, the city's firemen could have put them out. But the earthquake had also broken the city's main water pipes. Firemen hooked up their hoses and found almost no water. The fire chief, the man who had planned for exactly this kind of emergency, had been badly hurt when a building fell during the earthquake, and he did not survive.

For three days, the fires spread. They joined together into one huge fire that moved across the city block by block. In desperation, soldiers and firemen tried to stop it by blowing up whole rows of buildings, to make empty gaps that the fire could not jump. Some of those explosions, set by men with little training, started new fires instead. In the end, the fire was finally stopped at a wide avenue, Van Ness, where the buildings on one side were cleared and the fire had nothing left to burn.
===
When it was over, about 28,000 buildings had been destroyed, across nearly 500 city blocks. More than half of the city's people had lost their homes. Thousands of people died, many more than the city's leaders admitted at the time; they worried that the true number would scare away the businesses the city needed to rebuild. Families camped in parks and on the beaches for months, cooking in the streets because a stove indoors was not allowed until chimneys were checked.

And yet, the city rebuilt with astonishing speed. Within a few years, most of the burned area was covered with new buildings. San Francisco held a world's fair in 1915 to show the world it was back.

The disaster taught engineers and scientists lessons that still protect cities today. Scientists studying the broken fences and roads came up with the idea of elastic rebound: rock along a fault bends slowly for years, like a stick being bent, until it snaps back all at once. That idea is still the basic explanation of how earthquakes happen. Engineers learned that soft, filled ground is dangerous to build on, that a city's water system must be built to survive the shaking, and that fire after an earthquake can do more harm than the earthquake itself.

The next big earthquake on the San Andreas Fault will come. No one knows when. But because of what was learned in 1906, San Francisco today has stronger buildings, a separate water system just for fighting fires, and a much better idea of what to do when the ground begins to roll.
