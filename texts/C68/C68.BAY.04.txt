On the Chesapeake Bay, a person who makes his living catching crabs, oysters and fish is called a waterman. The word is old, and so is the job. Some families on the bay have worked the water for more than two hundred years, with sons learning from fathers and grandfathers.

A waterman's year follows the seasons. The bay tells him what to catch and when. In warm months he goes after blue crabs. In cold months he turns to oysters. Between them come weeks of fixing boats, mending gear and waiting for the weather to change.

A waterman's day usually starts long before sunrise. He may be awake at three or four in the morning, drinking something hot in the dark kitchen and checking the wind. By first light, his boat is already out on the water. A waterman might say, "If you're not out there when the sun comes up, you're already late."
===
Crab season in Maryland runs from spring into late fall, and summer is the busiest time. Watermen use two main tools.

The first is the trotline, a long rope that can stretch for a mile or more along the bottom. Every few feet, a piece of bait, often a chicken neck or a chunk of salted eel, is tied to it. The waterman lays the line out early in the morning. Then he runs his boat slowly along it, lifting the line over a roller at the side. Crabs hang on to the bait as it rises, and he scoops them up with a long-handled net before they let go.

The second tool is the crab pot, a wire cage shaped like a cube. Crabs crawl in through funnels to reach the bait and cannot find their way out. A waterman may set hundreds of pots, each marked by a colored float. He hauls them up one by one, empties them, rebaits them and drops them back. It is hot, wet, heavy work, done over and over until the boat is full or the day's limit is reached.
===
When the water turns cold, the crabs bury themselves in the mud, and oyster season begins. In Maryland it runs from fall through the winter. Some watermen use hand tongs, two long wooden poles with metal rakes at the ends, joined like a giant pair of scissors. Standing at the edge of a rocking boat, the waterman opens the tongs, closes them on the bottom and pulls up a load of oysters and shell. Then he sorts them by hand, throwing back any that are too small.

Winter oystering can be bitter. Spray freezes on the deck, and wind cuts through even the warmest coat. A sudden storm can turn the bay dangerous in minutes, so a waterman learns to read the sky and to know when to head for home.

Some oysters are still caught by skipjacks, wooden sailboats with a single tall mast and a wide, low deck. They were built to drag oyster dredges under sail. Around 1900 there were hundreds of them. Today only a few dozen survive, and just a handful still dredge for oysters. They are often called the last working sailing fleet in North America. Maryland named the skipjack its official state boat in 1985.
===
Waterman communities are close and proud. Smith Island sits out in the open bay, about twelve miles from the mainland town of Crisfield. It has no bridge, so everything, from groceries to schoolchildren, travels by boat. Tilghman Island, on the Eastern Shore, is connected by a small drawbridge and has been home to skipjack captains for generations. In places like these, neighbors know every boat by the sound of its engine.

The work has changed a great deal. Wooden sailboats gave way to diesel engines. Radios, depth finders and GPS now help watermen find their way. Maryland sets limits on how many crabs and oysters may be taken and requires each waterman to hold a license. Some watermen have started oyster farms, growing oysters in cages or on leased plots of bottom.

There are also far fewer watermen than there once were. Catches are smaller, costs are higher, and many young people choose other jobs. Yet on any summer morning, before the sun is up, engines still rumble to life in harbors around the bay. A waterman might say, "The water's in my blood. I can't imagine doing anything else."
