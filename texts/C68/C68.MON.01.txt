title: From Barter to Coins

Imagine you have a basket of apples and you need a pair of shoes. Long ago, before money, you would have to find a shoemaker who wanted apples. If the shoemaker wanted wool instead, you were stuck. You would need to find someone with wool who wanted apples, trade with him, and then carry the wool back to the shoemaker. Trading one thing straight for another is called barter, and for thousands of years it was how people got what they needed.

Barter has a big problem. Both people must want what the other one has, at the same time. Economists call this the "double coincidence of wants." A farmer with a cow cannot easily buy a loaf of bread, because nobody will hand him change in small pieces of cow. A fisherman cannot save his catch for next winter, because fish spoil in a few days. Barter works in a small village where everyone knows everyone. It works badly in a large town, and it hardly works at all between strangers.

So people began to use certain goods as a kind of money. In different places, people used salt, grain, cattle, shells and pieces of metal. A good kind of money had to be easy to carry, hard to spoil and easy to divide into smaller amounts. It also had to be something that many people wanted, so that anyone would accept it. Salt and grain could be eaten. Shells were pretty and hard to find. Metal was the best of all: it lasted for years, it could be melted and divided, and a small amount was worth a lot.

Each of these early kinds of money still had weak points. Cattle could get sick. Grain could be eaten by mice. Shells could be gathered on a beach by anyone who found the right one. The more easily a kind of money could be made or lost, the less people trusted it.
===
Metal money did not start as coins. At first, people weighed pieces of silver or gold on a scale each time they traded. We see this in the Torah. When Avraham Avinu bought the cave of Machpelah to bury Sarah, he weighed out four hundred shekels of silver for Efron. The Torah adds that it was silver that merchants would accept. That small detail shows something important: even weighed silver was only useful if the people around you trusted it and agreed to take it.

Weighing silver at every sale was slow, and a buyer could cheat with silver that was mixed with cheaper metal. About 2,600 years ago, in the kingdom of Lydia, in what is now Turkey, rulers began to stamp small lumps of metal with a mark. The mark promised that each lump had a set weight and a set purity. These were among the first coins. A buyer no longer needed a scale. He could count the coins and trust the mark.

Coins spread quickly from one kingdom to the next, because they made trade so much easier. A merchant could carry his wealth in a small bag. A worker could be paid for a day's work and spend part of his pay today and part next week. A family could save for a wedding or a new roof. Prices could be written down and compared. For the first time, people could easily ask, "How much does it cost?" and get an answer everyone understood.
===
Even coins had problems. They were heavy when a merchant needed many of them, and they could be stolen on the road. About one thousand years ago, merchants in China began to leave their heavy coins with trusted shopkeepers and receive a paper receipt instead. Before long, people traded the receipts themselves. Later the Chinese government printed official paper money. A piece of paper is worth almost nothing by itself. Paper money works only because people trust that others will accept it.

In the United States, Congress set up the dollar and the United States Mint in 1792. For many years, American paper dollars could be traded at a bank for a set amount of gold or silver. That is no longer true. Today, a dollar is backed by the trust that people have in the government and in the country's economy. If people stopped trusting it, a dollar would buy very little.

Today, much of our money is not coins or paper at all. When a parent pays with a card or a phone, numbers move from one bank's computer to another. Nobody carries silver, and nobody weighs anything. Yet the idea underneath has not changed since Avraham weighed out silver that merchants would accept. Money is a promise. It works only as long as people trust it.

So the next time you pay for a slice of pizza, think about the long road that brought you there: from apples and shoes, to salt and shells, to weighed silver, to stamped coins, to paper, to numbers on a screen. Each step made trade easier. And each step depended on one thing that cannot be printed or stamped: trust.
--- words
barter: trading one thing straight for another, with no money
economists: people who study how money, work and trade are shared
purity: how much of a metal is real, with nothing cheaper mixed in
receipt: a paper that shows something was paid or left in your care
official: made or approved by the government or a leader
