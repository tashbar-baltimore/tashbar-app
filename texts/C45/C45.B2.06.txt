"Three flashes," whispered Tzvi Hirsch. "Three. Like the whistle. Three means help!"

"Or three means I'm looking for you," said Avrumi. His heart was racing. "Blow your whistle. Now!"

They both grabbed their whistles and blew as hard as they could. Three long blasts. Then three more. The sound went ringing out over the dark trees and came echoing back to them from the next hill.

They held their breath and stared at the spot where the light had been.

Nothing.

Then, there it was again. Blink, blink, blink. Then darkness. Then blink, blink, blink.

"They heard us!" shouted Tzvi Hirsch. "They're answering!"

"Maybe," said Avrumi. He tried to think clearly, the way his Abba would. "Or maybe they're just signaling, and they didn't hear us at all. It's very far."

They blew again. Three blasts. Three more. The light blinked three times, the way it had before. Then it went dark.

They waited for it to come back. They waited a long time.

It did not come back.
===
Tzvi Hirsch was already on his knees, crawling out from under the ledge. "Come on," he said. "It's down there. It's not that far. If we go straight toward it, we'll find them!"

He stood up in the dark and took one step toward the light. Then another.

Then he stopped.

Avrumi could not see his face, only the dark shape of him against the stars. The shape did not move for a long time.

"It's too dark," said Tzvi Hirsch at last, very quietly. "I can't see my feet. We'd fall. Or we'd go the wrong way, and then they'd come up here and find nobody." He turned around and crawled back under the ledge. "We stay. Right?"

"We stay," said Avrumi. And in the dark, where nobody could see, he smiled. A few hours ago, Tzvi Hirsch would never have stopped.

"But we keep signaling," said Tzvi Hirsch. "Every few minutes. So they know we're still here."

"Every few minutes," Avrumi agreed. "And we take turns sleeping. One sleeps, one listens."
===
Tzvi Hirsch took the first watch. Avrumi lay down with his head on his backpack, sure he would never fall asleep on cold sand in the middle of a forest. He fell asleep almost at once.

He dreamed he was at home, at the Shabbos table, and his little brothers were banging spoons. Then he realized the banging was Tzvi Hirsch, poking his shoulder.

"Your turn," whispered Tzvi Hirsch. "I blew it every few minutes, like we said. The light didn't come back. But one time I think I heard something. Like a voice, really far. Then nothing." He lay down and was snoring before Avrumi could answer.

Avrumi sat up and pulled his knees to his chest. He blew three blasts. He listened. He looked at the North Star, still in exactly the same place, while the Big Dipper slowly swung around it, like the hand of a giant clock. He blew three more blasts. The night went on and on. It was the longest night of his life.
===
When Avrumi opened his eyes again, the world had turned gray.

It was morning. Very early morning. But something was wrong with it. He could not see the trees at the edge of the hollow. He could not see the orange poncho. Everything past about ten steps was a soft, white blank.

"Fog," said Tzvi Hirsch, sitting up and rubbing his eyes. "Oh, no. Now nobody can see anything."

Avrumi looked at the white air, thinking hard. In his notebook, there was a line he had copied from his camp nature book: fog is a cloud that touches the ground. After a cool night, wet air near the ground turns into tiny drops of water, too small to see one by one. When the sun warms the air, the drops dry up, and the fog lifts. He had watched it happen over Pine Lake every morning that week.

"It'll lift," he said. "When the sun gets higher. It always does. And when it lifts, we need to be ready."

"Ready how?"

Avrumi opened his notebook to his new map. He pointed to the open stone patch, just past the big rock. "Remember this? The bare place with no trees? From the air, or from the next hill, that's the only spot anyone could really see. If we make a sign there, a big one, it could show them exactly where we are."
===
They found the stone patch easily. It was only about twenty steps from the ledge, close enough that they could still see the big rock behind them in the fog. It was a flat, bare slab of gray stone, about as big as their whole classroom, with cracks running through it and little puddles from the rain.

And all around its edges were white rocks. Some were as small as a fist. Some were as big as a loaf of challah.

"An arrow," said Tzvi Hirsch. "A giant arrow. Pointing to the ledge."

"Yes," said Avrumi. "Make it huge. As long as we can."

They worked for an hour. They carried rocks, rolled rocks, and dragged rocks. Tzvi Hirsch, who could not sit still for five minutes, turned out to be a champion rock carrier. Avrumi laid the rocks out in straight lines, the way he drew a map. Their hands got scraped and their backs ached. Little by little, a huge white arrow took shape across the gray stone, longer than three cars, pointing straight at their rock ledge.

Then Tzvi Hirsch went back to the hollow and climbed up to retie the orange poncho higher, right on top of the big rock, where it could be seen from every side.

When he came back, he stopped next to Avrumi. Both boys stood still.

Somewhere above them, above the fog, there was a sound. A thin, high buzzing, like a giant mosquito. And it was getting louder.

watch: a turn to stay awake and keep guard
blank: empty, with nothing in it
slab: a big, flat, thick piece of stone
champion: the very best at something
