Yehuda gave the umbrella back on Sunday night.

Zeidy opened the front door, saw the duck's head, and was quiet for a second. Then he took it and said, "Thank you, Yehuda'le." That was all. He did not ask where Yehuda had found it. He did not say anything about the history society. He just put the umbrella back in its stand and asked if Yehuda wanted a cup of tea.

All week, Yehuda carried his questions around like stones in his pockets. They were heavy in class. They were heavy at night. Why did Zeidy go alone? Why didn't he say anything? Who was Reb Z? And where was the sefer Torah now?

"Ask him," said Tzvi Hirsch at recess. "Just ask him."

"Maybe he's not ready," said Nosson.

"Ready for what?" said Tzvi Hirsch. "It's his own Zeidy!"

On Friday afternoon, Yehuda's family walked the few blocks to Zeidy and Savta's house for Shabbos. The sun was low and orange. Esther and Malka ran ahead in their Shabbos coats, and Yehuda carried the challah his Ima had baked, still a little warm in its bag.

Savta's house smelled the way it always did on Friday: of chicken soup, and fresh challah, and the spicy red fish that Savta made every week, with peppers and garlic and so much paprika that it glowed. Ima and Savta lit the Shabbos candles, and the whole house seemed to grow softer and quieter around them.
===
Zeidy made Kiddush in his deep voice. They washed, and Zeidy cut the challah and passed it around. Savta's fish came out, and everyone said it was the best ever, which they said every week, and which was true every week.

Yehuda tried. He really did. He ate his fish, and he answered when Abba asked about school, and he helped Malka cut her challah. But the questions were pushing at him from the inside, harder and harder, until they just came out.

"Zeidy," he said, "why did you go to the history society by yourself?"

Everyone at the table looked up.

Yehuda kept going. He could not stop. "You read the same page we read. You saw the name Mordechai Rosen. Did you know about Ohr Chodosh? Do you know who Reb Z was? Did your Zeidy ever take you to the harbor? Why didn't you tell us? We could have helped. We could have..."

"Yehuda," said Abba quietly.

Yehuda stopped.

Zeidy had put down his fork. He was not looking at Yehuda. He was looking at the Shabbos candles, and his face had closed, like a door.

"Excuse me," he said. He stood up slowly, took a sefer from the shelf, and went into the living room. A moment later they heard his chair creak.

Nobody said anything. Malka looked at Esther. Esther looked at her plate.

"Is Zeidy sad?" Malka whispered.
===
"Yehuda," said Savta brightly, "come help me bring out the chicken."

Yehuda followed her into the kitchen. His face was hot. He felt as if he had broken something, but he did not know what.

Savta did not take out the chicken. She sat down on the kitchen stool and patted the one next to her. Yehuda sat.

"I am going to tell you something," she said, "and then we are going to bring out the chicken, and you are going to be a good boy and not say anything about it at the table. Yes?"

"Yes, Savta."

"When Zeidy was ten years old," said Savta, "his Zeidy Mottel was already old and very weak. One Sunday morning, his Zeidy called him over. He said: Come with me today. I want to show you something. I want to see it one more time."

Yehuda held his breath.

"But Zeidy was ten," said Savta. "And that Sunday there was a ball game in the park with all his friends. So he said what boys say. He said: Next week, Zeidy. I'll come next week."

The kitchen was very quiet. Through the wall, Yehuda could hear Esther telling a story about her class.

"There was no next week," said Savta softly. "That Thursday, his Zeidy passed away."
===
Yehuda felt the stones in his pockets grow very heavy.

"So he never found out," he whispered. "What his Zeidy wanted to show him."

"Never," said Savta. "For more than sixty years, he has thought that he lost his chance. That he was the one who said next week." She smoothed her apron. "And then, on Chanukah, his grandson comes down a ladder with his Zeidy's map. It was as if his Zeidy knocked on the door. He wants to know, Yehuda. And he is afraid to know."

"Is that why he went alone?"

Savta nodded. "A man does not always want his grandson to see him cry."

Yehuda looked down at his hands. "And I asked him a hundred questions in front of everybody."

"You asked because you care," said Savta. "That is a good thing. But a question is like hot soup." She stood up and lifted the big pot lid, and steam rolled out. "You don't throw it at a person. You hand it to him, slowly, when he is ready to hold it."

Yehuda almost laughed, even though his eyes were stinging. He wiped them with the back of his hand, and Savta pretended not to notice.

"Now," said Savta, "carry the chicken. Carefully."
===
After the chicken, Yehuda went into the living room. Zeidy was sitting in his big chair, with the sefer open on his lap, but he was not reading. He was looking at the window.

"Zeidy," said Yehuda. He did not know how to start, so he just said it. "I'm sorry. I asked too many questions, too fast. I didn't think about how you felt."

Zeidy looked at him for a long moment. Then he put his hand on Yehuda's head, the way he did when he gave him a bracha every Friday night.

"A detective has to ask questions," he said. "That is what a detective is." He sighed. "My Zeidy would have liked you, Yehuda'le. He liked boys who asked."

Yehuda wanted to ask, Then why did you stop asking? But he remembered the hot soup, and he kept that question in his pocket.

They went back to the table together. Everybody sang zemiros, and Malka sang loudest of all, and the candles burned lower and lower. But Yehuda noticed something. Zeidy, who always sang the loudest, did not sing at all. He only listened, with his eyes half closed, and tapped the table softly with one finger.

On Shabbos day, Zeidy was kind and quiet. He did not say one word about the map.
===
On Motzaei Shabbos, after Havdalah, when the braided candle had hissed out in the wine and everyone had smelled the spices, Zeidy went upstairs. When he came down, he was carrying the flat wooden box.

He put it on the table in front of Yehuda, with the faded tallis bag on top. Then he put the rusty little key beside it.

"Yehuda," he said. His voice was gentle, but it was very firm. "I want you to take this back up to the attic. Put the map inside, the way you found it. Lock it. Tape the key underneath, where it was."

Yehuda stared at him. "But Zeidy, we're so close. We have the minute book, and the newspaper, and..."

Zeidy held up one hand, and Yehuda stopped.

"Some things," said Zeidy, "should stay lost."

He looked at the box for a moment longer, as if he were saying goodbye to it. Then he turned and walked out of the room.

Yehuda sat very still. In front of him were the box and the key. In his back pocket was his notebook, full of clues.

And in his head was one question that was bigger than all the others: what do you do when the person you most want to help asks you to stop?
