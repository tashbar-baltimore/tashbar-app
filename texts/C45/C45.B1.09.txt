All morning, the class could think of nothing but the surprise.

During math, Tzvi Hirsch guessed it was pizza. During spelling, Dovid guessed it was a trip to the science museum. At lunch, Binyamin said quietly that a good chess player does not guess; he waits to see the next move. Everyone told him that was very wise and also very annoying.

At two o'clock, Rabbi Feldman closed the door, set the silver box on his desk, and took out his phone.

"Boys," he said, "the esrog you saw yesterday came from very far away. I thought you should meet the man who grew it."

He pressed a button and set the phone on the desk. It rang twice. Then a warm, crackly voice filled the room.

"Shalom, shalom! Is this the famous class from Baltimore?"

"Reb Elazar," said Rabbi Feldman, smiling, "you are on the speaker. All twenty-two boys are listening."

Twenty-two boys said "Shalom!" at once, so loudly that Reb Elazar laughed.

"I heard," he said, "that my esrog had quite an adventure yesterday."

Rabbi Feldman told him the whole story: the empty box, the leaves on the stairs, the yellow note behind the radiator, the drawing, and the little wisp of flax. Reb Elazar listened without interrupting. When the story was over, he was quiet for a moment.
===
"You know," he said, "on my farm, we also have to be detectives. Let me tell you about my trees."

He told them that his orchard sat on a hillside in the north of Eretz Yisrael, where the summers are long and hot. He told them that esrog trees have sharp thorns on their branches, and that the wind can push a young esrog against a thorn and scratch it. "So we tie the branches carefully," he said, "and we check every esrog almost every day, from the time it is as small as a pea."

"Every day?" said Dovid. "For how long?"

"Many months. An esrog grows slowly. It stays on the tree through the whole summer." Reb Elazar chuckled. "My grandchildren say I know each esrog by its face."

Avrumi was drawing a hillside with rows of trees. Yehuda was writing so fast that his pencil squeaked.

"Reb Elazar," asked Binyamin, "how do you know which esrog to send?"

"Ah, a good question. I look, and I look again, in the morning light and the evening light. And when I am not sure, I do what your rebbe did. I ask a Rav." He paused. "A good farmer is not ashamed to ask. And it sounds like a good rebbe isn't either."

Rabbi Feldman smiled and looked down at the desk.
===
Before he said goodbye, Reb Elazar had one more thing to say. "Boys, I have a request. When you hold this esrog on Sukkos, when you make the bracha, please say a little tefilla for my family and my trees. Then this esrog will have traveled very far for a very good reason."

When the call ended, the classroom was quiet in a happy way.

"That," said Tzvi Hirsch, "was better than pizza."

"I am not finished," said Rabbi Feldman. "Here is the real surprise." He held up a sheet of paper. "On Chol HaMoed, every boy in this class is invited to my family's sukkah, a few at a time, to hold this esrog and make the bracha on the lulav and esrog, just as Reb Elazar asked. I made a list of groups."

The class cheered so loudly that Mrs. Rivkin opened the door, looked in, and closed it again with a smile.

Yehuda looked at the list on the board. The first group, Monday afternoon, had six names: Yehuda, Avrumi, Dovid, Binyamin, Tzvi Hirsch and Nosson.

"The Glen Avenue Crew," whispered Avrumi.

"Six of us now," said Tzvi Hirsch, and he punched Nosson lightly on the arm. Nosson punched him back, and both of them laughed.
===
On Sunday night of Chol HaMoed, at the Lerner house, the radio played the weather report while Dovid ate supper. "A strong wind is expected on Monday afternoon," said the voice. "Gusts may be strong enough to damage outdoor decorations. Please secure loose items."

Dovid put down his fork.

"Monday afternoon," he said slowly. "That's our turn in the rebbe's sukkah."

Then he pushed back his chair and ran down the stairs to the basement.
