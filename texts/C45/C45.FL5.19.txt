Good morning, everyone. My name is Nosson Abergel, and my report is about the man who made the electric light bulb practical: Thomas Edison.

Picture a long wooden building in a small New Jersey town called Menlo Park. It is 1879. Inside, there are glass jars, coils of wire, bottles of chemicals, and more than a dozen young men working late into the night. This is Edison's laboratory, and they have one goal: a light bulb that does not burn out in a few minutes.

Here was the problem. To make light, electricity runs through a thin thread inside the glass, called a filament. The filament gets so hot that it glows. But most materials glowed for a moment, and then burned up and broke.

So Edison and his workers tested material after material: platinum wire, paper, fishing line, cork, even hair. They tested thousands of things. Over and over, the light glowed and then went dark.
===
Then, in October 1879, they tried a plain cotton sewing thread, baked until it turned to carbon. They put it inside a glass bulb, pumped out almost all the air, and turned on the electricity.

The thread glowed. It kept glowing. One hour. Five hours. Ten hours. The men took turns watching it all night. It lasted more than thirteen hours.

But Edison was still not satisfied. Thirteen hours was not enough for a family's home. So the testing went on. About a year later, his workers found that a thin strip of carbonized bamboo could glow for more than a thousand hours.

People say that Edison once said he had not failed; he had only found thousands of ways that did not work. Nobody knows for sure if he said it exactly that way. But it is true that his team kept trying, again and again, until the light stayed on.

So the next time you switch on a lamp, remember: behind that tiny glow are thousands of tries that did not work, and one that did. Thank you.
--- words
practical: useful in real life, not only in an experiment
laboratory: a room where scientists do experiments
filament: the thin thread inside a bulb that glows
carbonized: baked until it turns into carbon, like charcoal
satisfied: happy that something is good enough
