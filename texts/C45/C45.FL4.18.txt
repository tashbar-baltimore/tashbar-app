Welcome, everyone, to the Brooklyn Bridge! Please stay on the walkway, and watch out for the bicycles. They come fast.

Look up at that tall stone tower. Now look at the thick cables that swing down from it, all the way across the East River to the next tower. This is a suspension bridge. The road hangs from those cables, the way a swing hangs from its ropes.

The man who planned this bridge was John Roebling. In 1869, while he was measuring the spot for a tower, a boat crushed his foot against a dock. He became very sick, and a few weeks later he died. The bridge had not even been started.

So his son, Washington Roebling, took over. Washington worked deep under the river, where the men dug out the bottoms of the towers. The air down there was squeezed and heavy. When he came up too fast, he got a terrible sickness called caisson sickness. After that, he could not walk to the bridge at all.
===
Did he give up? No! For years, Washington sat at his window and watched the work through a telescope. His wife, Emily, learned all about bridges. She carried his instructions to the builders every day, and she brought their questions back to him.

Fourteen years after John Roebling began measuring, the bridge was finished. It opened on May 24, 1883. It was the longest suspension bridge in the world.

But many people were afraid to cross it. Was it really strong enough? So, one year later, in 1884, a famous showman named Phineas Barnum had an idea. He led twenty-one elephants across the bridge, one after another, trunk to tail.

The bridge did not shake. It did not crack. It held.

And it is still holding today, more than one hundred and forty years later. So, are you ready? Let's walk across!
--- words
suspension: hanging from something above
cables: thick ropes made of twisted wire
caisson: a big box that lets workers dig under water
instructions: words that tell you how to do something
showman: a person who puts on big shows for crowds
