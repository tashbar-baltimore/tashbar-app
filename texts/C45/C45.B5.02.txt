For the next hour, they worked faster than they had ever worked in their lives.

Reb Shimon wanted the outside jobs finished before the weather turned. Dovid and Binyamin brushed a first coat of white paint on the railing they had scraped. Nosson carried tools inside and stacked them neatly by the stove. And Tzvi Hirsch helped Reb Shimon with the fog bell.

The fog bell hung from a frame on the gallery, the narrow walkway that ran all the way around the outside of the cottage. It was made of bronze, as big as a barrel, green with age. Long ago, on foggy nights when nobody could see the light, the keeper rang it every few seconds so that ships could hear where the lighthouse was. Its heavy clapper, the metal tongue that swings inside a bell, had been missing for years.

"We will never find the old clapper," said Reb Shimon. "It is somewhere at the bottom of the bay. But Dovid has an idea."

Dovid had brought a heavy iron bolt from his father's workshop. He wrapped one end in thick rope and hung it inside the bell. Then he swung it. BONNNG. The sound rolled out across the water, deep and long. Seagulls flapped up off the railing in a panic. Tzvi Hirsch laughed so hard he had to hold onto the frame.

"Not bad," said Reb Shimon, with a little smile. "Not bad at all."
===
But by then, nobody was laughing for long.

The wind had picked up. It came from the south in long, steady pushes, and it was getting stronger. Out on the bay, the water had changed. In the morning it had been smooth and gray-green, like a sheet of glass. Now it was choppy and dark, and the tops of the waves were breaking into white foam.

"Whitecaps," said Reb Shimon. "Do you know what makes them?"

The boys shook their heads.

"The wind. Wind blowing across water pushes it into waves. The stronger the wind blows, and the longer it blows, the bigger the waves grow. When the wind gets strong enough, it pushes the top of each wave faster than the bottom, and the top tips over and breaks into foam. When you see whitecaps all over the water, the wind is blowing hard enough to make a small boat's ride rough."

He looked at the Shoshana, tied to the ladder below them. She was bobbing up and down, up and down, and bumping against the iron leg with a hollow thud.

"Gentlemen," he said, "put down your brushes. We are going home now."
===
They climbed down the ladder into the boat. Reb Shimon started the engine, untied the rope, and turned the Shoshana toward Baltimore.

At first it was exciting. The waves came from behind them, and each one lifted the back of the boat, carried her forward with a rush, and set her down again. Cold spray flew up and splashed their faces. Tzvi Hirsch whooped.

But the farther they got from the lighthouse, the bigger the waves became. Out here, in the open bay, the wind had miles of water to build the waves. The waves were not just bumps anymore. They were hills of dark water, coming one after another from behind, close together and steep. Each one lifted the back of the Shoshana high and shoved her sideways, so that she slid and twisted down its front. A wave broke over the side and soaked Binyamin to the knees. Dovid grabbed his toolbox before it could slide away.

Nobody was whooping now.

Reb Shimon's face was calm, but his big hands were tight on the wheel. He slowed the boat and watched the waves for a long minute. Then he picked up the radio microphone.

"Coast Guard, Coast Guard, this is the workboat Shoshana, near Kettle Shoal Light."
===
A voice came back right away, crackly but clear. Reb Shimon explained: a small workboat, one adult and four boys, trying to cross to Baltimore.

The voice on the radio did not waste words. The storm was arriving many hours ahead of the forecast. The waves between the lighthouse and the harbor were already too steep for a small boat, and they would get worse all afternoon and all night. The Coast Guard's advice was clear.

"Return to the lighthouse and stay put," said the voice. "That structure has stood through more storms than any of us. It is the safest place you can be. We will check on you by radio through the night."

Reb Shimon thanked them and hung up the microphone. He turned the Shoshana around. Going back, heading into the waves, was slow and wet, but the Shoshana rode them much better with her pointed front cutting through each one, and in ten minutes they were tying up at the iron ladder again.

They climbed up, wet and quiet, and stood on the gallery.

Tzvi Hirsch looked toward Baltimore. He could not see it anymore. The city had disappeared behind a gray curtain of mist.
===
Inside, the cottage felt small and warm. Reb Shimon took out his cell phone. "Before anything else," he said, "we call home. Your parents must hear your voices."

One by one, the boys called. Nosson spoke softly to his father in French, then in English. Dovid's Abba asked a hundred questions about the screwpile legs and was actually a little jealous. Binyamin's Zeidy, who had taught him chess, said that a good player stays calm when the board surprises him.

When Tzvi Hirsch called, Ima answered on the first ring. He could hear the twins crying in the background and the bakery's big mixer roaring.

"We're okay, Ima," he said. "We're at the lighthouse. The Coast Guard says we have to stay. We can't come home for Shabbos."

There was a pause. Then Ima said, in a voice that was trying very hard to be calm, "Tzvi Hirsch. Do you still have the box?"

Tzvi Hirsch looked across the room. There it was, under the table where Nosson had set it, a little damp at one corner. Two challos. Grape juice. Chicken. Babka. Candles.

"Yes, Ima," he said, and suddenly his throat felt tight. "We have the box."

After the calls, Reb Shimon looked at the clock on the wall. It said 1:20. Shabbos would start at sundown, a little after seven. Then he looked at the four wet, quiet boys, and at the cardboard box under the table, and something warm came into his tired face.

"Well, boys," said Reb Shimon. "It seems we are making Shabbos here."
--- words
gallery: a narrow walkway with a railing around the outside of a building
clapper: the metal piece that swings inside a bell and makes it ring
choppy: full of short, rough waves
whitecaps: the white foam on top of waves that are breaking in the wind
forecast: what the weather is expected to be
structure: something that has been built, like a building or a bridge
jealous: wishing you had what someone else has
