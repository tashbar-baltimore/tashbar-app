Welcome, boys, to my bee yard! My name is Reb Gershon, and I have kept bees for thirty years. Please stand behind the rope. My bees are calm today, but they like their space, just like you.

Look at this wooden box. Inside it live about fifty thousand honeybees. That is more people than live in many towns! And every bee has a job.

There is only one queen. Her job is to lay eggs, up to two thousand eggs in one day. The male bees are called drones. And the rest, almost all of them, are the worker bees. The workers do everything else. They clean the hive, feed the babies, build the wax, guard the door, and fly out to find food.

Now, here is something amazing. When a worker bee finds a field full of flowers, how does she tell the others where it is? She cannot talk. So she dances!
===
Back inside the hive, she runs in a straight line, wiggling her body from side to side. Then she circles around and does it again. This is called the waggle dance. The direction of her run tells the other bees which way to fly. The longer she wiggles, the farther away the flowers are. It is like a map, made out of dancing.

And the honey? A worker bee drinks sweet nectar from flowers and carries it home. In the hive, the bees fan it with their wings until most of the water dries out. That thick, sweet liquid is honey.

But here is the surprising part. One worker bee, in her whole life, makes only about one twelfth of a teaspoon of honey. So every jar of honey on your table on Rosh Hashanah was made by hundreds and hundreds of bees, working together.

Any questions? Yes? No, you may not pet the bees. But you may taste the honey. Come!
--- words
drones: the male bees in a hive
nectar: the sweet juice inside a flower
direction: the way something is facing or going
liquid: something wet that can be poured, like water
surprising: not what you expected
