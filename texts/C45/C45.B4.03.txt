The big library downtown had tall stone columns, and inside it was as quiet as the beis medrash during a test.

It was Sunday morning. Yehuda's Abba had driven the six boys in the van, and he had made one rule on the way: "Inside, we whisper. Outside, Tzvi Hirsch can be as loud as he wants."

"That's not fair," said Tzvi Hirsch. "Why only me?"

"Because only you need it," said Binyamin, and everybody laughed, Tzvi Hirsch the loudest.

The Messenger, the librarian explained, was a Baltimore newspaper that had stopped printing a long, long time ago. But the library still had every page of it on microfilm.

"Micro what?" asked Dovid.

She showed them. Microfilm was a long roll of thin film, like the film in an old camera, with tiny photographs of newspaper pages on it, one after another. You put the roll on a machine with a screen, turned a knob, and the pages slid by, big enough to read.

Dovid's eyes lit up. "Can I turn the knob?"

"Gently," said the librarian.

The box for February 1904 was labeled in neat handwriting. Dovid threaded the film onto the machine, almost the way you would wind a sefer Torah, and turned the knob slowly. Old pages rolled across the screen, gray and crowded with tiny print.

Then Dovid stopped turning. The whole top of the page was one enormous headline.

THE GREAT FIRE. HEART OF BALTIMORE IN RUINS.
===
For a long time, nobody whispered anything at all.

There were drawings of whole streets with only broken walls left standing. There were lists of streets, and lists of companies, and lists of fire engines. Abba read parts of it out loud in a low voice, and Yehuda wrote fast in his notebook.

Later the librarian brought a history book about the fire, and Abba read from both. The fire had started on Sunday morning, February 7, 1904, in a big warehouse downtown. Strong winds pushed it from building to building. It burned all that day, all night, and into Monday, for about thirty hours. When it was over, more than 1,500 buildings were gone, across about 140 acres. That was almost the whole downtown.

"How many people were hurt?" asked Nosson.

Abba read for a moment. "Some were hurt," he said. "But as far as anyone knows, not one person died in the fire itself. The history book says people still talk about it."

"Baruch Hashem," said Binyamin quietly.

Firemen had come by train from other cities to help: from Washington, from Philadelphia, from New York. But when they arrived, many of them found a terrible problem. The ends of their hoses did not fit Baltimore's fire hydrants. Many cities used a different size.

"Like a key that doesn't fit the lock," said Dovid. He shook his head. "All that water, and they couldn't use it."

"They even tried dynamite," said Abba, reading, "to knock down buildings in the fire's path, so it would have nothing to burn."
===
"Page 6," said Yehuda. "February 10."

Dovid turned the knob. February 8. February 9. Then February 10. One page, two pages, and then a page with a heading across the top:

INCIDENTS OF THE GREAT FIRE.

Under it were many short pieces, one after another, each with its own small title. Some were sad, and some were strange, and one was about a parrot that was rescued from a hat shop and would not stop shouting.

Tzvi Hirsch had to cover his mouth with both hands.

Then Avrumi pointed at the screen. "There," he whispered.

The small title said: A YOUNG MAN AND HIS BUNDLE.

Abba leaned close and read it out loud, very softly:

"Late on Sunday, as the flames spread east, a young man in a cap was seen hurrying through the crowd with a long bundle in his arms, wrapped in a striped shawl. A wagon driver offered him a ride, but the young man refused, saying that what he carried, he would carry himself. He was last seen crossing a bridge over the Falls, going toward the harbor."

Nobody said a word.

Yehuda's pencil was shaking. "A young man in a cap," he whispered. "Going over the Falls. Toward the harbor."

"Just like the dots on the map," said Avrumi.

"A striped shawl," said Nosson. He looked at Yehuda. "What kind of shawl has stripes?"

Yehuda knew. Every boy at the table knew. Their fathers wore one every morning.

"A tallis," he said.
===
"It could be anybody," said Binyamin. "Lots of young men wore caps."

"Then let's find out more," said Yehuda.

The others wanted to go on to the next page, but Binyamin did not move. He was reading every short piece on page 6, one at a time, from the top of each column to the bottom. That was how Binyamin played chess, too. He never moved until he had looked at every square.

"Binyamin, come on," said Tzvi Hirsch. "We found it already."

"We found one piece," said Binyamin calmly. "There are four more columns."

So they waited, and Tzvi Hirsch drummed his fingers on the table without a sound. At the bottom of the fourth column, Binyamin found a long list called FIRMS BURNED OUT. It was in tiny print. He read down it with his finger, very slowly.

Then his finger stopped.

"Hollander and Sons," he read. "Caps and hats. German Street."

Nosson already had the photograph out of its envelope. Zeidy had let them borrow it for the day. Nosson held it up next to the screen. Across the top of the burned wall were the broken letters: ...LANDER & SO...

"Hollander and Sons," said Nosson softly. "On German Street. It matches."

"So that's the building in the picture," said Dovid. "And that's where the cap-makers' minyan was."

Yehuda wrote it down and underlined it twice.
===
Then Binyamin read the piece right under the list. It was very short. Its small title was: A HEBREW CONGREGATION BURNED OUT.

"Hebrew?" whispered Tzvi Hirsch.

"That is how many newspapers said Jewish, back then," said Abba. "Read it, Binyamin."

Binyamin read it in a careful whisper:

"Among the many losses was a small Hebrew congregation which met in a room on the top floor of the Hollander building, German Street. Nothing remains of the room. Its members fear that their sacred scroll was lost in the flames."

For a moment, the only sound was the hum of the microfilm machine.

"Sacred scroll," said Avrumi. "That means a sefer Torah."

Yehuda felt something cold in his stomach. He looked down at his notebook, at the words he had copied from Zeidy at the dining room table: Not one letter touched the fire.

"But Zeidy's Zeidy said he saved it," said Tzvi Hirsch. "The most precious thing in Baltimore. He said so!"

"And the newspaper says it burned," said Dovid.

Six boys looked at the screen. The words did not change.

Yehuda closed his notebook slowly.

"Then one of them is wrong," he said. "Either the newspaper is wrong, or Zeidy's Zeidy was."
--- words
microfilm: film with tiny photos of old pages, read on a special machine
enormous: very, very big
hydrants: posts on the street where firemen hook up their hoses
dynamite: a strong explosive used to blow things up
congregation: a group of people who daven together in one shul
sacred: holy and very special
