Going down a mountain should be easier than going up. But on Ridge Trail that afternoon, it did not feel easier at all.

Reb Akiva led the way, walking fast. He looked back every few steps and counted heads. "Eight, nine, ten," he said under his breath. "Eight, nine, ten." The boys walked in a tight line, closer than before. Nobody was talking about camp records now.

The cool breeze had become a wind. It pushed against their backs and rushed through the tops of the pine trees with a sound like the ocean. The sunny sky over their heads was turning the color of a dirty nickel. Behind them, in the west, the anvil cloud had grown so big that it covered half the sky.

Then they heard it. Far away, low and long, like a heavy table being dragged across a floor upstairs.

Thunder.

Reb Akiva stopped. "Boys," he said, "when we hear thunder, there is a bracha. Say it with me." Eleven voices said the bracha together: "Baruch Atah... shekocho u'gevuraso malei olam." Blessed is the One whose strength and power fill the world.

Tzvi Hirsch said it loudly. Saying it loudly made him feel a little less nervous.
===
"Keep moving," said Reb Akiva. "We have about an hour to the bottom. The storm may catch us. Ponchos keep us dry, but lightning is the danger, so we get off this high ridge fast. We stay together, we stay on the trail, and we keep going down."

They kept going down. They passed the second split, with its blue mark on a fat oak tree. Avrumi checked it against his map. They crossed a rocky part where the trail ran along the side of the hill. On their right, the ground went up in boulders. On their left, it sloped down steeply into thick bushes and young trees.

That was where the wind took Tzvi Hirsch's cap.

It was not just any cap. It was red, with a gold letter G on the front, for Gold's Bakery. His father had given it to him on the last day of school. His father had worn it himself when he was a boy, and the bakery had been his Zeidy's bakery then. Tzvi Hirsch wore it every single day of camp, even on Shabbos afternoon, when everybody teased him about it.

A gust came roaring up the slope. It snatched the cap right off his head. The cap tumbled once in the air, landed on the slope, and rolled away down the hill like a red wheel, bouncing between the bushes.

"My cap!" yelled Tzvi Hirsch.

And without one more word, he ran after it.
===
Avrumi was right behind him in the line. He saw Tzvi Hirsch step off the trail and go skidding down the slope in a little shower of dirt and stones.

"Tzvi Hirsch, wait!" Avrumi called. But the wind tore his words away.

Avrumi looked ahead. Reb Akiva was at the front of the line, helping Dovid over a slippery rock, with his back turned. The other boys had their heads down against the wind. Nobody else had seen.

Avrumi looked down the slope. Tzvi Hirsch's blue shirt was already disappearing into the bushes.

Avrumi had about one second to decide. He did not like deciding things in one second. He liked to look first. But his friend was going down the side of a mountain alone, with a storm coming.

He grabbed his whistle and blew three blasts, but a roll of thunder swallowed them.

"I'll bring him right back," Avrumi said to nobody, and he went down after him.

The slope was steeper than it looked. Avrumi slid on the loose dirt, grabbed a branch, slid some more. Thorny bushes caught at his pants. Below him he could hear Tzvi Hirsch crashing through the leaves.

"I see it!" Tzvi Hirsch shouted from somewhere below. "It's stuck on a bush! I almost have it!"

That was when the rain came.
===
It did not start with a few drops. It came all at once, like someone had turned over a giant bucket. One second the boys were dry. The next second the rain was so loud and so thick that Avrumi could not see more than a few trees ahead.

He found Tzvi Hirsch at the bottom of the slope, soaked, holding a stick. There was no cap at the end of it.

"It blew away again," said Tzvi Hirsch, panting. "It went that way, down into the gully. I almost had it."

"Forget the cap," said Avrumi. He was breathing hard. "We have to get back to the line. Now."

They pulled their ponchos over their heads. Avrumi's notebook was inside his shirt, but his map, the one from the camp office, had been in his pocket. When he pulled it out, it was a wet lump of paper. It came apart in his fingers.

They started climbing back up.
===
Climbing up was much harder than sliding down. The dirt had turned to mud. Every few steps one of them slipped and had to grab a root or a rock. Water ran down the slope in little brown streams, right into their sneakers.

They climbed for a long time. Longer, it seemed to Avrumi, than they had slid down.

At last the ground leveled out. They stood up, gasping, with rain pouring off their ponchos. They had reached the top of the slope.

But there was no trail.

There were trees, and rocks, and rain. There was no rocky path running along the side of the hill. There were no boys in a line. There was no Reb Akiva.

"Reb Akiva!" Tzvi Hirsch shouted. "Reb Akiva! Bunk Daled!"

Only the rain answered.

"We must have come up in a different place," said Avrumi. His voice sounded strange to him, too high. "The trail has to be close. We just have to find a blue mark."

The two boys turned in a slow circle, looking at every tree.

Pine trees. Oak trees. A white birch. Gray trunks, brown trunks, trunks covered in green moss.

They turned all the way around.

There was not one blue mark on any tree.

slope: ground that goes up or down at a slant, like the side of a hill
gully: a narrow ditch made by running water
leveled: became flat
gasping: breathing in quickly and hard, with an open mouth
