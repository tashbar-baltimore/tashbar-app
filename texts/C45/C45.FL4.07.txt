Binyamin Yusupov did not like to lose. Not at chess, not at punchball, not at anything.

So when Zeidy Yusupov set up the chessboard on Motzei Shabbos and said, "Tonight, I will not go easy on you," Binyamin sat up straight and cracked his knuckles.

The game started well. Binyamin moved his knight. Zeidy moved a pawn. Binyamin took one of Zeidy's bishops, and he could not stop smiling. He was sure, completely sure, that tonight would be the night he finally won.

But then Zeidy moved his queen, very quietly, all the way across the board.

"Check," said Zeidy.

Binyamin stared. He moved his king. Zeidy moved his rook.

"Check," said Zeidy again, softly.

Binyamin's face grew hot. His hands felt cold. He looked at every square on the board. There was nowhere left to go.

"Checkmate," said Zeidy.
===
Binyamin wanted to sweep the pieces off the table. He wanted to say, "That's not fair!" He wanted to run to his room and close the door.

Instead, he took a long, slow breath. He counted to five in his head, the way his rebbe had taught him.

Then he did something very hard. He looked up and said, "That was a good move, Zeidy. How did you see it?"

Zeidy's eyes lit up. He set the pieces back, exactly where they had been, and showed Binyamin how the queen had been sneaking closer, move by move, while Binyamin was busy being happy about the bishop.

"You see?" said Zeidy. "When you win, you learn a little. When you lose, and you ask why, you learn a lot."

Binyamin nodded. Then he set up the board again.

Zeidy laughed his big, warm laugh and pushed the plate of almonds across the table.

"One more game," said Binyamin. "And this time, Zeidy, I'm watching your queen."
--- words
knuckles: the bumps where your fingers bend
pawn: the smallest chess piece
checkmate: the end of a chess game, when the king cannot escape
sweep: to push things off with one big move of your arm
sneaking: moving quietly so nobody notices
