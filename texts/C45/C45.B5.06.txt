"A green light," Nosson said, louder now. "Reb Shimon! There's a green light out there, low on the water!"

Reb Shimon pushed himself up from the chair and came to the window. He stood behind the boys and looked out, squinting. For a long moment he did not see it. Then the backup lamp flashed above them, and in the dark that came after, the tiny green spot appeared again, bobbing.

"Yes," he said. "I see it. That is a boat."

"How do you know it's the Miss Ellie?" asked Dovid.

"I don't know for sure," said Reb Shimon. "But no one else would be out in this."

He went to the radio. Behind him, the four boys kept their faces against the glass, watching the green light so hard that their eyes began to water.

"Why green?" whispered Tzvi Hirsch. "Why not white, like a flashlight?"

It was Binyamin who answered. He had been reading about boats all week, ever since Reb Shimon invited them, because Binyamin always studied the board before a game.

"Every boat has two side lights," he said quietly. "Green on the right side. Red on the left side. Sailors call the right side starboard and the left side port. So if you see a boat's green light, you're looking at its right side."
===
Tzvi Hirsch frowned. "So what?"

"So," said Binyamin, and his voice got faster, the way it did when he saw a winning move on the chessboard, "think about it. If we can only see the green light and not the red, then the boat's right side is facing us. That means the front of the boat is pointing..." He traced a little boat shape in the air with one finger, then an arrow. "That way. To the right. Toward the white water. Toward the shoal."

Nosson looked at Binyamin's finger, then out at the green light. "And it isn't steering," he said. "It's just going where the wind pushes it."

Reb Shimon had been listening with the microphone in his hand. He looked at Binyamin for a long second, the way a teacher looks at a student who has just said something smart.

Then he pressed the button. "Coast Guard, this is the keeper at Kettle Shoal. We have a boat in sight, south and a little west of the light, about a mile out, maybe less. We see only her green side light. She is drifting north and west, toward the shoal."

"Kettle Shoal, thank you," said the Coast Guard. "Our rescue boat is fifteen minutes out. Keep watching. Tell us anything that changes."
===
So they watched.

It was the longest fifteen minutes any of them had ever lived through. The four boys stood shoulder to shoulder at the south window. Every few seconds, one of them called out what he saw, and Reb Shimon repeated it into the radio.

"Still green," said Nosson. "Still bobbing."

"It's a little closer to the white water," said Dovid. "I think."

"There's a light on the boat now, a white one, moving," said Tzvi Hirsch. "Like someone walking with a flashlight."

"That's the captain," said Reb Shimon softly. "Checking his son, perhaps. Or the anchor."

Tzvi Hirsch thought about that. Somewhere out there, a father was walking on a pitching, rolling deck in the dark, with the wind screaming and the rain in his face, to check on his son. He thought about his own father, braiding challah at five in the morning. He thought about how scared his own father would be, if it were him out there.

Without deciding to, he began to whisper a chapter of Tehillim that he knew by heart. A moment later, he heard Nosson whispering it too, in his soft French accent, and then Dovid, and then Binyamin. Reb Shimon, at the radio, closed his eyes and moved his lips with them.
===
"Anchor," said Reb Shimon suddenly. "If he can get his anchor down, it might catch on the bottom and hold the boat before the shoal."

"Can he do that?" asked Dovid.

"If the water is shallow enough, and if the anchor catches. The bottom here is soft mud. Sometimes an anchor drags right through it." Reb Shimon pressed the microphone again and passed on the idea to the Coast Guard, who passed it on to the Miss Ellie.

The boys pressed closer to the glass.

The green light bobbed. It slid a little to the right. A little more. Then it seemed to stop.

"Did it stop?" said Tzvi Hirsch. "I think it stopped!"

"Wait," said Binyamin. "Count. Watch it against the flashes."

They watched for three flashes of the lamp. Eighteen seconds. The green light stayed in the same spot, swinging a little, but not sliding.

"It's holding," breathed Reb Shimon. "The anchor caught. Baruch Hashem, it's holding."
===
Then, far to the north, a new light appeared. It was bright and white and swept back and forth across the water like a giant broom: the searchlight of the Coast Guard rescue boat. It came closer, rising and falling over the huge waves, throwing up white spray. The radio crackled with voices, calm and quick, talking to each other in words the boys did not understand.

The searchlight swept left. It swept right. It found the green light and stopped, holding it in a bright white circle. For the first time, the boys could see the shape of the Miss Ellie: a small, white fishing boat, tipped and rolling, with a tall pole in the middle and a dark cabin.

"They found her," said Nosson.

The boys held their breath. Then, just as the rescue boat slowed beside her, a huge wave rose up between them, dark and enormous, and the searchlight beam slid off the fishing boat and swung wildly across the empty water.

When it swung back, the spot where the green light had been was dark.

The green light was gone.
--- words
starboard: the right side of a boat, when you face the front
port: the left side of a boat, when you face the front
pitching: rocking up and down hard, front and back
anchor: a heavy hook on a chain that is dropped to hold a boat in place
searchlight: a very strong lamp that can be pointed far away
enormous: very, very big
