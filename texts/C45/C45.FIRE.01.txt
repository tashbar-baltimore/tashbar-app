title: Fifty Years Later: A Firefighter Remembers
grade: 5
kind: speech
topic: The Great Baltimore Fire of 1904
source: original
---
This speech is made up, but the facts about the fire are true. Imagine an old firefighter named Captain Walter Briggs. In 1954, fifty years after the fire, a class of boys visits his old firehouse in Washington. He sits down on a bench next to a shiny red fire engine and tells them what he saw.

Boys, come close, and I will tell you about the biggest fire I ever fought. I was nineteen years old. I had been a firefighter for less than a year.

It was Sunday, February 7, 1904. Just after lunch, the bell in our firehouse rang and kept on ringing. A telegram had come from Baltimore, about forty miles away. Their whole downtown was burning, and they were asking for help. Our chief picked two engine companies to go. I was in one of them.
===
We did not drive there. In those days a fire engine was pulled by horses, and forty miles was too far. So we loaded our engine, our hoses and our horses onto a special train. The train raced north faster than I had ever traveled in my life. I remember pressing my face to the window. Even before we reached the city, I could see a great black cloud in the sky ahead of us. When it grew dark, the bottom of that cloud glowed orange.

When we stepped off the train, the heat hit my face like an open oven. The wind was blowing hard, and it carried sparks and burning bits of wood from one roof to the next. Whole buildings, six and seven floors tall, were on fire from top to bottom. Glass windows cracked and fell into the street. The noise was like a train that never stopped passing.
===
We pulled our engine to a fire hydrant, and I ran to hook up our hose. I pushed the end of the hose against the hydrant and began to turn it. It would not turn. I tried again. Our captain pushed me aside and tried it himself. Then he stood up, and I will never forget his face.

"It doesn't fit," he said. "Our hose doesn't fit their hydrant."

Boys, every city made its hose ends and hydrants a little differently in those days. The ends of our Washington hoses were the wrong size for the Baltimore hydrants. It was like trying to open your front door with your neighbor's key. We had come forty miles to fight the biggest fire in the country, and we could hardly use a drop of water.

We did not give up. We wrapped the joint with canvas and held it together with our hands. Some water got through, and a great deal sprayed out the sides and soaked us. It was February, so that water froze on our coats. By midnight my sleeves were stiff as boards.
===
All night and all the next day we fought that fire. We were so tired that some men fell asleep standing up. I saw firemen from Philadelphia, from New York and from many other cities. Many of them had the same trouble with their hoses. I also saw men try dynamite. They blew up buildings in the fire's path, so the fire would have nothing to eat. Sometimes it worked. Sometimes it only made more fires.

The people of Baltimore were kind to us. A man from a bakery brought us coffee and bread in a wagon. He would not take a penny for it.

The fire finally stopped on Monday, after about thirty hours. In the east, it stopped at a river called the Jones Falls. Our men and the Baltimore men held it there with every hose we could get water through. When it was over, more than fifteen hundred buildings were gone.
===
So what did I learn, boys? I learned that brave men are not enough. You need the right tools, and the tools have to fit together. After that fire, people all over the country began to talk about making every hose end the same size.

I am an old man now, and my memory is not perfect. I may have some of the small details wrong. Fifty years is a long time. But I will never forget the moment my captain stood up and said, "It doesn't fit." Nobody who was there could forget it.
--- words
telegram: a short message sent fast over a wire, before there were telephones in every home
hydrant: a post on the street where firefighters hook up their hoses to get water
canvas: a strong, heavy cloth
dynamite: a strong explosive used to blow things up
joint: the place where two things are joined together
