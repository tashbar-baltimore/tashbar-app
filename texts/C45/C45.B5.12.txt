The opening of the new wall was on a Tuesday evening, three weeks later. The bay was calm and gold in the setting sun, and two boats carried everyone out to Kettle Shoal Light: the Shoshana, and a bigger boat that the museum had borrowed for the night.

There were more people than the little cottage had held in a hundred years. All six boys of the Glen Avenue Crew, in their Shabbos shirts. Their fathers. Rabbi Feldman, their rebbe, and Mr. Halpern, their science teacher. Two people from the museum, and a Coast Guard officer in a white uniform. Reb Shimon, in his navy blue cap, looking a little nervous, which none of the boys had ever seen before. Tzvi Hirsch's father had sent a whole tray of rugelach, which the boys guarded like treasure.

The wall was on the north side of the cottage, where the old keepers' bunks had been. The boys had worked on it for three weeks, after school and on Sundays, with the museum's help.

At the top, in big blue letters, it said: ONE NIGHT, MANY EYES.
===
Under the title, side by side, hung four frames.

The first held the Bay Courier article, with a small card beside it. The card said: Written the next morning by a reporter who was not there, from a phone call. Notice what it leaves out.

The second held the Coast Guard report, with its military times. Its card said: Written by officials, for the record. Short, careful and exact. It tells when, but not how it felt.

The third held a copy of Reb Shimon's log. Its card said: Written by the keeper, who was in the lighthouse, on Motzaei Shabbos, from memory.

The fourth held Captain Turner's story, which Tzvi Hirsch had written down carefully, and which the captain had read and signed. Its card said: Told by the captain of the Miss Ellie, who saw the night from a dark, rocking boat a mile away.

Below the four frames was Avrumi's chart, redrawn neatly in ink, showing where the four accounts agreed and where they did not. And beside it, in a glass case, lay the old 1903 to 1905 logbook, open to February 7, 1904, with its two short sentences about the red glow over the burning city, and the pencil note in the margin.
===
At the very bottom of the wall was a fifth frame. It was smaller than the others. It held a page from Tzvi Hirsch's own notebook, in his own handwriting, which was not very neat. The museum people had wanted to type it, but the boys had said no. It had to be in his handwriting.

It said:

I was one of the four boys in the lighthouse that night. This is what I learned. Everyone who tells about something sees it from his own place. The reporter, the Coast Guard, my great-uncle, the captain, and me. Nobody saw everything. So if you want to know what really happened, you have to listen to all of them, and ask where each one was standing, and what he could see, and why he was telling it. That is what a good detective does. My friend Yehuda taught me that. And my Uncle Shimon taught me something else. If someone helps you, even if he never knows it, you have to say thank you.

Rabbi Feldman read it twice. Then he put his hand on Tzvi Hirsch's shoulder and did not say anything at all, which Tzvi Hirsch understood was the best thing a rebbe could say.
===
Then there was the sound of a boat engine, and everyone went out onto the gallery.

A small white fishing boat was pulling up to the iron ladder. Its side was freshly painted, and its name was written in new blue letters: MISS ELLIE. Captain Turner stood at the wheel, and Cody stood at the front, his hand healed, holding the rope.

The captain climbed up the ladder with something heavy wrapped in an old canvas sack. He set it down on the table, in front of Reb Shimon, and pulled the sack away.

It was a clapper. A big, heavy bronze clapper, green and crusty with age, shaped like a giant teardrop, with a ring at the top.

Reb Shimon stared at it. Then, very slowly, he reached out and touched it.

"When they towed the Miss Ellie off the shoal," said Captain Turner, "this came up, tangled in our anchor chain. The museum lady says it's from your fog bell. It's been lying on the bottom out there for fifty years, maybe more." He cleared his throat. "Seemed to me it should come home."

Dovid made a sound like a balloon losing its air. "The clapper," he whispered. "The real one. The one I made the bolt for."
===
They hung it in the old fog bell that same evening, Dovid and Cody and Captain Turner together, with Reb Shimon telling them exactly how. When it was ready, Reb Shimon stepped back.

"Tzvi Hirsch," he said. "You ring it."

Tzvi Hirsch grabbed the rope. He looked out over the gold, still water, toward Baltimore, where the lights of the city were starting to come on. He thought about the keeper in 1904, watching the red glow. He thought about the keeper in 1971, ringing this bell all night in the fog, never knowing that a nineteen-year-old named Shimon Gold was out there listening. He thought about Captain Turner, looking for the light like his own front door.

He pulled the rope.

BONNNNG.

The sound rolled out over the bay, deep and long and clear, the voice the bell had not had in fifty years. Far away, on the shore, a seagull answered.

Later, after the rugelach were gone and the guests had begun to climb down to the boats, Reb Shimon sat down at the table with the old logbook. He took a pencil from his pocket. Under his own note from many years before, he wrote one more line, in his careful, shaky hand.

S.G. This light, and four boys, helped two more get home.

Then he closed the book, and above them, right on time, the beacon came on.

THE END

Kettle Shoal Light, its keepers, the Miss Ellie and the people in this story are made up. Screwpile lighthouses are real, and some still stand in the Chesapeake Bay. The Great Baltimore Fire of 1904 is real.
--- words
nervous: a little worried or uneasy
treasure: something very valuable that you keep safe
clapper: the metal piece that swings inside a bell and makes it ring
tangled: twisted together in a messy knot
crusty: covered with a hard, rough layer
canvas: a strong, heavy cloth
