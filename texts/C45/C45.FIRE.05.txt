title: Why the Hoses Did Not Fit
grade: 5

---
During the Great Baltimore Fire of 1904, fire engines raced to help from Washington, Philadelphia, New York and other cities. Brave firemen jumped off the trains, ready to fight. Then many of them found that their hoses would not fit Baltimore's fire hydrants. How could something so small cause such a big problem? To understand, you need to know how a hose hooks up to a hydrant.

A fire hydrant is a short metal post on the sidewalk. Under the street, it is joined to the big water pipes of the city. On the side of the hydrant is a round metal opening called an outlet. A firefighter takes off its cap and joins a hose to it. Then he opens a valve, and water rushes through the hose to the fire engine, which pumps it out hard and fast.
===
The hose is joined to the hydrant by a coupling. A coupling is a metal ring at the end of the hose. Inside the ring are threads. Threads are the spiral ridges you see on the top of a jar or the end of a screw. The outlet on the hydrant has threads too. When the firefighter turns the coupling, the two sets of threads fit into each other and pull tight. Now the water cannot leak out.

Here is the problem. For the threads to fit, two things must match. The rings must be the same size across, and the threads must be spaced the same way. If either one is different, the coupling will not turn. It is like trying to put the lid of one jar onto another jar. It might look almost right, but it will not close.
===
In 1904, there was no rule that every city had to use the same size. Each city, and sometimes each company that made fire equipment, chose its own. Washington's couplings were one size. Baltimore's outlets were another. Many of the engines that came to help simply could not get water from the hydrants.

The firemen did what they could. Some wrapped the joints with canvas or rope and held them together with their hands. Water sprayed out everywhere, and only a little reached their engines. Others stood by and watched, unable to help, while the fire spread. Nobody can know for sure how much sooner the fire might have stopped if every hose had fit. But almost everyone agreed that it would have helped.
===
The problem was not new. Fire experts had talked about it for years. But it took a disaster as big as the Baltimore fire to make people act. In 1905, the year after the fire, a group of fire experts agreed on one standard size for hose couplings and hydrant outlets. A standard is a rule that everyone agrees to follow, so that things fit together. If every town used the standard, a fire engine from anywhere could hook up anywhere.

But changing every hydrant and every hose in the country costs a lot of money and takes a long time. Many towns kept their old sizes for years. Even today, not every town uses the standard size, so some fire departments carry small adapters. An adapter is a short piece that has one size on one end and another size on the other.

The lesson of the hoses goes far beyond fire engines. Plugs and outlets, batteries, train tracks and even the size of a sheet of paper all follow standards. When everyone agrees to make things the same way, people can help each other much more easily. In 1904, Baltimore learned that lesson the hard way.
--- words
outlet: an opening that something comes out of
valve: a part that opens and closes to let water flow or stop it
coupling: a metal ring that joins the end of a hose to something else
threads: spiral ridges, like the ones on a screw or the top of a jar
standard: a rule everyone agrees to follow so that things fit together
adapters: short pieces with one size on one end and another size on the other
