Dear Science Museum,

My name is Dovid Lerner. I am eleven years old, I live in Baltimore, and I am writing to ask you a question that nobody in my house can answer. My Abba is an engineer, so that is saying a lot.

Last month, I decided to build a machine that waters my Ima's plants while we are away for Pesach. I used a plastic bottle, a long tube, a battery, a small pump from an old fish tank, and a timer. I drew the plan three times. I tested every part by itself. Every part worked.

Then I put all the parts together, and the machine flooded the kitchen.

Not a little. A lot. The water ran under the refrigerator, across the floor, and into my sister's slippers. My mother was very patient. My sister was less patient.

So I took it apart and tried again. The second time, the pump did not turn on at all. The third time, it watered the plants beautifully for one hour, and then it stopped, and I do not know why.
===
My friend Binyamin says that in chess, you learn the most from the games you lose. My friend Yehuda says I should write down every test in a notebook, like a detective, so I can find the clue. My rebbe says that the Mishnah in Pirkei Avos teaches, "Lo habayshan lamed": a person who is too embarrassed to ask cannot learn.

So I am not embarrassed. I am asking.

Here are my questions. Why would a pump work for one hour and then stop? Could the battery be getting tired? Could the tube be getting blocked? And do real engineers flood kitchens too, or is it only me?

I have attached my drawing and my notebook. Please write back. I will keep trying either way, but I would like to stop mopping.

Thank you very much for your time.

Sincerely,
Dovid Lerner
Glen Avenue, Baltimore
--- words
engineer: a person who plans and builds machines, bridges or buildings
flooded: covered with too much water
patient: calm while waiting or when things go wrong
embarrassed: feeling shy and silly in front of others
attached: added along with something else
