Out on the library steps, Tzvi Hirsch was finally allowed to be loud.

"It's not fair!" he said. "We found the young man, we found the building, we found the minyan, and then the newspaper says the sefer Torah burned!"

"The newspaper says the members fear it burned," said Yehuda. He had his notebook open on his knee. "Fear. That's not the same as knowing."

Binyamin nodded. "If they knew, they would have said so."

Abba was smiling. "So what would a good detective do now?"

"Find something written later," said Nosson. "When people knew more."

Abba took out his phone. "The history society," he said. "The Jewish history society, near the old part of town. They keep old papers from Baltimore shuls. Let me see if anyone is there on a Sunday."

Someone was. His name was Mr. Pollack, and he was the archivist, which meant he took care of the society's old papers. He said six careful boys could come at two o'clock.

They were there at a quarter to two.

The archive room was in the back, behind a heavy door. It was cool and dry inside, and it smelled like the attic, only cleaner. Gray boxes lined the shelves.

"Three rules," said Mr. Pollack. He was a short man with a short beard and a pencil behind each ear. "Clean hands. Pencils only, never pens. And no food." He looked at Tzvi Hirsch, who quietly put a sfenj back in his pocket.
===
Yehuda told Mr. Pollack everything they had found: the map, the photo, the Hollander building, the cap-makers' minyan, and the newspaper that said its sefer Torah was lost.

Mr. Pollack listened with his head on one side. "After a fire like that," he said, "a little minyan with no room would need a new place to daven. Usually another shul took them in. And shuls kept minute books."

"Minute books?" asked Dovid. "Books about minutes?"

"Books about meetings," said Mr. Pollack. "Every time the leaders of a shul met, someone wrote down what they decided. Who would lead the davening. What to fix on the roof. Who was getting married." He walked down the shelves and ran his finger along the labels. "Here is one I know. A small shul in East Baltimore, on the other side of the Falls. Ohr Chodosh."

He set a tall, thin book on the table. Its cover was cracked, and its pages were brown at the edges, just like the map.

"It is in Yiddish," he said. "I will read it to you in English."

He turned the pages slowly. Avrumi leaned so close that Mr. Pollack gently moved his glasses back with one finger.

Then Mr. Pollack stopped. "Here," he said. "March 1904. One month after the fire."

He read it slowly, translating word by word.
===
"Sunday, the thirteenth of March. It was decided that the members of the Cap-Makers' Minyan, whose room was burned in the great fire, may daven with us until they find a home of their own. Their holy sefer Torah, which the young man Mordechai Rosen carried out of the fire with his own hands, wrapped in his tallis, will rest in our aron kodesh."

Yehuda stopped breathing.

"Mordechai Rosen," he said. "Mottel. That's Zeidy's Zeidy!"

"Carried out of the fire," said Tzvi Hirsch. "With his own hands!" He forgot about whispering and nearly shouted it, and then put his hand over his mouth.

"Then the newspaper was wrong," said Dovid.

"Wait," said Yehuda. His heart was pounding, but he made himself slow down. "We have two papers that say two different things. How do we know which one to believe?"

Mr. Pollack raised his eyebrows. He looked as if he liked that question very much.

Avrumi thought out loud. "The newspaper was printed on February 10. That was only a day or two after the fire. The whole city was still smoking. Nobody knew anything yet."

"And it only says the members fear it was lost," said Binyamin. "They weren't sure."

"But the minute book was written a month later," said Nosson. "By people who could open their aron and see it."

Yehuda wrote it down: The newspaper was early and not sure. The minute book was later and sure. He looked up. "So the minute book is stronger proof."
===
"And there is more," said Mr. Pollack. He ran his finger along the next line. "On the night of the fire, it rested in the shop of..."

He stopped.

The boys leaned in. Across the bottom of the page was a wide, wavy brown mark, the shape of a cloud. The ink underneath it had spread and blurred into nothing.

"A water stain," said Mr. Pollack, with a sigh. "Maybe a leak in the roof, a hundred years ago. It happens to old books more than you would think."

"Can you read any of it?" asked Yehuda.

Mr. Pollack tilted the lamp and held a magnifying glass over the stain. They all waited. Dovid held his breath so long that his face turned pink.

"There are only a few letters I can see," Mr. Pollack said at last. "In the shop of Reb... and then a letter that could be a zayin. After that, nothing."

"Reb Z," said Yehuda, writing it down. "The shop of Reb Z."

"A shop near the harbor," said Avrumi. "On the safe side of the Falls. Where the star is."

Yehuda looked at his notebook. In one week, the clues had grown from three Hebrew letters to a whole page. A young man in a cap. A striped shawl. A bridge over the Falls. A minyan in the Hollander building. A shop of Reb Z, near the harbor.

And still the same question: where was the sefer Torah now?
===
Mr. Pollack closed the minute book very carefully. "I will make you a copy of this page," he said. "And I will tell you something else. You are not the first person this week to ask me about the Cap-Makers' Minyan."

The boys looked at him.

"On Thursday," said Mr. Pollack, "an older gentleman came in. A gray beard, a kind face. He asked about the fire of 1904. I showed him this same book. He sat in that chair for almost an hour, reading this same page." Mr. Pollack shook his head. "He did not write anything down. He did not say very much. And then he left in such a hurry that he forgot this."

He reached under the table and brought out a big black umbrella.

Its handle was carved out of wood, in the shape of a duck's head.

Tzvi Hirsch opened his mouth to say "quack," and then he closed it again.

Yehuda stared at the umbrella. He had seen it in the stand by Zeidy's front door every week of his life. And Zeidy had not said one word about the history society. Not on Friday night, not at shul on Shabbos, not once.

Mr. Pollack looked from the umbrella to Yehuda's face.

"That's my Zeidy's," Yehuda whispered.
