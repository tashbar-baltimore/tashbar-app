Avrumi shook the flashlight. He banged it gently against his hand. He clicked it off and on, off and on.

Nothing.

"The rain got inside it," he said quietly.

Tzvi Hirsch didn't say anything. He looked at the sky. The sun was gone behind the ridge now. The trees were turning from green to gray to black, one by one, as if someone was slowly turning down a dimmer switch on the whole mountain.

"Okay," said Avrumi, and he made his voice sound braver than he felt. "We have to get ready before it's all the way dark."

They worked fast. They brushed the wet leaves out from under the rock ledge and found that the ground underneath was dry and sandy. They spread the green poncho on the sand, like a floor. They put their backpacks at one end, for pillows. They lined up their water bottles and the trail mix where they could find them by feel.

By the time they crawled under the ledge, it was so dark that Avrumi could barely see his own hand.
===
It is hard to describe how dark a forest is at night, if you have only ever slept in a house or a bunk. In Baltimore, there is always a little light: a streetlight, a porch light, the glow of the city on the clouds. Here there was nothing. When Avrumi closed his eyes, it was dark. When he opened them, it was exactly as dark.

And the forest was not quiet. That surprised him most. There were drips, and creaks, and rustles. Something small ran across the dry leaves, skitter-skitter, and stopped. Far away, an owl called: hoo, hoo-hoo, hoo. A branch fell somewhere with a long, slow crack.

The boys sat shoulder to shoulder with their knees pulled up. Their clothes were still damp. Their socks were soaked. Avrumi's teeth started to chatter, and he clamped his jaw shut so Tzvi Hirsch wouldn't hear.

"Avrumi," said Tzvi Hirsch in the dark, after a long time.

"What?"

"I'm scared."

Avrumi was so surprised that for a moment he did not answer. Tzvi Hirsch Gold, who jumped off the high dock at the lake, who went first on every ride, who once stood up to a bunch of big boys teasing a kid on the bus. Tzvi Hirsch was scared.
===
"Me too," said Avrumi.

"Really?"

"Really. My teeth are chattering. I was trying to hide it."

Tzvi Hirsch laughed a little, a shaky laugh. Then he was quiet again. When he spoke, his voice was different, very low.

"It's my fault," he said. "All of it. If I didn't run after that cap, we'd be at camp right now. Eating supper. You'd be in your bunk, drawing. Your parents wouldn't be worried. It's all because of me."

Avrumi thought about this for a long time. He wanted to say the right thing.

"You know what my Abba does," he said finally, "when he makes a mistake in a sefer Torah?"

"What?"

"He doesn't throw the whole thing away. And he doesn't just sit there feeling bad about it, either. He stops. He looks very carefully at what went wrong. Then he fixes it, one careful letter at a time. He says a mistake isn't the end of the story. It's the part where you learn to be careful."

Tzvi Hirsch didn't answer. But after a moment, Avrumi felt him lean a little closer.

"Running after the cap was a mistake," said Avrumi. "But then you stopped. You listened. You stayed. That's the fixing part. And you had the idea with the orange poncho."

"Yeah," said Tzvi Hirsch. His voice sounded a little steadier. "I did, didn't I?"
===
"We should say Shema," said Avrumi.

So they did. They said the words together, out loud, in the dark, the same words they said every night in their beds at home, the same words their fathers and grandfathers had said. Avrumi put his hand over his eyes, even though it was already dark. Tzvi Hirsch said the words slowly and carefully, the way they had davened Mincha.

When they finished, something had changed. The forest was still dark. Their socks were still wet. But the scared feeling in Avrumi's chest had gotten smaller, small enough to fit in a pocket.

"Hey," said Tzvi Hirsch suddenly. "Look up. Through the trees."

Avrumi leaned out from under the ledge and looked up.

The clouds were gone. The storm had blown them all away to the east. Through a gap in the branches, the sky was full of stars, more stars than Avrumi had ever seen in his life, thousands and thousands of them, like sugar spilled across a black tablecloth.
===
"I know some of these," said Avrumi, excited now. His Abba had shown him on their hike. He searched the sky, and there it was: seven bright stars in the shape of a big pot with a long, bent handle.

"The Big Dipper," he said, pointing. "See the pot? Now look at the two stars on the front edge of the pot. They're called the pointer stars. Draw a line through them, up and out, about five times as far. See that star, all by itself? Not very bright, but alone?"

"I see it."

"That's the North Star. It's the only star that stays in the same place all night. All the other stars turn around it. If you face it, you're facing north. Always."

Tzvi Hirsch stared at it for a long time. "So even without a compass," he said, "even without a flashlight, you can always know which way is north."

"Always," said Avrumi. "As long as the sky is clear."

They sat for a while, looking at the stars and not saying anything. Avrumi felt his eyes getting heavy.

Then Tzvi Hirsch grabbed his arm. "Avrumi! Down there!"

Far below them, through the black trees, a tiny white light was shining. As they watched, it blinked. Once. Twice. Three times.

Then it went dark.

Then it blinked three more times.
--- words
dimmer: a switch that makes a light slowly brighter or darker
skitter: to run with quick, light steps
chatter: to knock together fast, like teeth when you are cold
clamped: held something tightly shut
steadier: more firm and calm, less shaky
pointer: something that points the way
