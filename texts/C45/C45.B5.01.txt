"Ima, it's a work trip," said Tzvi Hirsch Gold. "We'll be home by one o'clock. Why do we need a whole box?"

His mother did not stop packing. Into a sturdy cardboard box from the bakery went two round challos, still warm. A bottle of grape juice. A foil pan of cold roast chicken. A whole chocolate babka. A box of matches, two Shabbos candles in little tin cups, and a stack of paper plates.

"Because," said Ima, taping the box shut, "your Uncle Shimon always says the same thing."

From the kitchen, Tzvi Hirsch's twin baby brothers banged their spoons on their high chairs, as if they agreed.

Tzvi Hirsch rolled his eyes and said it with her, in a deep, slow voice, the way his great-uncle said it: "The bay keeps its own schedule."

"Exactly," said Ima. "Just in case."

It was a Friday, the week after Purim. There was no school, and Tzvi Hirsch had been waiting for this day for a month. His great-uncle, Reb Shimon Gold, had invited him and three friends to come out to Kettle Shoal Lighthouse and help get it ready for spring.
===
Reb Shimon was waiting for them at the dock in the harbor at half past seven, next to a small, sturdy workboat with SHOSHANA painted on the side in blue letters. He was about seventy-five years old, with a white beard, a navy blue cap and the biggest, gentlest hands Tzvi Hirsch had ever seen. For forty years he had been an engineer on ships, down in the hot, roaring engine rooms where the big motors lived. Now he was retired, and he spent his time taking care of an old lighthouse.

"Welcome aboard, gentlemen," he said in his soft, gravelly voice.

The three friends climbed in after Tzvi Hirsch. Nosson Abergel, quiet and polite, sat by the side and watched the water. Dovid Lerner had brought a toolbox, because Dovid always brought a toolbox. Binyamin Yusupov sat very still in the middle of the boat, the way he sat in front of a chessboard, thinking several moves ahead.

Tzvi Hirsch set the box from the bakery under a seat. Reb Shimon looked at it and raised one white eyebrow.

"My mother says just in case," said Tzvi Hirsch, a little embarrassed.

"Your mother," said Reb Shimon, "is a wise woman."

The engine coughed, then rumbled, and the Shoshana pulled away from the dock.
===
They motored out past the big cargo ships, past the cranes that looked like giant metal giraffes, and out into the wide, gray-green water of the Chesapeake Bay. The morning was cool and calm. Seagulls followed the boat, crying.

After almost an hour, Nosson pointed. "There it is!"

Far out in the water, where there was no land at all, stood a little white house.

As they came closer, the boys could see it better. The house had six sides, like a honeycomb cell, with a red roof shaped like a hat. On top of the roof sat a round glass room with a black iron railing around it. That was the lantern room, where the light lived. And the whole house stood high above the water on iron legs, like a table standing in a bathtub.

"Why is it on legs?" asked Dovid.

"Good question," said Reb Shimon. He slowed the boat. "Long ago, lighthouses in the ocean were built of heavy stone. But the bottom of this bay is not rock. It is soft mud, many feet deep. A heavy stone tower would sink. So the builders came up with a clever idea. They made each leg out of iron, with a wide, flat spiral at its bottom, like a giant screw. Then they turned each leg, around and around, and screwed it deep into the mud."

"A screwpile," said Binyamin, who had read about it the night before.

"A screwpile lighthouse," said Reb Shimon, pleased. "And the legs do a second job. Waves can pass right under the house, between the legs, instead of smashing into a wall. The bay goes under, and the house stays dry."

"Only one thing worried the old keepers," he added. "In a very cold winter, thick sheets of ice could float down the bay and push against the legs like a giant hand. Ice was the one enemy these legs feared."
===
Kettle Shoal Light had been built in 1883, Reb Shimon told them as he tied the Shoshana to the iron ladder. For many years, keepers had lived inside it with their families, keeping the lamp burning every single night. A shoal is a place where the water is very shallow, he explained, with sandbars just under the surface. A ship that runs onto a shoal can get stuck, or even break apart. The light warned ships to stay away.

Today, the light was automatic. A modern lamp on top, run by the Coast Guard, turned itself on whenever it grew dark and flashed one white flash every six seconds, all night long.

"Every lighthouse has its own pattern," said Reb Shimon. "Some flash red. Some flash twice. Ours flashes white, once every six seconds. A captain who sees that pattern in the dark knows exactly which lighthouse it is, and so he knows exactly where he is."

The cottage itself was now a little museum. Reb Shimon was its volunteer keeper. Every spring, he scraped and painted and fixed whatever the winter had broken.

The boys climbed the ladder, one by one. Inside, the house smelled of old wood and salt. There was a small kitchen with a black iron stove, a table with four chairs, bunks built into the wall, and shelves of old logbooks with cracked leather covers. A barometer hung by the door, a round brass dial with a needle that showed the pressure of the air.
===
They worked all morning. Dovid and Binyamin scraped peeling paint off the railing. Nosson swept out the lantern room. Tzvi Hirsch held the ladder for Reb Shimon and talked the whole time, until Reb Shimon laughed and said that Tzvi Hirsch could talk the paint right off the wall without a scraper.

Reb Shimon turned on the lighthouse radio, the kind ships use to talk to the Coast Guard, to hear the weather. A calm voice said that a big storm was coming up the coast. "It is expected to reach the Chesapeake Bay late Saturday night," said the voice, "with strong winds and high water."

"Saturday night," said Tzvi Hirsch. "Motzaei Shabbos. We'll be home way before that."

At eleven o'clock, Reb Shimon stopped working. He walked over to the barometer by the door and tapped its glass gently with one finger, the way he probably did a hundred times every spring.

The needle jumped, and settled lower. Reb Shimon looked at the little pencil mark he had made beside the dial at eight o'clock that morning. The needle was far below it.

Reb Shimon did not say anything for a moment. The boys stopped scraping. They had never seen him look so serious.

"When the air pressure drops like that," he said quietly, "a storm is coming. And coming fast." He looked out the window, to the south, where the sky over the water had turned a strange yellow-gray.

"That storm," said Reb Shimon, "is not waiting for Motzaei Shabbos."
--- words
sturdy: strong and hard to break
retired: stopped working at a job, usually after many years
automatic: working by itself, without a person running it
shoal: a place where the water is very shallow, with sand just under the surface
volunteer: someone who works without being paid, because he wants to help
barometer: a tool that measures the pressure of the air, to help tell the weather
pressure: how hard something pushes
