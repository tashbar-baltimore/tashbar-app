Every lighthouse keeper keeps a log. It is a book where he writes down, hour by hour, what happens at the light: the weather, the ships that pass, anything that goes wrong. Reb Shimon did not write in his log on Shabbos, of course. But on Motzaei Shabbos, after Havdalah, he sat down at the little table and wrote everything that had happened that long night, while it was still fresh in his mind. He let the boys read it. This is what it said.

KETTLE SHOAL LIGHT. KEEPER'S LOG.
Written on Motzaei Shabbos about Friday night. All times are as close as I remember them.

8:52 p.m. Main beacon failed during a heavy squall. Wind from the south, very strong. Water very high on the legs.

8:58 p.m. Coast Guard call to all boats: Kettle Shoal light is out. Fishing boat Miss Ellie reports engine failure, two aboard, drifting somewhere south of the light.

9:06 p.m. Asked Coast Guard if they need the light. Answer: yes, at once. Pikuach nefesh, as my Rav instructed me years ago.

9:15 p.m. Backup lamp lit and set to the regular pattern: one white flash every 6 seconds.
===
9:20 p.m. Nosson A. sighted a green side light, south and a little west, under a mile out. Binyamin Y. worked out from the green light that the boat's right side faced us and she was drifting north and west toward the shoal. Reported this to Coast Guard.

9:22 p.m. Coast Guard rescue boat about 15 minutes away.

9:25 to 9:35 p.m. All four boys at the south window, calling out what they saw. Passed every report to Coast Guard. The boys said Tehillim. So did I.

9:36 p.m. Suggested the Miss Ellie try her anchor. Coast Guard passed it on.

9:39 p.m. Green light stopped moving. Watched for three flashes to be sure. Anchor holding.

9:41 p.m. Rescue boat searchlight seen to the north.

9:44 p.m. Searchlight found Miss Ellie. Then a large wave, and we lost sight of her green light. A terrible minute.
===
9:46 p.m. Radio: Coast Guard has the Miss Ellie. The large wave knocked the boat over onto her side and broke her green light, but she came back up. The anchor held. Captain and son both wearing life jackets, both safe.

9:47 p.m. The boys cheered so loudly that I am sure the Coast Guard heard it without the radio.

10:30 p.m. Coast Guard reports Miss Ellie is stuck on the edge of the shoal, on her side, but safe until morning. Captain and son warm and dry aboard the rescue boat. They will tow the Miss Ellie off at high tide in daylight.

11:15 p.m. Wind beginning to ease. Water no longer rising.

11:40 p.m. The boys asleep on blankets on the floor, all four, in a row, like sardines. Did not have the heart to wake them to move to the bunks.

1:00 a.m. Main beacon still dark. Backup lamp steady. Checked it every hour through the night.

5:30 a.m. Storm passed. Rain stopped. Stars over the bay.

6:45 a.m. First light. A calm, gray morning.
===
That was the end of the log for the night. But Tzvi Hirsch remembered the morning better than any log could tell it.

He woke up on the floor, stiff and warm under a scratchy wool blanket, with Dovid's elbow in his ear. For a moment he did not know where he was. Then he heard the silence. No wind. No booming waves. No rain on the shutters. Only the soft lap of small waves on the iron legs and the cry of a seagull.

He got up and went to the window. The bay was flat and gray and quiet, like a sheet of metal. Logs and branches and pieces of wood floated everywhere, pushed out of rivers and creeks by the storm. Far to the south, he could see a small white fishing boat lying on her side at the edge of the shoal, like a tired dog asleep in the sand. The Miss Ellie. A Coast Guard boat waited beside her.

Reb Shimon was already standing at the east window in his tallis, davening Shacharis, swaying slowly. Behind him, the sun was rising out of the bay, pink and gold, and its light lay across the water in a long, shining road.

One by one, the boys woke up and joined him at the window. They davened together, quietly, facing the sunrise. When Tzvi Hirsch had first opened his eyes that morning, he had whispered Modeh Ani, and he had never meant it so much in his life.
===
After davening, they made Kiddush with the last of the grape juice and ate the Shabbos meal with what was left of the challah and the chicken. Then there was nothing to do but rest. The boat could not take them home until after Shabbos. It was the quietest, calmest Shabbos morning any of them had ever had.

Nosson went to the shelf of old logbooks by the door. They were the logs of the old keepers, the ones who had lived here long ago, with cracked leather covers and yellow pages. Reb Shimon said they could read them, as long as they were careful. Nosson took one down. On the spine, in faded gold letters, it said 1903 to 1905.

He turned the pages slowly. They were full of neat, old-fashioned handwriting in brown ink. Wind northwest, light. Schooner passed, bound for Baltimore. Fog. Rang the bell all night.

Then he stopped on one page. He read it twice. Then he said, "Tzvi Hirsch. Come look at this."

The entry was dated February 7, 1904. In the old keeper's neat brown writing, it said: A great red glow to the northwest all night. Baltimore is burning.

But that was not what made Nosson call him. In the margin, next to it, someone had written a second note, much later, in gray pencil, in a shaky but careful hand.

S.G. 1971. This light saved my life.
--- words
log: a book where you write down what happens, day by day or hour by hour
squall: a sudden, strong burst of wind, often with rain
sighted: saw something, especially something far away
sardines: small fish packed very close together in a can
schooner: a sailing ship with two or more tall masts
margin: the empty space along the edge of a page
