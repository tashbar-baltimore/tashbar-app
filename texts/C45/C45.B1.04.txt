The footsteps on the stairs were slow and heavy. Step. Step. Step.

Six boys pressed themselves against the wall of the third-floor hall. There was nowhere to hide. On one side was Rav Mizrachi's closed door, with the two voices still murmuring behind it. On the other side was the staircase, and whoever was climbing it.

"It's Mrs. Rivkin," whispered Dovid. "We're finished."

"It's not," said Avrumi, who was closest to the top step. "Look at the shoes."

A pair of work boots, dusty and gray, came into view. Then the legs of blue work pants, then a toolbox, and then the friendly face of Mr. Gomez, the custodian.

He stopped when he saw them. He raised one eyebrow. Then he raised the other one.

"Six boys," he said, "on the third floor, during reading time. This must be a very important reason."

Nobody could think of a single word to say. Finally Nosson stepped forward.

"Mr. Gomez," he said politely, "you told us Rabbi Feldman asked you to close our window. Where were you when he asked you?"

Mr. Gomez looked at Nosson for a long moment. Then he set down his toolbox and scratched his chin.
===
"Right here," he said. "I was fixing the radiator in this hall. The rebbe came up the stairs in a big hurry. He was holding something in both hands, very carefully, like it was a baby bird. He said, 'Mr. Gomez, please close my classroom window, it's blowing everything around,' and then he knocked on the Rav's door and went in."

"Holding something," repeated Yehuda. "What did it look like?"

"Couldn't see. He had it wrapped up in his handkerchief." Mr. Gomez picked up his toolbox. "Now, I'm going to finish this radiator. And you six are going to be back downstairs before I finish. That will take about one minute."

He winked. The boys did not need to be told twice.

They were back in their seats, books open and faces innocent, when Mrs. Rivkin looked in a moment later. She nodded, satisfied, and left again.

As soon as she was gone, the whispering started.

"He has it," whispered Dovid. "The rebbe has the esrog. Mr. Gomez saw him carrying it."

"Wrapped in a handkerchief," said Avrumi, and he drew a tiny handkerchief on his map, next to the arrow that pointed up the stairs.

"So nobody stole anything," said Binyamin. "The case is solved."

But Yehuda was frowning at his notebook. Something still seemed peculiar.
===
"Then why did he say that?" he asked. "'The esrog is ruined, and the boys must never find out what I did.' What did he do? Why is he hiding it from us?"

The others had no answer.

Tzvi Hirsch had been quiet for a long time, which was not like him. Now he turned around in his chair and looked at Nosson.

"Nosson," he said. His ears were red. "I said you took it. In front of everybody. I was wrong."

Nosson shrugged one shoulder. "You didn't know."

"I didn't know," said Tzvi Hirsch, "and I said it anyway. That's worse." He took a deep breath. "I'm sorry."

For a second Nosson didn't answer. Then he smiled, a real smile this time, all the way up to his eyes. "In Montreal," he said, "my rebbe used to say: a good detective looks at all the clues before he decides. A bad detective decides first and looks for clues later."

"Then I'm a very bad detective," said Tzvi Hirsch, and everyone laughed, even him.

Just then, the classroom door opened.

Rabbi Feldman walked in. He had his hat on. His face looked tired and serious. In his hands, wrapped in a white handkerchief, he carried something small and round.

He walked to his desk and put it down next to the empty silver box. He did not unwrap it.
===
"Boys," he said quietly, "I need to tell you something. And I am not proud of it."

Twenty-two boys held their breath.
--- words
murmuring: talking softly so the words are hard to hear
innocent: looking like you did nothing wrong
satisfied: happy that things are the way they should be
peculiar: strange; not the way it should be
handkerchief: a small square of cloth you keep in your pocket
