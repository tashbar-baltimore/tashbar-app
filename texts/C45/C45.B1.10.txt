On Monday afternoon, the sky over Baltimore was a strange, bright gray, and the trees were already swaying.

The six boys walked together to Rabbi Feldman's house. Dovid walked a little behind, carrying a shoebox with great care, as if it held eggs.

"What's in the box?" asked Tzvi Hirsch for the fourth time.

"You'll see," said Dovid for the fourth time.

Rabbi Feldman's sukkah stood in his backyard, made of white boards with green branches on top. Paper chains hung from the s'chach, and pictures his children had drawn were taped to every wall. On a little table in the corner, on a folded white cloth, sat the silver box.

"Welcome," said Rabbi Feldman. "Chag sameach!"

Before anyone sat down, Dovid opened his shoebox. Inside was a small wooden board with a toy fan on a stick, a wire, a magnet, a battery and a little round buzzer.

"It's a wind alarm," he explained, a bit out of breath. "When the wind gets strong, the fan spins a little metal arm until it touches the wire, and the buzzer goes off. Then you know to bring the decorations and cushions inside before they blow away." He looked up at the rebbe. "The radio said a big wind is coming this afternoon. I built it last night."
===
Rabbi Feldman looked at the little machine for a long moment. "Dovid," he said, "may I put it on the roof of my sukkah?"

Dovid turned the color of a ripe pomegranate. "Yes, Rebbe."

They set the alarm on a corner board where the wind could reach it. Then they sat down around the table. Rabbi Feldman opened the silver box, folded back the white cloth, and lifted out the esrog.

It was just as beautiful as the first time they had seen it. Bright yellow, bumpy in a neat and even way, with its little stem standing straight.

One by one, each boy held the lulav and the esrog, said the bracha slowly and carefully, and shook them gently in every direction. When it was Nosson's turn, Tzvi Hirsch whispered, "Don't forget Reb Elazar's trees," and Nosson nodded and closed his eyes for a moment before he began.

Yehuda went last. As he held the esrog, he thought about everything that had happened since that Monday a week ago. He thought about the empty box, and how sure everyone had been that someone had stolen it. He thought about how wrong that turned out to be.

Just as he finished, a sharp BZZZZT came from the roof.
===
Everybody jumped. Dovid's alarm was buzzing as loudly as a fire drill. Over their heads, the green branches began to toss, and one of the paper chains snapped loose and flew against the wall.

"The wind!" shouted Binyamin. "Quick, the decorations!"

The boys sprang into action. Avrumi and Binyamin unhooked the paper chains. Tzvi Hirsch grabbed the cushions. Yehuda and Nosson carefully pulled the children's drawings off the walls before the wind could tear them. Rabbi Feldman closed the silver box and held it tight against his coat. And Dovid climbed onto a chair and rescued his alarm just as a gust nearly lifted it off the roof.

In less than two minutes, everything was safe inside the house, piled on the kitchen table. Outside, the wind howled through the sukkah, but nothing was left to blow away.

Rabbi Feldman looked at the pile, and then at the six red-cheeked boys, and he began to laugh.

"In all my years," he said, "I have never had a sukkah saved by a buzzer."

"It was a team," said Dovid, but he looked very proud.

Later, when the wind had calmed down and the boys were walking home along Glen Avenue, Yehuda took out his blue notebook. He turned to a clean page at the very back, and he wrote down his conclusion, slowly, in his best handwriting:
===
A good detective looks at all the clues before he decides. Dan l'kaf zechus: judge people favorably.

"What are you writing?" asked Nosson.

Yehuda showed him. Nosson read it and smiled. "That's what my rebbe in Montreal used to say."

"I know," said Yehuda. "You told me. That's why I wrote it down."

Behind them, Tzvi Hirsch was arguing with Dovid about whether a wind alarm could be made big enough to warn the whole city. Avrumi was already drawing it. Binyamin said that if Dovid built it, he would help, as long as it did not explode.

And Yehuda closed his notebook, put it in his back pocket, and hurried to catch up with his friends.

THE END
