The bugle at Camp Pine Lake blew at seven o'clock, the way it did every morning. But on Monday, Tzvi Hirsch Gold was already awake. He was already dressed. He had already laced his sneakers twice.

"Get up, Avrumi," he said, shaking the top bunk. "Today is the hike!"

Avrumi Katz opened one eye behind his round glasses. "The hike is after breakfast," he said. "Breakfast is after davening. Davening is in twenty minutes."

"So get up in nineteen," said Tzvi Hirsch, and he ran out the door.

Avrumi sighed and reached under his pillow for his notebook. Tzvi Hirsch ran everywhere. He ran to the lake, he ran to the dining room, and once he had run straight into the flagpole. Avrumi did not run. Avrumi looked.

Through the screen window, he looked at the sky. It was pale blue, with a few small puffy clouds over the lake, like cotton balls. He drew three little clouds in the corner of the page and wrote: Monday, 7:05. Small clouds.
===
After breakfast, the ten boys of Bunk Daled lined up at the edge of the woods with their backpacks on. Their counselor, Reb Akiva, walked down the line and checked every pack. He was nineteen, tall and calm, and he always wore a silver whistle on a cord around his neck.

"Water bottle?" he asked each boy. "Poncho? Whistle?"

Every camper at Pine Lake had a whistle. The camp gave one to each boy on the first day, on a bright yellow cord.

"Today we hike Ridge Trail to Eagle Rock," said Reb Akiva. "It is the best view at camp. You can see three lakes and a hundred hills. But first, three rules." He held up one finger. "One. Always stay where you can see the boy in front of you."

He held up a second finger. "Two. Follow the blue marks. Every trail here has its own color, painted on the trees. Ridge Trail is blue. As long as you can see a blue mark, you are not lost."

He held up a third finger. "Three. If you are ever in trouble, blow your whistle three times. Three blasts means: I need help. Everybody, say it back."

"Three blasts means I need help!" shouted ten boys, and a crow flew out of the trees in surprise.
===
The trail started flat and easy, under tall pine trees that smelled like Sukkos. Then it began to climb.

Tzvi Hirsch was right behind Reb Akiva, and then he was in front of him, and then he was so far in front that Reb Akiva had to call, "Tzvi Hirsch! Rule number one!"

Tzvi Hirsch waited on a rock, bouncing on his toes. "I'm counting the blue marks," he called back. "I'm up to eleven!"

At the back of the line, Avrumi was counting too, but he was counting other things. He counted the bridges: one, over a small stream. He counted the places where the trail split: two, and at each one a blue mark on a tree showed the way. He drew the trail as a wiggly line in his notebook, with a little star at every split. Avrumi drew maps of everything: the camp, the shul, his whole street at home. His Abba was a sofer, a scribe who wrote sifrei Torah, and Abba always said that careful hands start with careful eyes.

Nosson Abergel walked next to him. Nosson was new to Baltimore this year, and new to camp. He spoke softly, with a little French in his voice. "Why do you draw every turn?" he asked.

"So I know how to get back," said Avrumi.

"But Reb Akiva knows how to get back."

Avrumi shrugged. "It's good to know anyway."
===
By the time they reached the creek, it was lunchtime and very hot. The air felt thick and sticky, like the inside of a bakery. The boys sat on the big flat rocks by the water and ate their sandwiches. Dovid Lerner tried to build a dam with stones. Tzvi Hirsch announced that he had counted twenty blue marks, and that this was a camp record.

"There is no camp record for blue marks," said Dovid.

"There is now," said Tzvi Hirsch.

Avrumi was not listening. He was looking at the sky again.

In the morning, the clouds had been small and round. Now they had grown. One big cloud in the west had been puffing up all through lunch, higher and higher, like dough rising in a warm kitchen. Its bottom was dark gray. Its top was bright white, and it was so tall that Avrumi had to tip his head back to see the top of it.

He took out his notebook and looked at his drawing from the morning. Three little clouds. Then he looked up again.

The top of the giant cloud was no longer round. It had spread out flat and wide, like a table. Like the iron anvil in the picture of a blacksmith in his reading book.

Avrumi remembered something his Abba had told him on their hike last fall. A cloud with a flat top means trouble is coming.
===
"Reb Akiva," said Avrumi quietly.

Reb Akiva was helping Dovid fish his sandwich bag out of the creek. "One second, Avrumi."

"Reb Akiva." This time Avrumi stood up and pointed. "Look at that cloud."

Reb Akiva turned around. All the boys turned around. For a moment, nobody said anything. Far away, under the dark bottom of the cloud, there was a gray curtain hanging down to the hills. It was rain, and it was moving.

Then a breeze came across the creek. It was a cool breeze, much cooler than the air had been all day, and it made the leaves flip over and show their pale backs.

Reb Akiva's face changed. He stopped smiling. He put his whistle in his mouth and blew one short blast, the signal for "line up".

"Packs on," he said. "We are not going to Eagle Rock today."

"But we're almost there!" said Tzvi Hirsch.

Reb Akiva looked at the anvil cloud, and then at his ten boys.

"We turn back," he said. "Now."
--- words
bugle: a small horn that is blown to wake up a camp or call people together
counselor: a young man at camp who takes care of a group of boys
creek: a small, narrow stream of water
announced: said something out loud so everyone would hear
anvil: a heavy iron block that a blacksmith hammers hot metal on
curtain: a hanging cloth, or something that hangs down like one
signal: a sound or sign that tells people what to do
