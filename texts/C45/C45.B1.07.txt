Six boys leaned so close to the drawing that their heads almost bumped.

There it was. Near the top of Avrumi's esrog, right beside the little stem, was one dot that looked different from all the others. The other dots were tiny open circles, the bumps of the esrog skin. This one was filled in, dark and solid, like the period at the end of a sentence.

"Avrumi," said Yehuda slowly, "why did you color that one in?"

Avrumi pushed his glasses up his nose and stared at his own drawing as if someone else had made it. "Because I saw it," he said. "When I was drawing, I saw a tiny dark speck right there. I thought it was a special bump, so I drew it dark. I didn't think it mattered."

"You drew it in the morning," said Binyamin. "Before recess."

"Before the rebbe touched it," said Dovid.

Everyone was quiet as the idea sank in.

"Then the spot didn't come from his thumb," said Yehuda. His voice was getting faster. "It was already there! He thinks he made it, but he didn't. He's been feeling bad all day for nothing!"

"Unless," said Tzvi Hirsch, "Avrumi's dot is just a mistake. Maybe his pencil slipped."
===
Avrumi looked hurt for a second. Then he took out his sketchbook and flipped back to the morning page. "Look at the other drawings I made today. The clock, the bus, the window. Do you see any dots where they don't belong?"

They looked. Avrumi's drawings were neat and careful. There was not a single stray mark on any of them.

"Okay," said Tzvi Hirsch. "I take it back. Your pencil doesn't slip."

Mr. Abergel had been listening with his arms folded and a small smile on his face. Now he sat down at the table with them.

"I am not a Rav," he said, "so I cannot tell you if an esrog is kosher. Only the Rav can decide that. But I can tell you something my father taught me when I was your age. When you see a dark speck on an esrog, the first question is not 'Is it a problem?' The first question is, 'Is it really part of the esrog?'"

"What else could it be?" asked Nosson.
===
"Many things," said his father. "A grain of dust. A bit of dirt from the box. Sometimes a little piece of the wrapping." He held up his hand and rubbed two fingers together. "A real mark is in the skin. It doesn't move. Something that is only sitting on top can look exactly the same, until you look from the side, in good daylight."

Yehuda's hand flew to his notebook. He flipped back through the pages, past the coat and the hat and the leaves on the stairs, until he found the line he was looking for.

Flax on Nosson's sleeve. Soft, white. It sticks to everything.

He looked up. Nosson was looking at the same page, and his eyes were wide.

"The flax," Nosson whispered. "From the box I carried. My father always says it sticks to everything. If a tiny bit stuck to the esrog..."

"But flax is white," said Dovid. "The spot is dark."

"A tiny wisp, in the shadow of the stem?" said Mr. Abergel. "It can look dark. I have seen it before." He raised one finger. "But I have not seen THIS esrog. I am only telling you what to ask. The answer belongs to the Rav."

The boys looked at one another.

"We have to tell Rabbi Feldman," said Binyamin.
===
"It's nine o'clock at night," said Yehuda's mother on the phone, when Yehuda called to ask if he could stay longer. "You can tell him first thing in the morning."

So they made a plan. They would all come to school early, before davening, and go straight to Rabbi Feldman. Yehuda would bring the yellow note. Avrumi would bring his drawing. Nosson would bring the one thing that could explain everything: a tiny pinch of flax from the box, in a little plastic bag, so the Rav could compare.

That night, Yehuda could hardly fall asleep. He kept seeing the dark dot every time he closed his eyes.

The next morning was cold and bright. The six boys met at the school gate at seven-thirty, red-cheeked and out of breath. The building was almost empty. They climbed the stairs to the second floor. Their classroom was locked.

"He's not here yet," said Dovid.

But Yehuda was looking up the stairs to the third floor. The door at the top was open, and sunlight was pouring out of it into the hall.

They climbed up quietly. Through the open door, they could see Rav Mizrachi standing at his window, holding something small and yellow up to the light. Rabbi Feldman stood beside him with his hat in his hands.

The Rav turned the esrog slowly. He leaned closer.
===
And then, for a long, terrible moment, he frowned.
--- words
solid: filled in all the way, not hollow
stray: in a place where it does not belong
compare: look at two things to see how they are alike or different
wisp: a tiny thin piece of something soft
sank: slowly went deep inside
