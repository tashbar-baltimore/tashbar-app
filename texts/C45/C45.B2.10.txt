The campfire crackled. Sparks floated up into the dark like tiny orange stars. More than two hundred boys sat on the log benches in a big circle, and every one of them was looking at Tzvi Hirsch Gold.

Tzvi Hirsch stood up. His knees felt wobbly. He had stood up in front of people many times before: to tell jokes, to lead a cheer, to announce a camp record that did not exist. But this was different.

He looked at the newspaper in his hand. The Pine Lake Ripple. Two Campers Found! He read one line out loud. "They wandered for miles through the woods all night, looking for camp."

Then he folded the paper.

"That's not what happened," he said.

The circle went quiet. Shmuly Rosen, who had written the article, turned a little pink.

"It's not Shmuly's fault," said Tzvi Hirsch quickly. "He wasn't there. He heard it from other people at breakfast, and some of them weren't there either. So it got mixed up. That happens. But I was there. And I want to tell it right."
===
So he told it.

He told how the wind took his cap, the red one with the gold G, and how he ran after it without thinking, without telling anyone, without even looking back. He did not make it sound better than it was.

"That was my mistake," he said. "The whole thing started with me running. If I had stopped and told Reb Akiva, none of it would have happened."

He told how Avrumi came down the slope after him. How the rain came, and the trail disappeared. How Avrumi remembered his Abba's lightning rules, and they crouched in the hollow and counted the seconds.

"And then," said Tzvi Hirsch, "I wanted to walk down the mountain to find camp. I was sure I was right. And Avrumi said one word to me. He said 'Stop.'"

Two hundred boys were so quiet that you could hear the fire popping.

"We didn't wander for miles," said Tzvi Hirsch. "We didn't wander at all. We stayed in one spot, the whole night, right near where we left the trail. That's why they found us. If we had kept walking, like the newspaper says, we'd have gotten more lost. Maybe they'd still be looking."
===
Then Tzvi Hirsch sat down, and Reb Akiva looked at Avrumi.

Avrumi did not like talking in front of people. He liked drawing maps of people. But he stood up anyway, very slowly, and pushed his glasses up his nose.

"I want to say one thing," said Avrumi, so softly that the boys in the back leaned forward. "Tzvi Hirsch said it was all his fault. But he left something out."

He looked at his friend.

"He told me he was scared. In the dark, under the rock. He said it first. And after that, I wasn't so scared anymore, because I knew I wasn't the only one." Avrumi took a breath. "And then two times he wanted to run toward the lights, and two times he stopped himself. Nobody told him to. He just stopped. And the orange poncho on the rock, that the drone saw? That was his idea. Not mine."

Avrumi sat down fast, and his ears were bright red.

For a moment, nobody said anything. Then Dovid Lerner started clapping, and then Nosson, and Yehuda, and then the whole circle was clapping and stomping their feet on the ground, and Tzvi Hirsch put his face in his hands and laughed.
===
When the clapping stopped, Ranger Morales stood up. He had come to the campfire with Scout, who was lying by his feet, getting her ears scratched by six boys at once.

"I've been doing search and rescue for twenty-two years," he said. "Every summer, people get lost in these hills. Grown-ups, mostly. And most of them do the same thing. They panic, and they walk. And the more they walk, the harder they are to find." He looked around the circle. "These two boys did everything right after that first mistake. They stopped. They stayed together. They signaled with their whistles. They made themselves easy to see. If you ever get lost, anywhere, in the woods or at the mall, I want you to remember four words."

He held up four fingers, the way Reb Akiva had held up three on the first morning.

"Stop. Stay. Signal. Wait."

"Stop. Stay. Signal. Wait," said two hundred boys, and an owl in the trees hooted, as if it wanted to say it too.

Then Ranger Morales reached into his jacket. "Oh, and one more thing," he said. "Scout found this on the west slope this morning, hanging on a thornbush at the bottom of the gully."

He held up a red cap with a gold G on the front.
===
Tzvi Hirsch did not wear the cap the rest of the summer. He hung it on a nail above his bunk, where he could see it every night before he said Shema. Sometimes boys asked him why he didn't wear it anymore. He said, "It reminds me to stop and think."

On Friday, a big box arrived at camp from Gold's Bakery in Baltimore. Inside were rugelach, hundreds of them: chocolate, cinnamon, and raspberry, still soft, with a note in Tzvi Hirsch's father's handwriting. "For the oneg Shabbos. With thanks to everyone who looked for our boy, and everyone who said Tehillim for him." That Friday night, the whole camp sang zemiros late into the night, and nobody had ever tasted better rugelach.

On the last day of camp, Avrumi drew one more map. It was the best map he had ever made. It showed all of Ridge Trail, with every blue mark, every split, every bridge and the creek. It showed the rocky part, and the steep west slope, and the gully where the cap had stopped. And at the top of the slope, by a big rock with a ledge, he drew a tiny orange flag and a white arrow.

Next to them, in his neatest letters, he wrote five words.

Where we stayed put. Together.

THE END
--- words
crackled: made small, sharp popping sounds
wobbly: shaky, not steady
panic: a sudden, wild fear that stops you from thinking clearly
rescue: saving someone from danger
thornbush: a bush with sharp, pointy thorns on its branches
zemiros: songs sung at the Shabbos table
