"Uncle Shimon," said Tzvi Hirsch. His voice came out strange and thin. "The light."

Reb Shimon had his back to the window, stacking paper plates. He turned around. He looked at the window and waited. Six seconds passed. Ten. Twenty.

Only darkness, and rain streaking the glass.

Reb Shimon set down the plates very gently, as if they were made of glass. He walked to the window and pressed his face close to it, looking up toward the lantern room above them. The boys crowded behind him. Above the gallery, where the white beam had swept around and around since sundown, there was nothing. The whole lighthouse was dark except for their two little Shabbos candles, flickering on the table.

"The beacon has failed," said Reb Shimon quietly. "It happens sometimes in a bad storm. Water gets into a connection, or the wind shakes something loose."

"So what happens now?" asked Dovid.

Reb Shimon did not answer right away. He was listening. The radio on the shelf, which had been murmuring softly all evening, had begun to crackle with voices.
===
"All stations, all stations," said a calm voice on the radio. "This is the Coast Guard. We have a report that the light at Kettle Shoal is not working. All boats in the area, use caution."

Then a different voice broke in. It was rough and fast and full of static, and it was hard to understand every word.

"Coast Guard, Coast Guard, this is the fishing boat Miss Ellie. Our engine is dead. We are drifting. Two people on board, me and my son. We are somewhere south of Kettle Shoal. I can't see the light. I can't see anything. Repeat, I can't see the light."

The Coast Guard voice answered at once, steady and clear, asking questions: How many people? Did they have life jackets on? Which way were they drifting? A rescue boat was getting ready, it said, but in this storm it would take time to reach them.

The boys stood frozen in the middle of the room. Somewhere out there in the black, howling water, a father and his son were on a boat with no engine, being pushed by the wind toward the shoal. And the light that was supposed to warn them was dark.

Binyamin was the first to speak. "If they can't see the light," he said slowly, "they don't know where the shoal is."
===
Reb Shimon turned to face the four boys. In the candlelight, his face looked older than before, but very calm.

"Listen to me, boys," he said. "Today is Shabbos. You know that on Shabbos, we do not turn lights on or off. We do not fix machines. We do not use the radio. Shabbos is holy, and we guard it."

The boys nodded.

"But there is something even greater than all of those rules," said Reb Shimon. "It is called pikuach nefesh. When a person's life may be in danger, we do what is needed to save him, even on Shabbos. The Torah gave us Shabbos so that we would live, not so that someone would be lost. To save a life is a mitzvah, and doing it quickly is part of the mitzvah."

He looked at the dark window. "Many years ago, when I first became the keeper here, I asked my Rav a question. What do I do if the light ever fails on Shabbos, in a storm, when a boat may be out there? And my Rav told me exactly what to do. He said: you do not wait, and you do not ask again. You call the Coast Guard, and if they need the light, you turn on the light."
===
Reb Shimon picked up the radio microphone.

"Coast Guard, Coast Guard, this is the keeper at Kettle Shoal Light. I am in the lighthouse with four boys. Our main beacon has failed. I have a backup lamp. Do you want it lit?"

The answer came back at once. "Kettle Shoal, yes. Light the backup lamp immediately. The Miss Ellie needs a mark to steer by. Thank you, keeper."

Reb Shimon set down the microphone. "Stay here," he said to the boys. "Stay together, and stay away from the windows. I will be back in a few minutes."

He took a big flashlight from a hook by the door and went up the narrow iron stairs to the lantern room. The boys heard his slow, heavy footsteps on the steps. Then a door opened above them, and the sound of the wind came roaring down the stairs, and then the door banged shut, and the roar was muffled again.

The four boys stood in the middle of the room, not moving. None of them touched anything. None of them spoke. They had never seen anyone do something like this on Shabbos before. It felt strange and serious, like watching a doctor in a hospital.

"Is he allowed?" whispered Dovid.

"He asked his Rav," whispered Nosson. "Years ago. For exactly this."
===
Then, above them, a light came on.

It was not the same as the beacon. It was a little dimmer, a little more yellow. But it was there, shining out of the lantern room into the storm. And then it began to flash. On. Six seconds of dark. On. Six seconds of dark.

"He set it to the same pattern," said Binyamin. "One flash every six seconds. So the captain will know it's Kettle Shoal."

Reb Shimon came slowly back down the stairs, soaked from his beard to his knees, breathing hard. He sat down heavily in a chair by the stove. Tzvi Hirsch brought him a towel.

"It is lit," said Reb Shimon. "Now we wait, and we watch, and we daven." He looked at the four boys. "You can help. Not with your hands. With your eyes. Go to the south window, carefully. Tell me anything you see out there. Anything at all."

The boys went to the south window and pressed their faces to the cold glass. It was hard to see anything. Rain. Spray. Blackness. Every six seconds, the backup lamp lit up the falling rain for a moment, like a flashbulb, and then everything was black again.

Then Nosson grabbed Tzvi Hirsch's sleeve.

"There," he whispered. "Low on the water. Do you see it?"

Far out in the darkness, very small, there was a tiny green light. It bobbed up and down with the waves. And it was moving slowly toward the place where the water turned white and shallow, toward the shoal.
--- words
murmuring: talking in a low, soft voice
static: crackling noise on a radio that makes words hard to hear
drifting: moving slowly where the wind and water push, without power
caution: great care, to stay safe
immediately: right away, with no waiting
muffled: made softer and harder to hear
