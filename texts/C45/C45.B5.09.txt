The man in the yellow jacket waited on the deck of the Coast Guard boat until three stars came out and Reb Shimon made Havdalah. Then he climbed the iron ladder, slowly, with the boy right behind him. He was a big man with a gray mustache and a sunburned face, and he held onto the rails as if his legs were still tired from the night. The boy was about thirteen, thin and freckled, with a bandage on one hand.

"I'm Ray Turner," the man said, taking off his cap. "Captain of the Miss Ellie, for what's left of her. This is my son, Cody." He looked around the little room, at the four boys, at the old man with the white beard, at the two burned-down candle cups still on the table. "The Coast Guard told me who was out here last night. I asked them to bring me by before they took us home. I wanted to shake some hands."

He shook Reb Shimon's hand for a long time. Then he shook each boy's hand, one by one. His hand was rough and huge, like a baseball glove.

"Sit, please, sit," said Reb Shimon. "Tell us what happened. We saw only a green light. We want to know the rest."

So Captain Turner sat down at the little table, with Cody beside him, and told it his way.
===
"We were out late," he said. "Too late. That's my fault. The forecast said the storm wasn't coming till Saturday night, and the fishing was good, and I got greedy. When the wind came up so fast in the afternoon, we headed for home. And about eight thirty, the engine coughed, and coughed again, and quit. Just quit. I spent twenty minutes down in the engine with a flashlight in my teeth, and I couldn't get her started.

"Now, a boat with no engine in a storm is like a leaf in a river. The wind takes you wherever it wants. And I knew where it wanted to take us. North and west. Onto Kettle Shoal.

"Then I came up on deck and looked for the light. I've been looking at that light my whole life. Every night I've been out on this bay, there it's been: one white flash, six seconds dark. I look for it the way you look for your own front door.

"And it wasn't there.

"I can't tell you what that felt like. I've been fishing this bay for thirty years, and all of a sudden I didn't know where I was. Everything was black. Black sky, black water, rain in my face. Cody was holding on to the rail, and I could see in his face that he was scared, and I was trying very hard not to let him see that I was scared too."
===
Cody spoke for the first time. His voice was quiet. "Dad made me put on my life jacket. And then he called the Coast Guard. I heard him say he couldn't see the light. He said it three times."

Captain Turner nodded. "I said it three times," he admitted. "And then I waited. I didn't know if anyone could do anything. I figured the light was out for the night, and we'd just have to hope.

"And then, I don't know how long it was, maybe twenty minutes, maybe half an hour, I looked up, and there it was. One white flash. I counted. Six seconds. Another flash. Kettle Shoal. I can't describe it. It was like somebody turned on a light in a dark house and suddenly you can see the furniture again.

"Now I knew exactly where I was. And I knew we were much too close. Then the Coast Guard told me to try the anchor. I went forward and threw it out, and I prayed it would catch in the mud. And it caught."

He looked at Binyamin. "The Coast Guard told me afterward that somebody out here figured out which way we were drifting just from our green light. That they told the Coast Guard right where to look."

Binyamin turned red to the tips of his ears. "It was just logic," he mumbled. "Like chess."

"Well, son," said Captain Turner, "that's the best chess game anybody ever played for me."
===
"What happened when the big wave came?" asked Dovid. "We saw the searchlight find you. And then the wave came, and your light went out, and we thought..."

Captain Turner rubbed his mustache. "That was the worst moment," he said. "The rescue boat was right there, maybe fifty feet away. And this wave came up out of nowhere, bigger than all the others, and knocked the Miss Ellie right over on her side. I went sliding across the deck. Cody went into the rail and cut his hand. Our green light smashed. For a second, I thought we were going all the way over.

"But the anchor held. It held us. The boat came back up, slow, like an old man getting out of a chair. And then the Coast Guard was right alongside, and they had us both aboard in about two minutes. Cody first." He put his big hand on his son's shoulder. "Cody first."

Nobody said anything for a while. Then Reb Shimon said, quietly, "Baruch Hashem."

Captain Turner looked at him. "I'm not a Jewish man," he said. "But I know what that means. Thank G-d. And I say it too."
===
Then Captain Turner stood up to go. The Coast Guard boat was waiting. At the door, he turned around.

"You know," he said, "I keep thinking about how close it was. If your light hadn't come back on at 9:30, we'd have been on that shoal before I ever got the anchor down. Maybe right into those iron legs of yours. 9:30. I looked at my watch the second I saw it. I'll never forget that time as long as I live."

He waved and went down the ladder with Cody, and the Coast Guard boat pulled away toward Baltimore, its engines rumbling, its lights shining on the dark water.

The boys stood at the window and watched it go.

Then Tzvi Hirsch frowned. Something was bothering him. Reb Shimon had already sat down at the table with his logbook, writing down the whole night while it was still fresh in his mind. Tzvi Hirsch read over his shoulder and ran his finger down the page.

9:15 p.m. Backup lamp lit.

"Uncle Shimon," he said slowly. "The captain said the light came back on at 9:30. But your log says 9:15."

He looked up.

"They can't both be right. Can they?"
--- words
mustache: hair that grows on a man's upper lip
freckled: covered with small brown spots on the skin
greedy: wanting more than you need
admitted: said something true, even though it was hard to say
logic: careful thinking, one step at a time, to find what must be true
alongside: right next to, side by side
