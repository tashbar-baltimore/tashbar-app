Getting ready for Shabbos at home was something Tzvi Hirsch had done his whole life without thinking about it. On Fridays, the bakery was so busy that he helped pack challah orders until noon. Then he came home, and the house smelled like soup, and everybody showered and dressed, and the table was set with the white tablecloth and the silver candlesticks that had come from his great-grandmother.

Getting ready for Shabbos on a lighthouse in a storm was different. You had to think about everything.

"First," said Reb Shimon, sitting at the little table with a pencil and the back of an old envelope, "what do we have?"

They unpacked the box. Two challos. One bottle of grape juice. One pan of roast chicken. One chocolate babka. Two candles. A box of matches. Paper plates and plastic forks.

Then they looked in the lighthouse kitchen. Reb Shimon kept supplies there for long work days: crackers, a jar of peanut butter, cans of tuna, a bag of apples, tea bags and a big jug of drinking water. There was a little gas stove, and a cooler with milk and a block of cheese.

"We will not go hungry," said Binyamin, sounding surprised.

"No," said Reb Shimon. "Thanks to a wise mother, we will eat like kings."
===
There was a lot to do before sundown, and Reb Shimon gave everybody a job.

Dovid checked every window and latched every shutter, so nothing could bang or break in the wind. Binyamin filled two pots with drinking water and set them by the stove, and he put the chicken and the challos where they would stay dry. Nosson found an old white tablecloth folded in a drawer, a little yellow with age, and spread it on the table. Tzvi Hirsch swept the floor, then swept it again, because his mother always said a clean floor makes a house feel like Shabbos.

They washed their faces and hands at the little sink and changed into the dry clothes that Reb Shimon kept in a trunk for emergencies. The shirts were much too big. Dovid's sleeves hung down past his fingers like a scarecrow's, and the other boys laughed until he rolled them up.

Meanwhile, Reb Shimon set up the radio on a shelf where he could hear it, and turned the volume low. The Coast Guard would call at set times through the night. On Shabbos he would not turn the radio on or off, he explained, but it would stay on by itself, in case of an emergency.
===
By five o'clock, the wind was howling around the cottage, and the whole house hummed with it, like a giant guitar string.

The boys stood at the south window and watched. The bay had turned the color of slate. Big waves marched in long rows toward the lighthouse, and when each one reached the iron legs, it broke into a burst of white spray that flew up almost as high as the gallery.

"The water is higher than this morning," said Nosson. He pointed to one of the iron legs. "This morning I could see a ring of green weed on that leg, above the water. Now the weed is under."

"Very good," said Reb Shimon, coming to stand beside him. "You notice things. The water is higher for two reasons." He held up one finger. "First, the tide. Twice a day, the water in the bay rises and falls. That happens because the moon pulls on the oceans of the world, and the water bulges toward it. There is a bulge on the far side of the earth too, and the earth turns through both bulges every day. That is why the tide comes in twice. Right now the tide is coming in, so the water is rising."

He held up a second finger. "Second, the storm. Its strong wind pushes the water ahead of it, the way you push water when you blow across a bowl of soup. The wind is blowing from the south, up the bay, and it piles the water higher and higher at this end. That extra water is called a storm surge."

"So the tide and the surge add up," said Binyamin.

"They add up," said Reb Shimon. "And tonight, they will add up a lot."
===
Tzvi Hirsch looked down at the gray water swirling around the iron legs, far below. "Uncle Shimon," he said slowly, "how high can it get?"

Reb Shimon pointed to a wooden post on the gallery. Near its top, someone had carved a line, a date and two words.

August 1933. Water here.

The boys stared. The line was higher than the floor they were standing on.

"In the great storm of 1933," said Reb Shimon, "the water came up through the floor of this house. The keeper and his family climbed up into the lantern room and waited there all night, while the waves rushed through the kitchen below them. In the morning, the water went down. The house was still standing. Everyone was safe." He patted the old post. "These legs have stood through a hundred and forty years of storms. They will stand through one more."

Dovid let out a long breath. Nosson said nothing, but he kept looking at the line.

"Now," said Reb Shimon, in a different voice, a brighter one. "It is almost time. Who will set out the candles?"
===
They set the two little tin candle cups on a plate in the middle of the table, away from the windows, where the flames would be safe from drafts. Reb Shimon explained that when a man is away from home for Shabbos, he lights Shabbos candles where he is staying. Tonight, he would light for all of them.

Eighteen minutes before sunset, the four boys stood around the table in their giant borrowed shirts. Outside, the sky was almost dark, and the rain had started again, rattling against the shutters. The lighthouse beacon had already turned itself on, high above them, and every six seconds its white beam swept past the window and lit up the rain like falling silver.

Reb Shimon struck a match. He lit one candle, then the other. He waved his hands three times over the flames, covered his eyes, and said the bracha quietly. For a moment the little room was perfectly still, full of warm yellow light.

Tzvi Hirsch thought of his mother at home, lighting her candles at the same moment, with the twins on either side of her. He thought she was probably crying a little. He thought she was probably smiling, too.

Reb Shimon took his hands away from his eyes. "Gut Shabbos, boys," he said softly.

"Gut Shabbos," said the boys.

At that exact moment, the match in the dish gave a last little curl of smoke, and something enormous hit the lighthouse from underneath. BOOM. The whole house shook. The candle flames jumped. A wave had slammed into the iron legs below them, bigger than any wave all day, so big that they felt it in their feet.
--- words
latched: closed with a small lock or hook
emergency: a sudden, dangerous time when help is needed fast
slate: a dark blue-gray stone
tide: the rising and falling of the sea, twice a day
surge: a sudden, strong rush upward or forward
beacon: a strong light that guides or warns people
enormous: very, very big
