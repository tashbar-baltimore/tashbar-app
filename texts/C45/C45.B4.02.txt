Avrumi Katz came into class ten minutes late on Monday, with his round glasses pushed up on his red hair and dust on his sleeves.

"Sorry, Rebbe," he said. "I was helping my Abba carry a table into the beis medrash."

Rabbi Feldman nodded. Everyone knew that Avrumi's Abba was a sofer. For the next few weeks, he was going to sit in the corner of the yeshiva's beis medrash and repair the oldest sefer Torah the yeshiva had.

"How old is it?" Tzvi Hirsch Gold whispered at recess.

"Nobody knows," said Avrumi. "It was here before the building. My Abba says the letters are beautiful, but the wooden handles are black at the bottom, like burnt toast." He shrugged. "Nobody knows why."

But Yehuda was not really listening. He had been waiting all morning, and now he pulled out his blue notebook and opened it on the bench.

"Look," he said.

The crew leaned in: Avrumi, Tzvi Hirsch, Dovid Lerner, Binyamin Yusupov, and Nosson Abergel, who had joined them last year and now stood in his usual spot, a little to the side, where he could see everyone's faces.

Yehuda told them everything. The attic. The box with the rusty lock. The map in brown ink. The way Zeidy's singing had stopped. The young man in the cap.

When he finished, nobody said anything for a moment.

Then Tzvi Hirsch let out a long whistle. "A treasure map," he said. "A real one."
===
"We don't know it's a treasure map," said Yehuda. "We only know it's a map."

"With a star on it," said Tzvi Hirsch. "Stars on maps mean treasure. Everybody knows that."

Binyamin was looking at the three letters Yehuda had copied. "Samech, pei, reish," he said slowly. "Sefer. A book."

"Maybe," said Yehuda. He had thought the same thing.

"Or," said Tzvi Hirsch, with a grin, "it says sapar. My cousin in Yerushalayim told me that's how you say barber. Maybe your great-great-Zeidy buried a treasure under a barber chair."

Everybody laughed, even Yehuda. But he wrote it down anyway, because he wrote everything down.

Nosson had not laughed. He was looking at the letters too. "The same three letters," he said quietly, "can be read more than one way. That is what my rebbe in Montreal used to say. You have to see where they are standing."

"Where they're standing is next to a star," said Dovid. "By the harbor."

"Then we need to see the real map," said Avrumi. His fingers were already moving, as if they were holding a pencil. "A copy isn't enough. I need to see the lines."

That afternoon, Yehuda called Zeidy from the office phone. He was not sure what Zeidy would say.

Zeidy was quiet for a second. Then he said, "Bring them. Six heads are better than one."
===
Zeidy's dining room table had never been so crowded. The map lay in the middle, under the bright lamp, flat and brown and very old. Savta had put out a plate of leftover sfenj, and Tzvi Hirsch was already on his second one.

Dovid had come straight from home with his backpack, and he had nearly tripped over the umbrella stand by the front door. In it stood Zeidy's big black umbrella with its carved wooden handle in the shape of a duck's head.

"Quack," Tzvi Hirsch had said to it, and Malka had laughed for five minutes.

Now everyone was quiet. Avrumi bent so close to the map that his glasses almost touched it.

"German Street," he read. "Where is German Street?"

"It is still there," said Zeidy. "It is called Redwood Street now. They changed the name during the First World War. After the fire, many downtown streets were made wider."

Avrumi pointed to the shaded part. "These lines aren't just decoration. They cover all of downtown. Right up to here." His finger stopped at the wavy line. "Is this a river?"

"A stream," said Zeidy. "The Jones Falls. It runs through the city into the harbor."

"Then the shading is the fire," said Avrumi. "He shaded everything that burned. And it stops at the stream."

Zeidy looked at him with surprise. "That is exactly right," he said softly. "The fire stopped at the Jones Falls. My Zeidy told me that."
===
Nosson had been looking at the map from the other side of the table. "The star is on the other side of the stream," he said. "The side that did not burn."

"The safe side," said Binyamin.

"And the dots start at the cap," said Yehuda. "Zeidy, you said your Zeidy made caps. So the little square with the cap on top must be where he worked."

Zeidy nodded slowly. "On the top floor of that building," he said, "there was a small room where the cap-makers davened. A minyan, every morning, before work. My Zeidy told me about it when I was a boy."

Nosson picked up the photograph and held it under the lamp. "Look at the wall," he said. "The broken letters at the top."

They all crowded around. Across the top of the burned wall, in old painted letters, half of a sign was left: ...LANDER & SO...

"Somebody and Sons," said Dovid. "The name of the company."

Yehuda wrote it down. Then he turned to Zeidy. "Zeidy, what did he tell you? About the thing he saved? Exactly the words. Please."

Zeidy closed his eyes. "He said two things. He said: Not one letter touched the fire." He opened his eyes again. "And he said: Every Shabbos, it is read."

Six boys looked at one another.

"Letters," said Binyamin. "Read every Shabbos."

"It's a sefer," said Tzvi Hirsch. "Just like the map says. Sefer!"
===
"Maybe," said Yehuda. "But how do we know?"

"Your Zeidy said it," said Tzvi Hirsch.

"Zeidy said what his Zeidy said, sixty years ago," said Yehuda. "That's a clue. It isn't proof." He looked at the map. "We need more."

Zeidy smiled at that, a real smile this time. "My Zeidy also learned a perek of Mishnayos every night of his life," he said. "He would have liked you."

Dovid had been very quiet, which was unusual. Now he unzipped his backpack and took out a flat white panel with a cord. It was about the size of a sefer, and it glowed when he plugged it in.

"I built it out of an old computer screen," he said. "It's a light box. Avrumi uses it for tracing. When you put paper on top, the light shines through." He looked at Zeidy. "Old paper sometimes has things on the back that you can't see from the front. May I?"

Zeidy hesitated. Then he nodded.

Very carefully, Dovid slid the light box under the map.

The old paper lit up like a window. The streets, the stream and the star all glowed. And there, through the paper, faint and backward, they could see writing on the other side. It was not brown ink. It was pencil, light and gray, as if someone had wanted it to be almost invisible.

Avrumi read the backward letters out loud, one word at a time.

"Messenger," he said. "February 10. Page 6."
--- words
repair: to fix something that is broken or old
decoration: something added to make a thing look nice
hesitated: stopped for a second before deciding
shaded: colored in with lines or a darker color
invisible: not able to be seen at all
faint: very light and hard to see
