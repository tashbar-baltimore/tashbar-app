This newspaper story is made up, the way a Baltimore paper might have written it on the morning after the fire began. The Messenger is the same make-believe newspaper the boys find in The Map in Zeidy's Attic. Read it like a detective. A reporter writing in the middle of a disaster knows only part of the story.

THE MESSENGER. Baltimore, Monday, February 8, 1904.

GREAT FIRE SWEEPS THE HEART OF THE CITY. FLAMES STILL BURNING AS THIS PAPER GOES TO PRESS.

The worst fire in the history of Baltimore is burning as we write these words. Since Sunday morning, it has swept across the business district, and it has not stopped yet.
===
The fire began a little before eleven o'clock on Sunday morning, in the basement of the Hurst building, a large dry goods warehouse. Firemen rushed to the scene. Then there was a loud blast, and flames burst out of the windows. Within minutes, the fire had jumped to the buildings next door.

A strong wind from the west pushed the fire east and north. Sparks flew over whole streets and set new roofs burning far ahead of the main fire. By Sunday evening, the great banks, the tall office buildings and the warehouses along the busiest streets were burning. Many buildings that were said to be fireproof burned like the rest. Their stone walls stood, but everything inside them was gone.
===
Help has come from many cities. Fire engines arrived by train from Washington on Sunday afternoon, and more have come through the night from Philadelphia and Wilmington, and more are on the way from New York and other towns. Our reporter saw firemen from out of town standing beside their engines with their hoses in their hands, unable to use them. He was told the hoses did not fit our hydrants. We cannot yet say how many engines had this trouble.

Late on Sunday night, men began to blow up buildings with dynamite. They hoped to make an empty space that the fire could not cross. Our reporter heard the blasts from many blocks away. It is too soon to say if the plan has worked.
===
Many stories are passing through the crowds, and not all of them can be true. Some people say that whole families were trapped inside burning buildings. Others say that hundreds of lives have been lost. This newspaper has not been able to prove any of these stories. We print them only so that our readers know what is being said, and we pray they are false.

Thousands of people stand at the edges of the fire, watching. Shopkeepers have carried what they could into the streets. Wagons full of papers, cloth and furniture crowd every road leading away from downtown. Police are keeping people back from the flames.

At the hour this paper is printed, the fire is moving east, toward the Jones Falls. If it crosses the water, the homes of East Baltimore will be in its path. Every reader is asked to stay out of the fire district and to keep the roads open for the firemen.
===
A note for today's reader: The reporter wrote this before the fire was over. Later, people learned that the fire stopped at the Jones Falls on Monday, after about thirty hours. More than 1,500 buildings were gone. The terrible stories about hundreds of lives lost turned out to be false. As far as anyone knows, no one died in the fire itself. A newspaper written on the first day knows the least, because the story is still happening.
--- words
basement: the floor of a building that is under the ground
warehouse: a big building where goods are stored
fireproof: built so that it will not burn
district: one part of a city
reporter: a person who finds out the news and writes it for a newspaper
dynamite: a strong explosive used to blow things up
