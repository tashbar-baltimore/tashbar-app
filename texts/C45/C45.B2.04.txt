The bushes shook. A branch cracked. And out into the hollow stepped a deer.

She was a young doe, thin and brown, with big dark eyes and ears that turned this way and that like little satellite dishes. She took two steps toward the boys. Then she saw them.

For one long second, the deer and the two boys stared at each other. Nobody moved. Water dripped from the leaves.

Then the deer snorted, spun around on her thin legs, and bounded away into the trees with her white tail flashing, crash, crash, crash, until the sound was gone.

Tzvi Hirsch let out his breath in a whoosh. "A deer," he said. "It was a deer."

"She was more scared than we were," said Avrumi.

"I don't know about that," said Tzvi Hirsch. "I was pretty scared."

And then, for no good reason at all, they both started to laugh. They laughed so hard that Tzvi Hirsch fell off his backpack into the wet leaves, and that made them laugh even harder. It felt good, like opening a window in a stuffy room.

When they finally stopped, the hollow seemed less scary. It was just a place in the woods, with wet trees and a gray sky. And they were two boys in it, together.
===
"Okay," said Tzvi Hirsch, wiping his eyes. "We stay. You said we stay. So what do we do while we stay?"

Avrumi thought about it. Then he remembered a word that a ranger had once taught his class, on a trip to the nature center. The word was STOP, and each letter stood for something.

"S is for Stop," said Avrumi, counting on his fingers. "We did that. T is for Think. O is for Observe. That means look around carefully. And P is for Plan."

"Think," said Tzvi Hirsch. He scrunched up his face. "I'm thinking that I'm hungry."

"Then let's start with what we have," said Avrumi. "That's thinking, too."

They opened their backpacks and laid everything out on Tzvi Hirsch's poncho, like a store. Avrumi took out his notebook, which had stayed mostly dry inside his shirt, and made a list.

One water bottle, half full. One more water bottle, almost empty. A bag of trail mix, with more raisins than peanuts. Two squashed rugelach, wrapped in a napkin, from Gold's Bakery. One small flashlight. One compass. Two whistles. One orange rain poncho and one green rain poncho. One pencil. One notebook.

"That's it?" said Tzvi Hirsch.

"That's a lot," said Avrumi. "We have food, water, light, and a way to call for help. And we have each other."
===
Next came Observe. They looked around the hollow carefully, the way Avrumi's Abba looked at a letter in a sefer Torah, checking every corner.

The hollow was about the size of their bunk. Young trees grew all around it, close together. On the uphill side, a big gray rock stuck out of the ground, with a ledge on top like a roof. Under the ledge, the ground was almost dry. Beyond the rock, through a gap in the trees, Avrumi could see a bare, open patch on the hillside, where there were no trees at all, only flat gray stone.

"If someone is looking for us," said Avrumi slowly, "they need to be able to see us. But in here, with all these trees, nobody could see us even from twenty feet away."

Tzvi Hirsch looked at the two ponchos. One was green, the color of every leaf on the mountain. The other was bright orange, the color of a traffic cone.

"The orange one," he said. "We hang it up high, like a flag."

"Yes!" said Avrumi. It was a good idea, and it was Tzvi Hirsch's idea, and Avrumi could see that this made Tzvi Hirsch stand up a little straighter.

They tied the orange poncho to a branch at the edge of the hollow, as high as Tzvi Hirsch could reach standing on Avrumi's backpack. It hung there, glowing against the gray and green, flapping a little in the wind.
===
Then came Plan. Avrumi opened his notebook to a fresh page and drew a new map. The camp map had come apart in the rain, but he still remembered most of it, because he had copied it in his notebook that morning, one turn at a time.

He drew the trail, the creek, the two splits, the rocky part. He drew an X on the slope where the cap had blown away. Then he drew the hollow, and the rock with the ledge, and the open stone patch.

"Here's our plan," he said. "We stay right here, by the ledge. It's near the place where we left the trail, so it's near where they'll look. Every few minutes, we blow three blasts. We keep the poncho up. If it gets dark, we sleep under the ledge. And we don't go anywhere until someone finds us."

Tzvi Hirsch read the plan twice. Then he nodded. "Good plan," he said. "Now can we eat the rugelach?"

They ate one rugelach each, very slowly, a crumb at a time, to make it last. It was squashed and a little damp, and it was the best thing Tzvi Hirsch had ever tasted. He said it tasted like home. Avrumi said it tasted like chocolate. They were both right.
===
After they ate, Avrumi looked at the sky. The clouds were thinning in the west, and a little pale sunlight came through. "It's late afternoon," he said. "We haven't davened Mincha."

They looked at each other. At camp, Mincha was in the shul by the lake, with the whole camp. Here, there was nobody but the two of them.

"Which way is Yerushalayim?" asked Tzvi Hirsch.

Avrumi took out the compass. The red needle swung, wobbled, and settled, pointing north. "We're in Pennsylvania," he said. "Eretz Yisrael is far away across the ocean, to the east. So we face east." He turned the compass and pointed. "That way."

They stood side by side, facing east, toward the gray rock and the trees and the faraway ocean they could not see. They davened Mincha slowly, saying every word. Tzvi Hirsch had never davened so slowly in his life. When they came to the part where you ask for help, he closed his eyes tight and meant every single word.

When they finished, the sun was sinking toward the top of the ridge behind them. The shadows of the trees were long and blue.

Avrumi clicked on the flashlight to check it, just in case.

The beam came on, bright and yellow. Then it flickered. Then it faded to a dim orange glow, like a dying match.

Then it went out.
