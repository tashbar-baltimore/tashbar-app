"Only one account?" said Tzvi Hirsch into the phone. "But Uncle Shimon, they're all different!"

"I know," said Reb Shimon's soft, gravelly voice. "That is why I want you to choose. You have thought about it more than anyone. Come out to the lighthouse on Wednesday after school, all six of you, and tell me what you decide. I will bring the old logbook. I think it may help you."

On Wednesday, the Shoshana carried six boys out across a calm, blue bay. The water sparkled. A light breeze blew from the west. It was hard to believe it was the same bay that had roared and boomed under the iron legs only five days before.

The main beacon had been repaired on Monday. Reb Shimon said a Coast Guard technician had found a loose wire, soaked by the storm, and that it had taken her about ten minutes to fix. "Ten minutes," he said, shaking his head, "for what took us a whole night."

Inside the cottage, Reb Shimon set the old leather logbook on the table, the one marked 1903 to 1905. He opened it to the page from February 7, 1904.

Avrumi and Yehuda leaned over it at the same moment. Then they looked at each other.

"February 7, 1904," said Yehuda. "That's the day of the Great Fire."
===
Yehuda and Avrumi knew all about the Great Baltimore Fire of 1904. They had spent most of the winter studying it, ever since Yehuda found an old map in his Zeidy's attic. They knew it began on a cold Sunday morning in a building downtown. They knew the wind blew it from block to block for more than a day. They knew it burned more than fifteen hundred buildings, and that fire engines came from other cities to help, but many of their hoses did not fit Baltimore's fire hydrants. They knew it stopped at last at the Jones Falls, the stream that runs through the city.

They had read newspapers from that week, and letters, and the memories of people who were there.

And now here was one more account, written that very night, by the keeper of Kettle Shoal Light.

A great red glow to the northwest all night. Baltimore is burning.

That was all. Two short sentences.

"That's it?" said Dovid. "The biggest fire in the history of Baltimore, and he wrote twelve words?"
===
"Think about where he was," said Reb Shimon. "Five miles out in the water. More than that from the city. What could he see?"

The boys thought.

"Just the glow," said Nosson. "The sky turning red. He couldn't see the buildings. He couldn't see the fire engines or the people."

"He didn't know how big it was," said Binyamin. "He didn't know how it started, or which streets. He couldn't know any of that from out here."

"So he only wrote what he could see," said Avrumi. "A glow to the northwest. And then he guessed what it meant: Baltimore is burning. And he was right."

"Now," said Reb Shimon, "think about the newspapers you read. The ones from that week. Were those reporters standing on every street corner, watching every building burn?"

"No," said Yehuda slowly. "They talked to firemen. And shopkeepers. And people who ran away. And some of the first stories were wrong. One paper said the fire would never stop. Another said hundreds of people had died, but later reports found that almost no one died in the fire itself. They wrote fast, and they wrote what people told them."

"So each account is different," said Tzvi Hirsch, "because of who wrote it, and where he was standing, and what he could see, and why he was writing it."
===
Reb Shimon nodded slowly. "And that is true of last Friday night too," he said. "Isn't it?"

They took out Avrumi's chart again and laid it beside the old logbook.

The reporter at the Bay Courier was far away, on the phone, the next morning. He wrote fast, he got some things a little wrong, and he left out a lot.

The Coast Guard report was written by officials who wanted only the facts and the times, with no feelings at all. It was short and careful, and it was probably the most exact about the times.

Reb Shimon's log was written by someone who was right there, in the lighthouse. But it was written after Shabbos, from memory, so the times were as close as he could remember.

And Captain Turner was there too, but in a completely different place: out on the dark water, frightened, on a rocking boat. He told what he lived through. It was the most exciting account, and the most full of feeling. And his time, 9:30, was the moment the light came back for him.

"They're all true," said Nosson quietly, "from where each person was standing. And they're all a little bit not true, because nobody can see everything."
===
"So which one goes on the wall?" asked Reb Shimon.

The boys were quiet for a long time. The waves lapped softly against the iron legs below.

"None of them," said Tzvi Hirsch finally.

Everyone looked at him.

"I mean, not just one of them," he said, his words coming faster now, the way they did when he had an idea. "If we put up only the newspaper, people will think the light was out for an hour, and nobody will know about the green light. If we put up only the Coast Guard report, it's just numbers, and nobody will know how scary it was. If we put up only the captain's story, people won't know about the 9:15. Every account leaves something out."

"So we put them all up," said Avrumi, catching on. "Side by side. And next to them, a chart, like mine, showing where they agree and where they don't. And a note explaining why they're different."

"And the 1904 page too!" said Yehuda. "So people can see that it's always been like this. Every big event, people see it from different places."

Reb Shimon looked at the six boys. Then he smiled the biggest smile Tzvi Hirsch had ever seen on his face.

"I will tell the museum," he said. "I think they will say yes."

Just then, his cell phone buzzed in his pocket. He looked at it, and his eyebrows went up.

"It is Captain Turner," he said. "He says he wants to come to the opening of our wall. And he says he is bringing something that belongs to this lighthouse."
--- words
technician: a person trained to fix machines and wires
hydrants: pipes on a street where firefighters hook up their hoses to get water
officials: people in charge, who work for a city, a country or a group like the Coast Guard
exact: completely correct, with no mistakes
frightened: scared
lapped: touched gently, with small waves
