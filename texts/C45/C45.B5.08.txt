Tzvi Hirsch looked at the pencil note for a long time.

S.G. 1971. This light saved my life.

"S.G.," he said slowly. Then he looked up, across the room, at his great-uncle, who was sitting by the east window with a sefer open on his knees.

"Shimon Gold," whispered Nosson.

The two boys walked over, carrying the old logbook carefully in both hands, and set it open on the table beside Reb Shimon. Dovid and Binyamin came too. Nobody said anything. Tzvi Hirsch just pointed to the pencil note in the margin.

Reb Shimon looked at it. He took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes. Then he put them back on and smiled, a small, sad, happy smile.

"So," he said. "You found my secret. I wrote that the first time I came out here as a volunteer, many years later. I wanted to write it where the old keepers wrote. I suppose I promised you a story last night." He closed his sefer and set it aside. "Sit down, boys. It is Shabbos afternoon, and we have time. This is my story, and I will tell it to you the way I remember it."
===
"In 1971, I was nineteen years old," said Reb Shimon. "I was not much older than your big brothers. My father, Tzvi Hirsch's great-grandfather, had just opened the bakery, and there was no money. So I went to work on a ship to earn money for the family. I was the youngest man in the engine room of a cargo ship called the Atlantic Star. Down there, it was hot and loud and smelled of oil, and I loved it. I loved the engines. They were like a great, beating heart.

"One night in October, we were coming up the bay toward Baltimore, full of cargo. And the fog came in. Not a little fog. I mean a fog so thick, a man standing at the front of the ship could not see the back of the ship. It was like a wet, gray wall all around us.

"In those days we had radar, but ours was old, and that night it was broken. The captain was steering by his charts and by the clock, guessing where we were. And he guessed wrong. He thought we were farther east, in the deep channel, where the big ships go. But we were not. We had drifted west, toward the shoals.

"I was down in the engine room. I did not know any of this. I only knew that the captain had ordered us to go slow, and that everyone on deck was very, very quiet. When sailors are quiet like that, it means they are afraid."
===
Reb Shimon looked out the window at the calm gray bay.

"Then, through the fog, the lookout heard a sound. Bong. Then, a little later, bong. A bell. Slow and steady, ringing through the fog like a voice calling out a warning. And then, for one moment, the fog thinned a little, and he saw a light. One white flash. Then six seconds of dark. Then another flash.

"The captain knew that pattern. Every captain on the bay knew it. It was Kettle Shoal. And if Kettle Shoal was right there, close enough to hear its bell, then the ship was not in the channel at all. The ship was heading straight for the shoal.

"The captain shouted. He turned the wheel as hard as it would go, and he called down to the engine room for full power in reverse. I was the one who pulled that lever. I remember my hands were shaking. The whole ship shuddered. For a long, terrible minute, we did not know if we would stop in time.

"We did. We came so close to the shoal that in the morning, the captain said he could have thrown a stone onto the sand. But we did not touch it. The Atlantic Star turned and found the deep channel and came safely into Baltimore. Thirty-two men went home to their families. I went home to mine."
===
Nobody moved. Even Tzvi Hirsch, who never sat still, was perfectly still.

"In those days," Reb Shimon went on, "a keeper still lived here. He rang that bell by hand all night long, every time there was fog, so that ships could hear where the shoal was. He rang it for hours, in the cold and the wet, and he never knew if anyone heard it. I never met him. I never learned his name. But that night, he saved thirty-two lives, and he never knew it.

"When I came home, I told my father the whole story. And my father, the baker, said something I never forgot. He said, Shimon, a person who does good for you, even if he never knows it, you owe him hakaras hatov. Gratitude. You must remember him, and thank him, and pay it forward if you can.

"So years later, when I retired from the ships, and I heard that this old lighthouse was falling apart, and that the museum needed a volunteer to take care of it, I knew exactly what I had to do." He spread his big hands. "I cannot thank that keeper. But I can thank his light. I can keep it standing for the next ship, and the next boy who is afraid in the fog."
===
He looked at the four boys.

"And last night," he said softly, "the light needed me, and I was here. That is all."

Tzvi Hirsch felt his throat get tight. He looked at the old log, at the pencil note, at his great-uncle's big, gentle hands. He had always known Uncle Shimon as the old man who told long stories at the bakery and gave the twins candy. He had never known any of this.

"Uncle Shimon," he said. "You always talk about this lighthouse like it's a person."

Reb Shimon laughed. "It is an old friend," he said. "The truest friend I ever had. It never asked me for anything. It just stood out here in the water, night after night, and kept its light burning for whoever needed it."

They sat together as the afternoon light grew golden over the bay. Then they made seudah shlishis with the crackers and the last apples and sang the slow, sweet songs of the end of Shabbos, while the sun sank toward the shore.

They were in the middle of the last song when a deep horn sounded across the water, so loud that the windows hummed. The boys jumped up and ran to the window.

A Coast Guard boat was pulling up to the iron ladder. And standing on its deck, waving up at the lighthouse, was a man in a bright yellow jacket, with a boy beside him.
--- words
volunteer: someone who works without being paid, because he wants to help
cargo: the goods a ship or truck carries
channel: the deep, safe path where big ships can sail
shuddered: shook hard all over
gratitude: the feeling of being thankful
seudah: a meal, like the meals of Shabbos
