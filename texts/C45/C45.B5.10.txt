On Sunday afternoon, the whole Glen Avenue Crew sat around the big table in the back room of Gold's Bakery. It smelled like cinnamon and warm bread. Tzvi Hirsch's father had sent in a tray of rugelach, and the twins were asleep in their stroller by the door.

There were six boys now. Yehuda Benaim and Avrumi Katz had not been at the lighthouse, and they wanted to hear everything. So the four lighthouse boys told it, all at once, interrupting each other, until Yehuda held up his hand.

"Stop," he said. "One at a time. And I have a question." He took his small blue notebook out of his back pocket. "Tzvi Hirsch says the captain said 9:40. And Reb Shimon's log says 9:15. So which one is true?"

"That's what I want to know," said Tzvi Hirsch.

Yehuda tapped his pencil on the table. "Then we do what we did with the map in my Zeidy's attic. We put all the accounts side by side and compare them. What do we have?"

They had four.
===
The first was a newspaper. Dovid's father had brought it home that morning: the Bay Courier, Sunday edition. On page 3 was a short article.

STORM STRANDS BOAT, VOLUNTEER KEEPS LIGHT BURNING

A fishing boat with a father and son aboard ran aground on Kettle Shoal during Friday night's storm, after its engine failed. Both were rescued unharmed by the Coast Guard at about 10:00 p.m. The automatic beacon at the historic Kettle Shoal Lighthouse failed during the storm. A volunteer keeper, who was stranded at the lighthouse with four boys, lit a backup lamp. "The light was out for almost an hour," said a Coast Guard spokesman. The boat will be towed to Baltimore.

The second was a printout that Reb Shimon had gotten from the Coast Guard station. It was a short official report, written in the plain, careful style that officials use.

21:46 hrs. Fishing vessel Miss Ellie, 2 persons on board, recovered by rescue boat 1.5 nm south-southwest of Kettle Shoal Light. Vessel aground on the shoal, on her side. No injuries except minor cut, hand, juvenile. Kettle Shoal main light out of service 20:52 hrs. Backup light reported in service by keeper 21:15 hrs.

"What's 21:46?" asked Binyamin.

"Military time," said Avrumi. "You keep counting past twelve. 21 is 9 p.m. So 21:46 is 9:46 p.m."
===
The third account was Reb Shimon's log. Tzvi Hirsch had copied the important lines into his notebook.

The fourth was what Captain Turner had told them, which all four lighthouse boys remembered word for word, especially the end: "If your light hadn't come back on at 9:40, we'd have been on that shoal before I ever got the anchor down."

Avrumi pulled a big sheet of paper toward him. He drew a chart, the way he drew everything. Four columns: Newspaper. Coast Guard. Keeper's log. Captain. And down the side, the questions: When did the light go out? When did it come back on? When were the people rescued? Did the boat run aground?

They filled it in together, arguing about every box.

When they finished, they sat back and looked at it.

"Okay," said Yehuda. "What do they all agree on?"

"The light went out," said Nosson. "A backup lamp was lit. The Miss Ellie's engine failed. The father and son are safe. They all say that."

"And what don't they agree on?"
===
There was a lot.

The newspaper said the people were rescued at about 10:00. The Coast Guard said 21:46, which was 9:46. Reb Shimon's log said 9:46 too.

The Coast Guard said the light was out from 20:52 to 21:15. That was 23 minutes. The log said the same: 8:52 to 9:15. But the newspaper quoted a Coast Guard spokesman saying the light was out "almost an hour."

And the captain said 9:40.

"So the newspaper is just wrong," said Tzvi Hirsch.

"Maybe," said Yehuda. "But how do we know? Think about who wrote each one. The newspaper reporter wasn't there. He called somebody on the phone the next morning and wrote it fast. And look, the spokesman probably meant the main beacon. The main beacon was out for almost an hour. Longer, actually. It was out all night."

"So the spokesman was right about one thing," said Avrumi slowly, "and the reporter made it sound like a different thing."

Dovid pointed to another line. "And the newspaper says the volunteer kept the light burning. But it doesn't say one word about the boys who spotted the green light."

"Does that make it wrong?" asked Nosson.

They thought about that. "Not wrong," said Yehuda. "Just not the whole story. It leaves things out."
===
"Okay," said Tzvi Hirsch. "But what about the captain? He wasn't a reporter. He was right there! And he said 9:40. He even looked at his watch."

Binyamin had been quiet, studying the chart the way he studied a chessboard. Now he looked up.

"The log says the lamp was lit at 9:15," he said. "But think about where the captain was. He was a mile away, in the rain, on a rocking boat. And the lamp was dimmer than the main beacon. What if he didn't see it right away? What if the rain was so thick that he couldn't see it until 9:40, when he got a little closer?"

"Or," said Nosson softly, "what if his watch was wet? Or wrong? He was scared. When you are scared, you remember things a little bit wrong. Everybody does."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"So they can both be telling the truth," said Tzvi Hirsch slowly. "The lamp was lit at 9:15. And the captain first saw it at 9:40. Each one is telling what he saw, from where he was."

Yehuda wrote it in his notebook. Then the phone on the bakery wall rang. Tzvi Hirsch's father answered it, listened, and held it out to Tzvi Hirsch.

"It's Uncle Shimon," he said. "He says the museum called him. They want to put the story of Friday night on the wall of the lighthouse, so visitors can read it." He covered the phone with his hand. "But they only have room for one account. And they want you boys to choose which one."
--- words
strands: leaves someone stuck in a place they cannot get away from
spokesman: a person whose job is to speak for a group
official: coming from the people in charge
vessel: a ship or boat
juvenile: a young person, not yet a grown-up
account: a telling of what happened
