Good morning, everyone. I am Mr. Halpern, and today I want to tell you about a river. Not the Jones Falls, and not the Potomac. This river is in the middle of the ocean, and it is bigger than every river on land put together.

It is called the Gulf Stream. Picture a warm, fast current of water, about fifty miles wide, flowing out of the Gulf of Mexico, around the tip of Florida, and up along the east coast of America, past North Carolina, past Maryland, and then far out across the Atlantic toward Europe. It moves faster than a person can walk, day and night, and it never stops.

How did people find it? In the 1700s, ships carried mail between England and America. The English mail ships took about two weeks longer to reach America than the American merchant ships did. Nobody understood why. Then Benjamin Franklin, who was in charge of the mail, asked a sea captain named Timothy Folger. Folger laughed and said the answer was simple: the English captains were sailing straight into the Gulf Stream, against the current, and losing days.
===
American whale hunters knew all about it. They could see it. The water of the Gulf Stream is a deeper blue and warmer than the sea around it, and whales swim along its edges. When they met an English ship fighting the current, they would call out to warn it, but the English captains would not listen to a few whale hunters.

So Franklin and Folger drew a map of the Gulf Stream, and printed it, so that every captain could sail around the current going west and ride it going east. It was one of the first maps of an ocean current ever made.

Today, scientists know the Gulf Stream does even more. It carries warm water north, and that warmth helps keep the winters in England and Ireland much milder than they would be without it.

So the next time you stand on the beach at Ocean City and look out at the water, remember: out there, beyond what you can see, a giant warm river is flowing north, just as it has since the world was made.
