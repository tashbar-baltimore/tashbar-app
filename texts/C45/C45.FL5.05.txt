Everyone, please, one minute. Moshe, put down the sfenj. Yes, you can have it after. One minute.

Tonight is the fifth night of Chanukah. Five candles. And I am looking around this table, and I am counting too. I count children, and grandchildren, and one new son-in-law, and I think to myself: Benaim, how did you get so rich?

My father, may he rest in peace, came to this city from Meknes when he was nineteen years old. He had one suitcase, one wool scarf from his mother, and almost no English. He worked in a shoe shop on Lombard Street, six days a week, from early in the morning until dark. And every night, no matter how tired he was, he went to shul and he learned. Every night!
===
People asked him, "Yosef, why do you work so hard, and learn so hard, and sleep so little?" And he answered, "Because one day I will have a family, and I want them to have something to stand on."

Children, look at the candles. Each one is small. One small flame does not light a whole street. But we do not light one candle and stop. We add. Every night, one more. That is how a family is built, too. Not all at once. One more night of learning. One more kindness. One more Shabbos. One more.

My father lit his first Chanukah candles in this city in a tiny room above the shoe shop, all alone, in the window. Tonight, in this same city, his great-grandchildren stand around the menorah in our window.

So, my dear ones, when you look at the flames tonight, I want you to remember him. And I want you to remember: one more. Always, one more.

Now, Moshe. Now you may eat the sfenj.
--- words
suitcase: a bag with a handle for carrying clothes on a trip
kindness: being gentle and good to others
menorah: the lamp we light on Chanukah
sfenj: round Moroccan doughnuts fried for Chanukah
