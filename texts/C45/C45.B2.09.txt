Go back with us now to Monday afternoon, to the moment the rain began. This time, we will follow someone else: Reb Akiva.

When the rain came down like a wall on Ridge Trail, Reb Akiva did what he always did. He counted. He was at the front of the line, helping Dovid over a slippery rock, and as soon as Dovid was across, he turned around and counted heads through the pouring rain.

"One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight."

He counted again. Eight.

He had left camp with ten boys.

For a second, Reb Akiva felt as if the ground had dropped out from under him. Then he did what counselors are trained to do. He made himself breathe slowly. He blew his whistle, three long blasts, and called the two missing names as loud as he could. "Tzvi Hirsch! Avrumi!" The thunder rolled over his voice and swallowed it.

"Who was behind Tzvi Hirsch?" he asked the boys. "Who saw them last?"

The boys looked at each other. Nobody had seen. They had all had their heads down against the wind.

Then a quiet voice said, "I did."
===
It was Nosson Abergel. He was standing at the back of the line, soaked, with his hood pulled tight.

"Before the rain," Nosson said, "I saw Tzvi Hirsch's red cap fly off. The wind took it. It went over the edge, on the left side of the trail. Down the steep part." He pointed. "That way. West. I did not see the boys go after it. But I saw the cap."

Reb Akiva knelt down so that his eyes were level with Nosson's. "Are you sure, Nosson? The left side, by this rocky part?"

"I am sure," said Nosson. "It was red. You cannot miss red."

Reb Akiva could not take eight boys down a steep slope in a lightning storm to search. That would put eight more boys in danger. So he did the hard, right thing. He took the eight boys he had straight down the trail to camp, as fast as was safe, and he used the camp radio the moment they reached the bottom.

By four o'clock, Rabbi Weiss had called the park rangers. By five, Ranger Dan Morales and his search dog, Scout, were at the trailhead, looking at the camp map. Reb Akiva put his finger on the rocky part of Ridge Trail, and then on the steep west slope below it.

"Here," he said. "One of my boys saw the cap go over right here."

"Then that's where we start," said Ranger Morales.
===
Back at camp, nobody felt like eating supper. Rabbi Weiss gathered the whole camp in the dining room, more than two hundred boys, and they said Tehillim together. Some boys said it in a whisper. Some said it so loud that it echoed. Yehuda Benaim, from Bunk Hei, sat next to Nosson and Dovid and did not let go of his little Tehillim the whole time. Dovid kept looking at the window, where the rain was running down in streams.

Up on the mountain, the searchers spread out across the west slope. It was getting dark. They carried strong flashlights. Every few minutes, the lead searcher flashed his light three times, then three times again. That is a signal hikers learn: three of anything, three flashes, three blasts, three shouts, means "Here I am" or "I need help." If the lost boys saw it, the searchers hoped, they would know someone was looking.

Around ten o'clock that night, a searcher named Carl stopped walking. He held up his hand for quiet. Far up the slope, very faint, he heard something. Three short, high sounds. A pause. Three more.

"Whistle," said Carl. "Somebody's blowing a whistle."

They flashed their lights. Three, and three. The whistle answered. But it was very far away, and the sound bounced around the hills so much that it was hard to tell exactly where it came from. And the slope above them was steep and full of loose rocks. In the dark, climbing it would be dangerous, even for rangers.

"We know they're up there," said Ranger Morales. "We know they're answering. That means they're thinking clearly. We wait for first light."
===
At dawn, the fog was so thick that the searchers could barely see each other. But the rangers had one more tool: a search drone with a camera.

A young ranger set it up on the hood of the truck at the trailhead. Dovid Lerner, who had not slept all night, was allowed to stand behind her and watch the screen, because he was the president of the camp drone club and because Reb Akiva said, "Let him. He needs something to do."

The drone rose into the white fog and disappeared. On the screen there was only gray, gray, and more gray. Then the sun climbed higher. The fog began to thin. And as the drone flew over the west slope, the gray broke apart into patches of green trees and brown rock.

"Wait," said Dovid suddenly. "Go back. What's that?"

On the screen, in the middle of a bare patch of gray stone, there was a shape. A long white line, with two short lines at one end. An arrow. And next to it, on top of a big rock, a tiny spot of bright orange.

"That," said the young ranger slowly, "is not something nature made."

She flew the drone back and held it still above the arrow. On the screen, two small figures jumped up and down, waving their arms. She dipped the drone once, so they would know they had been seen. Then she picked up her radio.

"Dan," she said. "I've got them. West slope, the bare rock above the gully. Two boys, both standing. They built an arrow."

Here is a piece from the camp newspaper, The Pine Lake Ripple, printed on Wednesday. It was written by a boy in Bunk Beis who slept through the whole night and heard the story the next morning at breakfast.

TWO CAMPERS FOUND!
by Shmuly Rosen, Bunk Beis

Two boys from Bunk Daled, Tzvi Hirsch Gold and Avrumi Katz, got lost on Ridge Trail on Monday during the big storm. They wandered for miles through the woods all night, looking for camp. On Tuesday morning, a drone found them, and a ranger and his dog brought them back safe. The whole camp said Tehillim for them. Everyone is very happy they are back! The two boys will tell their story at the campfire tonight.

That night, the editor of The Pine Lake Ripple stood up at the campfire, in front of the whole camp.

"Tonight," he said, "the two lost campers will tell us what really happened."

And every head in the circle turned toward Tzvi Hirsch.
