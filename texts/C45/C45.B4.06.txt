On Sunday, the crew met in Dovid's basement, between a broken toaster and a half-built robot arm.

Yehuda told them what Zeidy had said. He did not tell them what Savta had told him in the kitchen. That was Zeidy's story, and it was Zeidy's to tell. He only said, "It makes him sad. It's from a long time ago. And he asked me to stop."

For a moment, nobody spoke. The robot arm twitched, and Dovid switched it off.

"Okay," said Tzvi Hirsch slowly. "So you put the map back. Fine. But first, we take pictures of every inch of it. Then we keep looking on our own, and we don't bother your Zeidy at all. He never even has to know."

"That's not a bad idea," said Dovid.

"It's a great idea," said Tzvi Hirsch. "Nobody gets hurt. And when we find the sefer Torah, we surprise him!"

Yehuda looked at the floor. It was a good idea. That was the problem. It would be so easy. Ten minutes with a camera, and nobody would ever know.

Binyamin had been setting up his small magnetic chessboard on top of the toaster, as if he were not listening. Now he moved one piece and said, "In chess, sometimes the strongest move is to wait."

"This isn't chess," said Tzvi Hirsch.

"Everything is a little bit chess," said Binyamin.
===
Then Nosson spoke, in his quiet voice that made everyone listen.

"If we find it behind his back," he said, "then we can't give it to him. Not really. It would be a present wrapped in a secret."

Tzvi Hirsch opened his mouth, and then closed it.

Avrumi pushed his glasses up. "But Yehuda already copied the map into his notebook. On the first night. Is that also behind his back? Does he have to tear out the pages?"

Everyone looked at Yehuda. It was a real question, and he did not know the answer.

"I don't know," he said. "I'm going to ask Rabbi Feldman."

On Monday morning, before davening, Yehuda waited by Rabbi Feldman's desk. He told him everything, from the attic to the box on the table after Havdalah. Rabbi Feldman listened the way he always did, with his chin on his hand and his eyes on Yehuda's face, as if nothing else in the world was happening.

When Yehuda finished, Rabbi Feldman was quiet for a moment.

"That is a real question," he said, "and it deserves a real answer. It is about kibbud, honoring your Zeidy. I am going to ask Rav Mizrachi."

All morning, Yehuda could hardly sit still. He got two math problems wrong that he knew how to do.
===
At lunch, Rabbi Feldman called him over. Without anyone asking, the other five boys drifted over too and stood in a half circle around the desk.

"I asked the Rav," said Rabbi Feldman. "Here is what he said. Honoring a Zeidy is a great mitzvah. So do exactly what your Zeidy asked, with a full heart, and with no tricks. Put the map back the way he said."

Tzvi Hirsch looked at his shoes.

"What you already learned," Rabbi Feldman went on, "is yours. Your notebook is yours. You do not have to tear out any pages. But you may not use it to go behind his back." He smiled a little. "And the Rav said one more thing. Someday, at a calm time, you may tell your Zeidy, with respect, what is in your heart. Then you listen to his answer."

Yehuda nodded slowly. It was not what he had hoped for. But somehow it made his chest feel lighter, as if one of the stones had fallen out of his pocket.

On the way out, Tzvi Hirsch walked next to him without saying anything, which was unusual. At the door he finally spoke.

"I thought being brave meant doing something," he said. "Running in. Like Mottel ran into the fire."

"He didn't run into it," said Yehuda. "He got there before the fire did. And in real life, you never go into a fire. You get out and let the firemen do their job."

"I know, I know." Tzvi Hirsch shrugged. "Maybe sometimes it means not doing something. When you really, really want to." He made a face. "That kind is harder."
===
On Tuesday after school, Yehuda went to Zeidy and Savta's house. Nosson came with him to hold the ladder. Zeidy was at shul for Mincha and Maariv, and Savta let them in with a plate of almond cookies and a long look at Yehuda that said, I know. Good boy. She gave him the box from the hall table, with the tallis bag lying on top, just as Zeidy had left it.

Dovid had given Yehuda a roll of blue painter's tape. "It won't hurt old wood," he had said. "Regular tape turns yellow and gets stuck forever."

The attic was cold and dark. Nosson held the flashlight while Yehuda knelt beside the trunk.

Yehuda picked up the tallis bag. He did not unfold the map. He did not look at it one more time, though he wanted to very badly. He put the tallis bag in the box, closed the lid, and turned the rusty key. It locked with the same crunch as before.

"Done," he said. His voice sounded strange in his own ears.

"Not yet," said Nosson. "The key goes underneath."

Yehuda turned the box over. On the bottom, there was a pale rectangle where the old yellow tape had been for so many years. The wood under it was lighter than the rest, like skin under a bandage.

He tore off a strip of blue tape and held the key over the middle of the pale rectangle.

"Wait," said Nosson.
===
He knelt down next to Yehuda and moved the flashlight. He did not shine it straight down. He held it low, almost flat against the floor, so that the light slid sideways across the bottom of the box.

"My Abba does this with an esrog," Nosson said softly. "Light from the side shows things that light from the top hides."

And in the sideways light, inside the pale rectangle, Yehuda saw them.

Tiny letters. Written in brown ink, so small and so close to the color of the wood that on Chanukah night, in the dark, with his heart pounding, he had not seen them at all. For more than sixty years, the old tape had covered them like a blanket.

They were Hebrew letters, but the words were not Hebrew. They were Yiddish, two short lines that Yehuda could sound out but not understand. In the middle of the first line, he saw three letters he knew very well by now.

Samech. Pei. Reish.

And right after them, a word that began with a zayin.

"Reb Z," whispered Nosson.

Yehuda's heart was pounding again, just like on the first night. In one hand, he held the key. His notebook was in his back pocket, and his pencil was right there, behind his ear.

He had promised to lock the box. He had promised no tricks.

Yehuda's hand moved slowly toward his pencil. Then it stopped.
