Dear Zeidy,

Greetings from camp! I am writing this on my top bunk with a flashlight, because it is rest hour and my counselor says rest hour is for resting. Writing is resting, I told him. He laughed and said fine.

First, the good news. I passed my swimming test on the first try! I had to swim two lengths of the lake dock and float on my back for one whole minute. The water was freezing, but I did not complain, because you told me that a strong person does not complain about cold water. Well, I complained a little, but only inside my head.

Second, the interesting news. Yesterday we went on our first real hike, up the blue trail to the lookout. Reb Shaya made us learn the four rules of the trail by heart. Halfway up, he suddenly held up his hand, and we all froze. Right next to the trail stood a mother deer and two baby fawns with white spots. Nobody moved. Nobody talked. Even Tzvi Hirsch, who has never been quiet for one minute in his whole life, was quiet for one whole minute!
===
Then the mother deer lifted her head, looked straight at us, and walked away into the trees, slowly, as if she were the one in charge. The fawns hopped after her. Reb Shaya said, "Boys, you just saw something most people never see, because you knew how to be still." Tzvi Hirsch says he was being still on purpose. I think he was just too surprised to talk.

Third, the bad news. The food is not like Bubby's. On Shabbos we had chicken, and it was fine, but it was not Bubby's chicken. Please tell her I said so. She will like that.

I miss you. I miss learning with you on Sunday mornings. I am trying to learn a little every night with my chavrusa, Binyamin. We are learning the same mishna you taught me, and I am the one who explains it! It is harder to explain than to listen.

Write back soon. And please send more of Bubby's mandelbroit. A lot more.

Your grandson,
Avrumi
--- words
greetings: a friendly hello
complain: to say that you are unhappy about something
lookout: a high place where you can see far
chavrusa: a learning partner
mishna: a part of the Oral Torah that boys learn by heart and explain
