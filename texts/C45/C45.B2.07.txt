The buzzing grew louder and louder, until it seemed to fill the whole white sky.

"What is it?" Tzvi Hirsch said. "A plane?"

"Too small for a plane," said Avrumi, squinting up into the fog. "Too loud for a bird."

The fog was thinning now. The sun had climbed above the ridge, and the white air was turning gold and full of light. Above them, a patch of blue sky opened up, and then another. The trees at the edge of the stone patch came back, one by one, dripping and shining.

And then they saw it.

It came gliding over the tops of the trees, about as high as a three-story building. It was small and gray, no bigger than a pizza box, with four spinning propellers, one at each corner. A tiny red light blinked on its belly.

"A drone!" said Tzvi Hirsch. "Dovid's drone club has one just like that! But bigger. No, smaller. No... I don't know!"

The drone flew straight across the stone patch, right over the giant white arrow. It went past the arrow, past the boys. Then it stopped in midair, as if it had changed its mind. It turned around slowly. It came back. It hovered right above the big rock, where the orange poncho was flapping in the breeze.
===
The boys jumped up and down. They waved their arms over their heads like windmills. They blew their whistles, three blasts, three blasts, three blasts, until they ran out of breath.

The drone hung in the air, buzzing. Its little red light blinked and blinked.

Then it dipped. It dropped down a little, then rose back up. Like a nod.

"Did you see that?" said Tzvi Hirsch. "It nodded! It saw us!"

The drone turned, rose higher, and flew away down the mountain, getting smaller and smaller, until its buzzing faded and was gone.

The two boys stood in the middle of their stone arrow, breathing hard.

"Did it really see us?" said Avrumi. "Or did it just fly over?"

Nobody answered. The forest was quiet again, except for birds and dripping leaves. A drone could see. But a drone could not talk. And it could not tell them if anybody had been watching its screen.

"It nodded," said Tzvi Hirsch again. But this time, he did not sound so sure.
===
Tzvi Hirsch looked down the mountain, in the direction the drone had gone. Avrumi could see exactly what he was thinking. He was thinking: follow it. It came from somewhere, and it went back to somewhere. Somewhere with people.

Tzvi Hirsch even took one step. His sneaker scraped on the stone.

Then he turned and looked at Avrumi.

Avrumi did not say anything this time. He did not have to. He just looked back.

Tzvi Hirsch let out a long breath. He took his foot back. He sat down on a rock at the edge of the arrow.

"We stay," he said. "If it saw us, they'll come here. Right to this spot. And if we leave, they'll come and find an arrow pointing at nobody."

Avrumi sat down next to him. "Yesterday you would have run," he said.

"Yesterday I did run," said Tzvi Hirsch. He pulled his knees up and rested his chin on them. "That's how we got here."
===
So they waited.

They ate the last of the trail mix, every raisin and every peanut. They drank the last sips of water, sharing it carefully, one sip for you, one sip for me. They davened Shacharis, facing east, toward the sun that was now warm and bright on their faces. They blew their whistles every few minutes.

The waiting was the hardest part of all. Harder than the storm. Harder than the dark. In the storm, there had been things to do: run, crouch, count. Now there was nothing to do but sit and listen and hope.

"Tell me something," said Tzvi Hirsch. "Anything. So the time goes faster."

So Avrumi told him about the sefer Torah his Abba was writing. How Abba made his own ink. How he used a feather from a turkey, cut to a sharp point, for a pen. How every single letter had to be perfect, and if one letter touched another letter, the whole word could be pasul. How it took his Abba a whole year to write one sefer Torah, letter by letter, line by line, with patience.

"A whole year?" said Tzvi Hirsch. "I couldn't wait a whole year for anything."

"You waited all night," said Avrumi.

Tzvi Hirsch thought about that. "Yeah," he said slowly. "I guess I did."
===
The sun climbed higher. The rock got warm. A small brown lizard came out of a crack in the stone and sat on the end of the arrow, looking at them, as if it wanted to know what they were doing there.

Avrumi was just about to blow his whistle again when he heard something.

Far down the slope, there was a sound in the bushes. Not the gentle rustle of a lizard. Not the crash of a deer. This sound was fast and steady, crunch-crunch-crunch, getting closer, like something running.

Then came a new sound. A bark. A loud, happy bark.

The bushes at the edge of the stone patch burst open, and out came a big brown dog with floppy ears. She was wearing a bright orange vest, the same color as the poncho on the rock. She ran straight across the arrow to the two boys, barking and wagging her tail so hard her whole body wagged with it.

And from somewhere behind her, in the trees, a man's voice called out, loud and clear:

"Tzvi Hirsch Gold! Avrumi Katz! Can you hear me?"
--- words
squinting: looking with your eyes partly closed, to see better
propellers: spinning blades that push a machine through the air
hovered: stayed in one place in the air
windmills: machines with big arms that spin in the wind
pasul: not kosher, not fit to use for a mitzvah
lizard: a small animal with scaly skin, four legs and a long tail
