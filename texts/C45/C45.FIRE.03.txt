This letter is made up, but the facts about the fire are true. Imagine a shopkeeper named Moshe Kaplan. He sold cloth in a small shop downtown, and he lived with his family on Lloyd Street, across the Jones Falls in East Baltimore. Two days after the fire, he wrote to his brother in New York. He wrote in Yiddish. Here it is in English.

Wednesday, February 10, 1904

My dear brother Avraham,

You have surely read in the New York papers that Baltimore burned. I am writing at once so that you do not worry. Baruch Hashem, we are all alive and well. Rivka, the children and I are safe, and our home on Lloyd Street is standing. But my shop is gone. Of the shop, Avraham, there is nothing left but ashes and four black walls.
===
Let me tell you how it was. On Sunday morning I was at home, learning with Shloimy. A neighbor ran in and shouted that there was a fire downtown. From our roof we could see the smoke, but it was far away, and I was not afraid. Fires happen.

By the afternoon, the sky was black. The wind was blowing hard, and people said the fire was jumping from street to street. Shloimy and I took the pushcart and hurried to the shop. The streets were full of people running the other way. Policemen shouted at us to go home. I said, "Officer, everything I own is in that shop."

We filled the pushcart with as many bolts of cloth as it could hold, the good wool on the bottom. Shloimy pushed, and I pulled. Twice the wheels got stuck in the crowd, and twice strangers helped us lift it free. When we turned around at the corner, the roof of the building across from my shop was on fire. That was the last time I saw my shop.
===
That night nobody on our street slept. First we stood on the bridge over the Falls and watched the fire come closer. The heat reached us even there. Someone said, "If it crosses the water, all of East Baltimore will burn." Then the men from our shul went inside and said Tehillim, chapter after chapter, until the sun came up. I was with them.

On Monday the fire reached the Falls, and there it stopped. The firemen stood along the water with their hoses and would not let it cross. Avraham, I cannot tell you what it was like to see it stop. Grown men cried in the street.

People are saying the fire started when someone dropped a cigar in a basement. Others say it was something else. Who can know? The firemen do not even know yet.
===
Now I will tell you the good part. Our neighbors have been like family. Mrs. Gordon from next door brought us a pot of soup on the first night, because Rivka was too tired to cook. Mr. Dunn, the coal man on the corner, who is not Jewish, let me keep my cloth in his dry shed for free. He said, "Kaplan, you would do the same for me." He is right. I would.

What will I do now? I have the cloth that Shloimy and I saved. I will find a new shop, maybe smaller, and begin again. The city is already talking about building everything back, stronger than before. With Hashem's help, we will build too.

Please do not send money yet. Send a letter, and tell Mama that we are well.

Your loving brother,
Moshe
--- words
pushcart: a cart with wheels that a person pushes by hand
bolts: long rolls of cloth
strangers: people you do not know
Tehillim: the book of Psalms, which Jews say to ask Hashem for help
cigar: a thick roll of tobacco leaves that people smoke
shed: a small, simple building for storing things
