For a moment after the wave hit, nobody moved. The candle flames shivered, then stood straight again. The plates on the shelf stopped rattling. Somewhere under the floor, water hissed and drained away between the iron legs.

Dovid let out a shaky laugh. "Well," he said. "Gut Shabbos to you too, Chesapeake Bay."

Everybody laughed then, even Reb Shimon, and the laugh made the little room feel warm again.

"Come," said Reb Shimon. "The bay is busy with its work. We have our own work. It is time to greet the Shabbos."

There was no shul out here, no aron kodesh, no chazzan. There were four boys in borrowed shirts and one old man with a white beard, standing by the south window in a six-sided room. But Reb Shimon said that Shabbos does not need a building to come in. It comes wherever Jews are ready for it.

They had already davened Mincha together, an hour before candle lighting. Now Reb Shimon began Kabbalas Shabbos. He did not have a strong voice anymore, but it was steady, and he knew every word by heart. The boys joined in. Outside, the wind howled and the rain beat against the shutters, and inside they sang, a little louder with every psalm, as if they were trying to sing over the storm.
===
When they came to Lecha Dodi, Tzvi Hirsch noticed something.

Lecha Dodi is a song with many verses, and each verse is like a little room in a house. The first verses talk about Shabbos as a queen, a bride, something precious that we go out to meet. The middle verses change. They talk to Yerushalayim, which was destroyed and is waiting to be rebuilt. They say: wake up, shake off the dust, do not be ashamed. And at the end, the last verse turns again, back to Shabbos, and everyone turns around to face the door, to welcome her in.

Tzvi Hirsch had sung it every Friday night of his life without thinking about the order. But tonight, with the storm outside, he heard it differently. First you go out to meet something good. Then you remember what is broken and hope it will be fixed. Then you turn around, and the good thing is already here.

When they reached the last verse, Reb Shimon turned to face the door of the cottage, and the boys turned with him. The door rattled in the wind. For a funny second, Tzvi Hirsch half expected it to fly open. It did not. But he bowed anyway, like his Abba always did, and he felt that something had come in.
===
Then it was time to eat.

Reb Shimon made Kiddush on the grape juice, poured into a plastic cup because there was no silver becher. His hand shook a little as he lifted it, but his voice did not. Then they all washed, one by one, at the tiny sink, pouring water from a coffee mug because there was no washing cup.

Tzvi Hirsch uncovered the two challos. They were a little squashed on one side from the boat, but they were from Gold's Bakery, and Tzvi Hirsch knew exactly which ones they were. They were the round ones his father braided himself every Friday at five in the morning, the ones with extra sesame seeds on top. Reb Shimon made the bracha, and tore the challah, and handed a piece to each boy.

Nobody said anything for a minute. They just chewed. It was the best challah any of them had ever eaten.

"My father makes these," said Tzvi Hirsch quietly. "He doesn't even know we're eating them out here."

"He knows," said Reb Shimon. "Your mother knows. Believe me, right now your whole family is thinking about exactly this table."
===
They ate Ima's cold chicken off paper plates, and apple slices, and crackers with peanut butter because Binyamin said it was the best way to finish chicken, which nobody believed until they tried it. Then Dovid cut the chocolate babka with a plastic knife, very carefully, into five exactly equal pieces, squinting at every cut like an engineer. Everyone said he was ridiculous. Everyone also checked that their piece was not smaller.

Then they sang zemiros.

At home, Tzvi Hirsch's family sang zemiros every Friday night, but the twins usually fussed, and the phone sometimes rang before Shabbos with last-minute bakery orders, and everybody had a different favorite tune. Here there was nothing to interrupt. They sang Menucha V'Simcha, and Ka Ribon, and Yom Zeh Mechubad. Nosson taught them a Moroccan tune his grandfather sang in Montreal, and Binyamin taught them a Bukharian tune, and they sang the same words three different ways. Reb Shimon closed his eyes when they sang. His foot tapped under the table.

Between songs, the storm filled in the silence. The wind moaned. The waves boomed against the legs below. But somehow, every song made the storm seem a little smaller.
===
After the zemiros, Nosson asked the question that all four boys had been wondering.

"Reb Shimon," he said politely, "why do you love this lighthouse so much? You come out here all the time. You fix everything. You know every board."

Reb Shimon looked at him for a long moment. He rubbed his white beard. Then he looked toward the window, where every six seconds the white beam from the lantern room above swept past and lit up the rain.

"When I was a young man," he began slowly, "not much older than Tzvi Hirsch's big cousins, I worked on a ship. One night, there was a fog..."

He stopped. He shook his head and smiled a small smile.

"Another time," he said. "It is a long story, and you are falling asleep in your chairs."

"We're not!" said Tzvi Hirsch, who was.

But Reb Shimon only chuckled and began to clear the plates. Tzvi Hirsch watched the window. Flash. Six seconds of dark. Flash. Six seconds of dark. It was like a heartbeat. It was the steadiest thing in the whole stormy night.

Flash. Six seconds of dark.

Flash.

He counted. Four. Five. Six. Seven. Eight. Nine. Ten.

The beam did not come back.

verse: one part of a song or poem
precious: very special and worth a lot
becher: a special cup for Kiddush, often made of silver
ridiculous: very silly, enough to make people laugh
interrupt: to break in and stop something for a moment
