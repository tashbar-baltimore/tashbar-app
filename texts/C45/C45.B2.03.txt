The whole sky flashed white.

For one second, every tree and every rock around them was as bright as noon. Then everything went dark gray again. Avrumi's heart was pounding so hard he could feel it in his ears.

Then came the thunder. It was not a far-away rumble anymore. It was a huge CRACK, like the sky was splitting in half, and then a long roll that shook the ground under their sneakers.

Tzvi Hirsch grabbed Avrumi's arm. "Avrumi. We're on top of a hill."

Avrumi knew what he meant. They were standing on high ground, in a storm, next to a tall pine tree. It was exactly the wrong place to be.

Avrumi tried to think. His Abba had taught him what to do in a lightning storm, on their hike last fall. Abba had made him say it back three times, the way Reb Akiva had with the whistle rule. Now the words came back to him, one by one, in his Abba's calm voice.

Get off the high ground. Stay away from the tallest tree. Find a low place with small trees all around. Crouch down low, on your feet, and make yourself small.

"Come on," said Avrumi. "Down. But not far. Just off the top."
===
They went a little way down the other side of the hill, where the ground dipped into a hollow. It was full of young trees, all about the same size, none of them much taller than a basketball hoop. The two boys set their backpacks on the wet ground and crouched on top of them, the way Abba had shown him, with their feet close together and their heads down.

The sky flashed again.

"Count," said Avrumi. "Count out loud, from the flash to the thunder."

"Why?"

"Just count!"

"One... two... three..." BOOM.

"Three seconds," said Avrumi. "Every five seconds is about one mile. So the lightning is a little more than half a mile away."

"How do you know that?" asked Tzvi Hirsch.

"Light is super fast. You see the flash almost right away. Sound is much slower. It takes about five seconds for thunder to go one mile. So the more seconds you count, the farther away the lightning is."

Another flash. This time, Avrumi remembered something else. "There's a bracha for lightning, too," he said. "Oseh ma'aseh vereishis." He said it, and Tzvi Hirsch said it after him, and then they were both quiet. It was a strange feeling, saying a bracha in the middle of a storm. Strange, but good. It reminded Avrumi that they were not really alone.
===
They counted every flash.

"One, two, three, four, five, six." Six seconds. More than a mile.

A while later: "One, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve." Twelve seconds. More than two miles.

"The numbers are getting bigger," said Tzvi Hirsch slowly. "So the storm is moving away from us."

"Right," said Avrumi. For the first time since the cap blew away, he let out a long breath.

The rain was still falling, but it was softer now, a steady patter on their ponchos instead of a roar. The thunder was a grumble again, far off to the east. The lightning flashes were dim, like someone flicking a light switch in another room.

Abba's rule was to wait until the thunder was gone for a long time, not just far away. So they stayed crouched, counting, until they could not hear it at all. Only then did they stand up.

Tzvi Hirsch stood up first. His legs were stiff from crouching. He took the yellow cord from around his neck, put the whistle to his lips, and blew as hard as he could.

Three blasts. Long and loud.

They listened.

Rain dripping from the leaves. Water gurgling somewhere. A bird starting to sing, as if to ask whether the storm was over.

Avrumi blew his whistle too. Three blasts.

Nothing.

They blew again, and again, and again, until their cheeks hurt. Nobody blew back.
===
"They can't hear us," said Tzvi Hirsch. "They're too far. They probably went all the way down to camp." He looked down the hill, toward the gully where his cap had disappeared. "Camp is down. Everything is down from here. Pine Lake is at the bottom of the mountain. If we just keep going downhill, we'll find it."

He took a step.

"Stop," said Avrumi.

Tzvi Hirsch turned around. "What?"

Avrumi swallowed. It was hard to say what he wanted to say. Tzvi Hirsch was the brave one, the fast one, the one who always had a plan. Avrumi was the quiet one. But he remembered the last thing his Abba had taught him, and it was the most important thing of all.

"When you're lost," said Avrumi, "you stop. You stay where you are. My Abba made me promise. If you keep walking, you get more lost, and you get farther from where people will look for you. The rangers say it too. Stop, stay together, and blow your whistle."

"But it's going to get dark!"

"That's why. It's worse to walk in the dark. We could fall. We could go the wrong way down the wrong side of the mountain. Reb Akiva counts heads every few steps. He'll know about where we went missing. That's where they'll look. We have to stay close to there."
===
For a long moment, Tzvi Hirsch did not move. The rain dripped off the end of his nose. Avrumi could see him thinking: his eyes went down the hill, and then up to the gray sky, and then back to Avrumi.

Tzvi Hirsch hated waiting. He hated sitting still. In class, his foot tapped all day. On Shabbos, he was always the first one out the door after davening.

"Okay," Tzvi Hirsch said at last, very quietly. "Okay. We stay."

He sat down on his backpack. Avrumi sat down next to him. Their shoulders touched. It was a little warmer that way.

"I'm sorry," said Tzvi Hirsch. "About the cap. About running. It's my fault you're out here."

Avrumi opened his mouth to answer. But just then, from the bushes on the far side of the hollow, came a loud crack, like a branch snapping. Then another. Then a heavy rustling, the sound of something big pushing through the wet leaves.

Both boys froze.

Something was crashing through the bushes. And it was coming straight toward them.
--- words
crouch: to bend your knees and get down low, close to the ground
hollow: a low place in the ground, like a shallow bowl
patter: a quick, soft, tapping sound
grumble: a low, rough sound, like a growl
rangers: people whose job is to care for a park and keep people safe in it
promise: to say for sure that you will do something
