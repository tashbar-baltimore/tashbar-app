Zeidy Benaim's attic was the dustiest place in Baltimore, and Yehuda loved it.

It was the second night of Chanukah, and the whole family had come to Zeidy and Savta's house, a few blocks from Glen Avenue. The kitchen smelled of hot oil and orange peel. Savta was frying sfenj, the round Moroccan doughnuts she made every Chanukah, and Yehuda's little sisters, Esther and Malka, stood on chairs to watch them puff up. In the dining room, Zeidy was singing Maoz Tzur in his deep, happy voice while he cleaned the menorah for the night's lighting.

Zeidy's father had come from Morocco as a young man, but Zeidy's mother had been born right here in Baltimore, to a family from Lithuania. Savta came from Meknes, in Morocco. So Yehuda called his grandfather Zeidy, the Yiddish way, and his grandmother Savta, and the Chanukah table had sfenj and latkes.

"Zeidy," said Yehuda, "is it time?"

Zeidy stopped singing just long enough to smile. "One box," he said, holding up one finger. "Just one. You know the rule."

Yehuda knew the rule. Every Chanukah since he was seven, Zeidy had let him climb the narrow ladder in the upstairs hall and choose one box from the attic. One box a year. Last year it had been a box of old seforim with gold letters. The year before, it had been letters from Morocco, with stamps of lions and palm trees.
===
Yehuda took the flashlight from its hook, climbed the ladder, and pushed open the square door in the ceiling.

The attic was cold and low, and it smelled of dust and old paper. Boxes were piled everywhere: cardboard boxes, a trunk with leather straps, a hatbox. Yehuda moved the flashlight slowly from one to the next. He was eleven now, in fifth grade, and he had learned that choosing a box was serious business. You only got one.

Then the light landed on something he had never noticed before. It was pushed far back under the slanted roof, behind the trunk, as if someone had wanted it to stay hidden.

It was a flat wooden box, about as big as a large sefer. The wood was dark and smooth, and the lock on the front was orange with rust.

Yehuda crawled over and pulled it out. He turned it over, and there, on the bottom, was a small iron key, held on by a strip of old tape that had turned yellow and stiff.

"This one," he whispered.

He peeled the tape off as gently as he could. The key fit, and the lid came up with a crunch.

Inside lay a faded tallis bag. Once, it must have been dark blue velvet. Now it was the color of a rainy sky, and one corner was brown and hard, as if it had been left too close to a stove.
===
Yehuda opened the tallis bag. There was no tallis inside. There was only a folded paper, brown at the edges and soft as cloth.

He unfolded the paper slowly, one fold at a time.

It was a map.

It was a map of Baltimore, but not the Baltimore Yehuda knew. Somebody had drawn it by hand, in brown ink, with a thin, careful pen. There was the harbor, with tiny ships. There were rows and rows of streets. But many of the streets had names he had never heard of. German Street? There was no German Street in Baltimore.

A large part of the map was covered with fine lines, close together, like the shading in a drawing. On the right side of the shading ran a wavy line, like a little river. In the middle of the shaded part sat a tiny square with a tiny picture on top of it. Yehuda brought it close to his eyes. It was a cap, the kind with a short brim that men wore in very old pictures.

A row of dots went from the little cap, across the wavy line, and down toward the water. There, near the harbor, someone had drawn a tiny star. Next to the star were three Hebrew letters:

ס פ ר

Yehuda read them under his breath. Samech. Pei. Reish.
===
His heart was beating fast. He took out the small blue notebook he always kept in his back pocket, and he copied everything: the shading, the wavy line, the cap, the row of dots, the star, and the three letters. He copied the letters twice, to be sure.

Then he folded the map along its old lines, slid it back into the tallis bag, and climbed down the ladder with the bag held against his chest, the way you hold something that matters.

Downstairs, Zeidy was still singing. He had reached the part of Maoz Tzur about the Greeks, and he sang it loudly, the way he always did, so that Malka would giggle.

Yehuda came into the dining room holding the map.

The singing stopped.

Zeidy put down the polishing cloth very slowly. He looked at the faded tallis bag. For a long moment he said nothing at all, and the only sound in the house was the sizzle of Savta's oil in the kitchen.

"Where did you find that?" he asked, in a voice Yehuda had never heard before.

"In the attic," said Yehuda. "In a flat wooden box, way in the back. The key was taped underneath." He swallowed. "Zeidy, I'm sorry. Was I not supposed to?"

Zeidy held out his hand, and Yehuda gave him the bag. Zeidy did not open it. He just held it, gently, with both hands, the way you hold a baby who has just fallen asleep.
===
"You did nothing wrong," Zeidy said at last. "One box. That was the rule." He tried to smile, but the smile did not reach his eyes. "Go help Savta. It's almost time to light."

They lit the menorah in the front window, two little flames and the shamash. Zeidy sang too, but quietly, and he kept looking at the sideboard, where the tallis bag lay next to the bottle of oil.

After the sfenj and the latkes, and a game of dreidel that Esther won by a lot, Yehuda went to the kitchen for a drink. He stopped in the doorway.

Zeidy was standing alone at the dark window. In his hands was an old photograph from the drawer of the sideboard, small and brown, with a white border. A young man in a cap stood in front of a building, or what was left of a building. The windows were black holes. The roof was gone. Only the front wall still stood, with a few broken painted letters across the top.

Zeidy turned the photo over. On the back, written in the same brown ink as the map, was a date: February 1904.

Yehuda did not mean to make a sound, but the floor creaked. Zeidy turned around.

He did not look angry. He looked like someone who had been very far away and had just come back.

"Come here, Yehuda," he said softly.
===
Yehuda came. Zeidy put the photo in his hand.

"This is my Zeidy," he said. "My mother's father. His name was Mottel Rosen. When this picture was taken, he was eighteen years old, and he made caps for a living."

Yehuda looked at the young man. He was thin, and his coat was too big for him, and he was not smiling. But he stood very straight, like someone who had done something hard and was not sorry.

"In February of 1904," said Zeidy, "a great fire burned the whole middle of Baltimore. My Zeidy was there. Everyone else was running away from the fire." He tapped the photo. "He ran toward it, while it was still a few streets away."

"Why?" Yehuda whispered.

"To save something. When I was a little boy, he told me he had carried the most precious thing in Baltimore out of that fire." Zeidy looked toward the dining room, where the tallis bag lay. "I always thought it was a sefer Torah. But which one, and where he took it, he never told me."

"More than sixty years I have wondered," said Zeidy. "And tonight a map comes down from my own attic, with a star on it."

He looked at Yehuda, and for the first time all evening, something bright came into his eyes.

"So tell me, detective," he said. "What do you think is under that star?"
--- words
slanted: leaning or sloping, not flat or straight up
faded: lost its color over a long time
peeled: pulled something off slowly, like a sticker
brim: the part of a cap or hat that sticks out in front
sizzle: the hissing sound food makes when it fries in hot oil
precious: worth very, very much; too special to lose
