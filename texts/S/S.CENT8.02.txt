Eli Mizrahi taped a long strip of paper across the newsroom wall. "Akiva wants facts and dates," he said, "so my story is a timeline." He began with the land itself.

For almost two thousand years, Jews all over the world have turned toward Eretz Yisroel when they daven. Some Jews always lived there, in Yerushalayim, Tzfas, Teverya and Chevron, and over the years many more came to live and learn in the holy land.

For about four hundred years, until World War I, the Ottoman Empire ruled the land. In 1917, during that war, Britain issued the Balfour Declaration, a letter that promised to help build a Jewish home in the land. After the war, the Ottomans lost the land. In 1920, Britain began to rule it, and Britain ruled until 1948. These years are often called the British Mandate.

The Jews and the Arabs who lived in the land could not agree on its future, and fighting broke out many times. On November 29, 1947, the United Nations, a group of the world's countries, voted to divide the land into two states, one Jewish and one Arab.

On May 14, 1948, the day the British left, the Jewish leaders declared the State of Israel. On the Jewish calendar, that day was the fifth of Iyar.

The very next day, the armies of five Arab countries attacked. This war, which began right after the State was declared, is called the War of Independence. It lasted into 1949. When it ended, the Old City of Yerushalayim, with the Kosel, was under the rule of Jordan, and Jews could not go there to daven.
===
Eli moved his finger along the strip to the next mark.

In June 1967, after weeks of rising tension with its neighbors, a war broke out that lasted only six days. It is called the Six-Day War. During that war, Israel took the Old City of Yerushalayim. For the first time in nineteen years, Jews could go to the Kosel, the Western Wall that still stands from the wall around the Har HaBayis. Thousands came to daven there that Shavuos.

In October 1973, on Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the year, Egypt and Syria attacked by surprise while most people were in shul. This was the Yom Kippur War. It was hard and costly, and it lasted about three weeks. Six years later, in 1979, Israel and Egypt signed a peace treaty. It was the first peace treaty between Israel and one of its neighbors.

"Those are the facts and the dates," said Eli. Akiva checked every one of them twice.

Then Benny Okafor-Levine added a paragraph about something that is not on any timeline. Through all those years, Torah learning in Eretz Yisroel grew and grew. Great yeshivos that had stood in Europe were built again there, and new ones were founded. Today, Yerushalayim, Bnei Brak and many other towns are full of shuls, yeshivos and kollelim. Boys from our own yeshiva go there to learn after high school, and families travel to daven at the Kosel.
===
Yoni Kaplan brought a faded sign to the newsroom. It said, in big painted letters, LET MY PEOPLE GO. "My Zeidy carried this in Washington," he said. "This is the story I want to write."

During the Cold War, about two million Jews lived in the Soviet Union. Their lives were very hard. The communist government fought against every religion, and it worked hardest of all against Judaism. Shuls were closed. Jewish schools were shut. Teaching Hebrew could land a man in prison, and a boy who wanted to learn Torah had to find a teacher in secret.

Many Jews held on anyway. They met in small apartments with the curtains drawn and learned Gemara late at night. They baked matzah at home before Pesach. Fathers whispered the alef-beis to their children so the neighbors would not hear.

Many Soviet Jews wanted to leave, most of all for Eretz Yisroel. But to leave, a person needed permission from the government, and it was usually refused. A Jew who asked to leave and was turned down was called a refusenik. Refuseniks were often fired from their jobs, and some were sent to prison camps. One of them, Natan Sharansky, spent nine years in prison before he was freed in 1986.

Jews in America and around the world would not stay quiet. Children wrote letters to refuseniks, and shuls hung their names on the wall. Then, on December 6, 1987, about 250,000 people gathered in Washington, D.C., the capital of the United States. It was one of the largest rallies the city had ever seen. Speakers stood on a stage near the Capitol, and the crowd filled the long lawn of the National Mall. They called on the Soviet leader to let the Jews go.

"Zeidy said it was freezing cold," Yoni told the others, "and nobody cared."

In 1989 the Berlin Wall fell, and in 1991 the Soviet Union came to an end. The gates opened at last, and more than a million Jews left. Many went to Eretz Yisroel, and many came to America. Some of the boys in our yeshivos today are their grandchildren.
--- words
declared: announced in an official way
Kosel: the Western Wall in Yerushalayim, where Jews come to daven
treaty: a written agreement signed by two or more countries
kollelim: places where married men learn Torah all day
refusenik: a Soviet Jew who asked to leave and was turned down
rallies: big gatherings of people who come together for a cause
