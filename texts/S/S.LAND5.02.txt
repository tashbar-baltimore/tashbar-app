Mr. Halpern filled a big jug with 100 small cups of water. "Pretend this jug holds all the water on earth," he told the class. Then he poured 97 cups into a bucket labeled OCEAN. "Ninety-seven out of every hundred parts of the earth's water are salty ocean," he said. "We cannot drink them, and farmers cannot water crops with them."

That left only three cups of fresh water. Fresh water is water with very little salt in it. We drink it, cook with it, wash with it and grow our food with it. Mr. Halpern lifted two of the three cups. "Most of the fresh water is not even easy to reach," he said. "It is frozen."

Most of the earth's fresh water is locked up as ice. Huge sheets of ice cover the land near the North and South Poles. Rivers of ice called glaciers creep slowly down tall mountains. All that ice is fresh water, but it is frozen, and most of it is far away from where people live.
===
Mr. Halpern held up the very last cup. "Almost all the fresh water people use comes from this little bit," he said. This last cup stands for the water in lakes, rivers, streams and wetlands, and the water hidden underground. It is only about one cup out of a hundred, yet it serves the whole world.

Here is a puzzle. Rain is fresh water, but much of it starts out in the salty ocean. How does salty water turn into fresh rain? The sun is the answer. The sun warms the sea, and some of the water evaporates. It turns into an invisible gas called water vapor and rises into the air. The salt does not rise with it. Salt cannot evaporate, so it stays behind in the sea.

High in the sky, the air is cool. The water vapor cools and turns back into tiny drops of water. The drops gather into clouds. When the drops grow heavy enough, they fall as rain or snow. That rain is fresh, because the salt was left behind in the ocean.
===
This journey of water, from the sea to the sky and back down again, is called the water cycle. It never stops. The same water has been going around and around since the world was created. The water in your cup at lunch may once have been part of a glacier or a cloud over a faraway sea.

Because there is so little fresh water to use, every drop matters. Families can save water in simple ways. Turn off the tap while you brush your teeth. Fix a dripping faucet. Take shorter showers. Run the dishwasher only when it is full. A dripping tap can waste many buckets of water in one week, so even small leaks are worth fixing. Water the garden in the evening, when less water will evaporate in the sun.

Avrumi Katz raised his hand. "So the ocean is huge," he said, "but the water we can really use fits in one little cup." Mr. Halpern nodded. "That," he said, "is why we must never waste it."
--- words
labeled: marked with words that tell what something is
evaporates: turns from a liquid into a gas
invisible: not able to be seen
vapor: water or another liquid in the form of a gas
glaciers: huge, slow-moving rivers of ice
wetlands: low, soggy land covered with shallow water, like a swamp
