Yoni Kaplan set a small recorder on his Zeidy's kitchen table. "It is for The Beacon," he said. "How did our family come to America?"

Zeidy poured two glasses of tea. "My own zeidy came in 1906," he began. "He was fifteen years old."

In those years, life was very hard for the Jews of Eastern Europe, in Russia, Poland and the lands around them. Many were very poor. Jewish boys could be taken into the czar's army for years. And there were pogroms, violent attacks on Jewish towns, and the police often did nothing to stop them.

So the Jews began to leave. From 1880 to 1924, about two million Jews came to America. It was one of the largest journeys in Jewish history.

Zeidy’s zeidy crossed the Atlantic Ocean by ship. The trip took about two weeks, and he slept in steerage, the crowded bottom deck. Then one morning everyone rushed up to see the Statue of Liberty in New York harbor.

Most immigrants were checked at Ellis Island, a small island in the harbor. In a great hall, doctors checked each person's eyes and health. Then officials asked questions and checked papers. For most people it took only a few hours. A ferry then carried them across to Manhattan.

"My zeidy told me he held his tefillin bag the whole time," said Zeidy. "It was the one thing he was afraid to lose."
===
Most new arrivals settled on the Lower East Side of New York. Whole families crowded into tenements, tall apartment buildings with tiny rooms and one sink in the hall. Some worked long hours in clothing shops. Others became peddlers, selling pots, buttons and fruit from pushcarts.

The streets of America were not paved with gold. But there was freedom: no czar, no pogroms, and a chance for every child to go to school.

The hardest test was Shabbos. In those days, most jobs required work on Shabbos. Many bosses had a rule: "If you don't come in on Saturday, don't come in on Monday." Some Jews gave in, and many of their children drifted away from the Torah.

Zeidy’s zeidy did not give in. Every Friday he lost his job, and every Sunday he went looking for a new one. "For two years he found a new job almost every week," said Zeidy, "and he never once worked on Shabbos." That is mesirus nefesh, giving things up for the sake of the mitzvos.

Slowly, men like him built shuls, mikvaos and the first yeshivos in America. In 1924, a new law closed the door to most immigrants, and the great wave ended. But the Torah they planted kept growing.

Yoni switched off the recorder. "Zeidy," he said, "I sit at your Shabbos table every week, and I never knew this story."

Gavriel Shirazi had the last story of the unit: World War I. He drew two teams of countries on the newsroom whiteboard.

In 1914, the prince who was next in line to rule Austria-Hungary was visiting the city of Sarajevo. There he was shot and killed. Austria-Hungary blamed its neighbor Serbia and declared war. Then, one by one, other countries joined in, because they had promised to help each other in a war. Within weeks, much of the world was fighting.

On one side were the Allies: Britain, France and Russia. On the other side were the Central Powers: Germany, Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire, which then ruled Eretz Yisroel. In 1917, the United States joined the Allies.

Soldiers dug long ditches called trenches and lived in them for months. Millions of soldiers lost their lives. Jewish soldiers fought on both sides. Many Jews of Eastern Europe were caught between the armies, and whole towns had to flee.

In 1917, Britain made a promise, the Balfour Declaration, to help build a Jewish home in Eretz Yisroel. That same year, British soldiers took Yerushalayim from the Ottomans.

The fighting stopped on November 11, 1918, with an armistice, an agreement to stop fighting. The Allies had won. The Ottoman Empire lost Eretz Yisroel, and after 1918, Britain ruled the land.

Akiva looked at the board. "In four years, the map of the world changed," he said. "And the next chapter of our story begins in that changed world."
--- words
pogroms: violent attacks on Jewish towns
immigrants: people who move to a new country to live there
steerage: the crowded bottom deck of a ship, where the cheapest tickets slept
tenements: crowded apartment buildings with tiny rooms
peddlers: sellers who carry their goods from street to street
trenches: long ditches that soldiers dug to hide and live in
armistice: an agreement to stop fighting
