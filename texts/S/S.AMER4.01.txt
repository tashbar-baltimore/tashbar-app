Avrumi Katz loves maps. When Mr. Halpern hung a giant map of the United States on the classroom wall, Avrumi stared at it. "Fifty states," he said. "How does anyone keep track of them all?"

"We put them in groups," said Mr. Halpern. "Neighbors that are alike go together. Each group is called a region."

A region is an area whose places share something: the same kind of land, the same weather, or the same way of life. In this unit we use five regions for our country: the Northeast, the Southeast, the Midwest, the Southwest and the West. Every one of the fifty states belongs to one of them.

The Northeast is a small region, but it is crowded with big, busy cities like New York and Boston. Many of the oldest towns in America are here, close to the Atlantic Ocean. Winters are cold and snowy, and in the fall the leaves turn red and gold.

The Southeast is warm and wet. Florida reaches far down into the sea, with long beaches and swamps. Farmers in this region grow peanuts, cotton and oranges.
===
The Midwest sits in the middle of the country. Its land is mostly flat, and its rich soil makes it one of the best farming areas in the world. Fields of corn and wheat stretch for miles. The five Great Lakes, giant lakes of fresh water, touch many of the Midwest states.

The Southwest is hot and dry. Much of it is desert, where a cactus can store water in its thick stem for months. Arizona, New Mexico and Texas are in this region. The Grand Canyon, a canyon more than a mile deep, is in Arizona.

The West is the biggest region of all. The Rocky Mountains run through it like a giant backbone. Its states stretch all the way to the Pacific Ocean, and California is on the Pacific coast. Alaska, far to the north, and Hawaii, a chain of islands in the middle of the ocean, belong to the West too.
===
To find one exact spot, mapmakers draw a grid of lines on the map.

Lines of latitude run across the map from east to west, like the rungs of a ladder. They tell how far north or south of the equator a place is. The equator is the line of latitude at 0 degrees, and it circles the middle of the Earth. The North Pole is at 90 degrees north. A place with a small latitude is close to the equator, and it is usually hot. A place with a big latitude is close to a pole, and it is usually icy.

Lines of longitude run up and down the map, from the North Pole to the South Pole. They tell how far east or west a place is. The line at 0 degrees longitude is called the Prime Meridian. It passes through Greenwich, in London, England.

"So where are we?" asked Avrumi. Mr. Halpern put his finger on Baltimore. "About 39 degrees north and 76 degrees west," he said. With just those two numbers, anyone in the world can find our city.

By the end of the day, Avrumi had his own map, with five colors for five regions and a neat grid of lines on top.
--- words
region: a big area whose places are alike in their land, weather or way of life
desert: a very dry place that gets almost no rain
canyon: a deep valley with steep rock walls, cut by a river
grid: lines that cross each other to make boxes, like a checkerboard
latitude: lines that run east to west and tell how far north or south a place is
equator: the line at 0 degrees latitude that circles the middle of the Earth
longitude: lines that run from pole to pole and tell how far east or west a place is
