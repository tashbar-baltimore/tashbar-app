Akiva Feldman pinned a map of Europe to the newsroom wall. "Gavriel, mark every country that sent its Jews away," he said. "Use a red pin, and write the year."

Gavriel Shirazi worked for a long time. When he stepped back, the map was full of red.

When a country forces a whole group of people to leave, it is called an expulsion. In the Middle Ages, the Jews were expelled from one land after another. The first was England, in 1290. France expelled its Jews in 1306. Some were later allowed back, and then they were expelled again.

The most famous expulsion was from Spain. Jews had lived in Spain for over a thousand years. They had great yeshivos, and rabbanim and poets whose seforim we still learn. But in 1492, the king and queen of Spain signed an order: every Jew must leave. The Jews were given about four months. Families had to sell their homes for almost nothing. They could take only what they could carry, and they were not allowed to take gold or silver. The last of them left close to Tisha B'Av. In 1497, Portugal, the land next door, forced its Jews out too.

"Four countries, four red pins," said Gavriel. "England, France, Spain and Portugal."

"And every pin is a family that had to pack," said Akiva.
===
Where could they go? Many of the refugees, the people fleeing, went to Italy, to North Africa, to Holland, and east to Poland. The Jews who came from Spain, and their children after them, are called Sefardim, from Sefarad, the Hebrew name for Spain.

One of the greatest helpers of the refugees was a woman named Doña Gracia Nasi. She was born in Portugal, to a family that had to keep its Judaism secret. She grew up to be very rich, running a great trading business, with ships that sailed between many ports.

Doña Gracia used her money and her ships to help Jews escape to safe lands. Her helpers hid families on her ships and moved them, port by port, out of danger. Many of them reached the Ottoman Empire, in today's Turkey. Its ruler, the sultan, let the Jews in, and great communities grew in cities like Istanbul and Salonika.

Later, Doña Gracia herself moved to the Ottoman Empire and lived openly as a Jew. She supported yeshivos, shuls and talmidei chachamim. With her nephew, Don Yosef Nasi, she even helped rebuild the town of Teverya in Eretz Yisroel, so Jews could live and learn there.

Benny read Gavriel's notes. "So the expulsions were not the end," he said.

"No," said Akiva. "Every time, Jews built again somewhere else. A kehilla is not a building. It is the people, and they take the Torah with them."
===
The last red pins pointed east, to Poland and Lithuania. For hundreds of years, these two lands had some of the largest Jewish communities in the world. Yoni Kaplan found his Zeidy's old town on Gavriel's map, a small dot not far from Lublin.

The Jews of Poland ran much of their own life. For almost two hundred years, a council of rabbanim and leaders met at the great fairs of Lublin, in Poland, and other towns. It was called the Council of Four Lands. It set the taxes each community paid, settled arguments between towns, and decided which seforim could be printed. It met until 1764.

Those years were not all peaceful. In 1648 and 1649, Cossack armies attacked and destroyed many Jewish communities. Jews remember those terrible years as Tach v'Tat.

Yet Torah grew stronger. The Baal Shem Tov, born in 1698, began the Chassidic movement. He taught that every Jew, even the simplest, can serve Hashem with joy. In Lithuania, the Vilna Gaon, born in 1720, became one of the greatest Torah scholars of all time. His talmid, Rav Chaim, opened the famous yeshiva of Volozhin, in Lithuania, and later great yeshivos also grew in Lithuanian towns like Kovno.

Girls needed Torah too. By the early 1900s, many Jewish girls knew little of their own Torah and were drifting away. In Krakow, Poland, a seamstress named Sarah Schenirer saw the danger. In 1917 she opened the first Bais Yaakov school, in a small room with a small group of girls. Today Bais Yaakov schools teach many thousands of girls around the world.
