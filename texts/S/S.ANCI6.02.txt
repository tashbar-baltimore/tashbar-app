title: Four Kingdoms
grade: 6

Akiva Feldman pinned four empty cards in a row on the newsroom wall. "Our sages teach that four kingdoms ruled over the Jewish people, one after another," he said. "Bavel, Persia, Greece and Rome. We will write one card for each."

Yoni Kaplan took the first two cards. He had asked his rebbe about them the night before.

The first kingdom was Bavel. It stood in the land between two great rivers, the Tigris and the Euphrates, in the country we call Iraq today. Its most powerful king was Nevuchadnetzar. His army conquered Yerushalayim and destroyed the first Beis Hamikdash. Then the Jews were taken away to Bavel. This was the first galus. Galus means being forced to live away from your own land.

But the Jews did not forget who they were. In Bavel, far from home, they kept learning Torah and davening. The navi Yirmiyahu had promised that after seventy years the Jews would come home, and they held on to that promise. "That is the first lesson of galus," Yoni wrote. "The Torah goes with us wherever we are sent."

Then a new power rose. Persia, in the land that is Iran today, conquered Bavel. A king of Persia named Koresh let the Jews return to Eretz Yisroel and rebuild the Beis Hamikdash, and many went back to Yerushalayim. Later, the story of Purim happened in Persia, in the days of King Achashveirosh.
===
Eli Mizrahi took the third card.

After Persia came Greece. A young Greek king, Alexander the Great, built a huge empire that stretched from Greece to Egypt and far past Persia. Eretz Yisroel came under Greek rule. The Greeks loved beauty, sports and wisdom. In some Greek cities, the citizens voted on their own laws. That idea is called democracy: rule by the citizens' votes. The Greeks also spread their language, their statues and their games, and some Jews began to copy Greek ways and leave the Torah behind.

Years later, a Greek king named Antiochus wanted every nation in his kingdom to live like Greeks. He banned keeping Shabbos, bris milah and learning Torah. Jews who kept the mitzvos were in great danger.

In the town of Modi'in, a Kohen named Mattisyahu and his sons refused to give up the Torah. This family of Kohanim is called the Chashmonaim. They fought back, led by Mattisyahu's son Yehuda HaMaccabi. They were few and weak, and the Greek army was huge and strong, yet with Hashem's help the Chashmonaim won.

They came to the Beis Hamikdash, cleaned it and rededicated it to the service of Hashem. They found just one small jar of pure oil, enough for one day, but it burned for eight days. That is why we light the menorah for the eight nights of Chanukah.
===
Gavriel Shirazi took the last card.

The fourth kingdom was Rome. From the city of Rome in Italy, it grew into a giant empire. The Romans spoke Latin, and they built strong stone roads that tied their lands together. Their roads, bridges and arches spread across the empire, and some of them are still used today. In time, Rome took over Eretz Yisroel too.

Rome ruled harshly. At last the Roman general Titus surrounded Yerushalayim, and his army destroyed the second Beis Hamikdash. The destruction of the Beis Hamikdash is called the Churban. Many Jews were killed, and many were sent away into a long galus that has not yet ended.

But part of the western wall around the Har Habayis, the mountain where the Beis Hamikdash stood, still stands today. We call it the Kosel. Jews from all over the world come to daven there every day. Every year on Tisha B'Av, we fast and mourn the Churban of both Batei Mikdash.

Akiva stepped back and read the four cards aloud, in order: Bavel, Persia, Greece, Rome. "Rome is the last of the four," he said, "and we are still waiting for that galus to end."

Mr. Gross nodded. "Every one of those mighty empires is gone," he said. "But the Jewish people, with the Torah, are still here."
--- words
conquered: defeated and took over
galus: being forced to live away from your own land
democracy: rule by the votes of the citizens
banned: did not allow; made against the law
rededicated: made holy and ready for its service again
harshly: in a hard, cruel way
mourn: to be sad over a great loss
