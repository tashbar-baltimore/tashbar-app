The last chapter of the unit was about the country right outside the yeshiva's door. Mr. Gross took The Beacon on a trip to Washington, D.C., to see the Capitol, the building where Congress meets.

"Every law of the United States starts in this building," said the guide, as the boys looked up at the great white dome.

Congress, the part of the government that makes the laws, has two parts, called houses. The House of Representatives has 435 members. A state with many people sends many representatives, and a state with few people sends only a few. The Senate is smaller. Every state, big or small, sends exactly two senators, so there are 100 senators in all.

A law begins as a bill. A bill is a plan for a new law, written down, that no one has to follow yet. A member of Congress brings the bill. First a small group called a committee studies it and may change it. Then the whole house votes. If more than half vote yes, the bill goes to the other house, which studies it and votes too. A bill must pass both the House and the Senate in the very same words.

Then the bill goes to the President. If he agrees with it, he signs it, and it becomes a law. If he does not agree, he can veto it, which means he refuses to sign it and sends it back.

A veto does not have to be the end. Congress can vote again. If two-thirds of the House and two-thirds of the Senate vote yes, the veto is overridden, and the bill becomes a law without the President's signature. Two-thirds is much harder to reach than half, so most vetoes stand.

"Why make it so hard?" asked Eli.

"So that no one person, and no one house, can make laws alone," said Mr. Gross. "Each part checks the others."

On the bus home, Yoni Kaplan held out his recorder and asked Mr. Gross about citizens.

A citizen is a member of a country. A person born in the United States is a citizen, and a person who moves here can become one by learning about the country and passing a test. Citizens have rights, freedoms that the government must protect: to practice their religion, to speak freely and to have a fair trial.

Citizens also have duties, things they must do. They must obey the law and pay their taxes. And when a court calls them, they must serve on a jury, a group of citizens who listen to a court case and decide it. In America, a jury of ordinary people, not one powerful man, decides whether a person accused of a crime is guilty.

One of the most important rights is voting. Every American citizen may vote from the age of 18. Citizens vote for the President, for members of Congress, and for the leaders of their state and their city. Voting is how citizens choose the people who make the laws.

"There is a teaching about all this in our own learning," said Mr. Gross. "Akiva, you learned it this week."

Akiva nodded. "Dina d'malchusa dina," he said. "It comes from the Gemara. It means that the law of the land is the law." A Jew must keep the fair laws of the country he lives in, pay its taxes honestly, and deal honestly with its government.

Many Jews in America go out to vote, and they thank Hashem for a country where they can learn Torah and keep mitzvos freely. "A country that lets us live as Jews," Yoni's Zeidy likes to say, "deserves good citizens."

Two weeks before Sukkos, Gavriel Shirazi went with his Zeidy to buy an esrog. The hall was packed. Long tables were covered with small boxes, and men held esrogim up to the light, turning them slowly to check every side.

Gavriel noticed the prices. A beautiful esrog cost much more than it would have cost a month before. "Why?" he asked.

"Supply and demand," said his Zeidy. Supply is how much there is to sell. Demand is how much people want to buy.

In the days before Sukkos, nearly every Jewish family wants an esrog, all at the same time. Demand is very high. The supply cannot grow to match it. An esrog takes many months to grow on its tree, so no grower can bring more esrogim in a week. When many buyers want something and the supply stays the same, the price goes up.

Gavriel's Zeidy told him what happens next. The day after Sukkos, almost no one needs an esrog. Demand drops to almost nothing, but the sellers still have boxes of them. To sell them at all, they lower the price. Some leftover esrogim are sold for a tiny part of what they cost the week before, and are cooked into jam.

Supply can change too. If a storm ruins many esrog trees, there is less to sell, and the price rises. If the trees give a huge harvest, there is more to sell than people want, and the price falls.

Back in the newsroom, Gavriel drew a seesaw with "supply" on one side and "demand" on the other. "In a free market," he wrote under it, "no one orders the price. Buyers and sellers set it together, through supply and demand."

veto: the President's refusal to sign a bill
overridden: beaten by a stronger vote
jury: a group of citizens who listen to a court case and decide it
supply: how much of something there is to sell
