Mr. Gross spread a large blank map across the newsroom table. "Our next issue of The Beacon is about the ancient empires," he said. "Bavel, Persia, Greece and Rome. But before we meet the empires, we need to know the land they all wanted."

Gavriel Shirazi picked up a pencil. He is the quiet one in the newsroom, and he draws every map the paper prints. He began with Eretz Yisroel, a narrow land, much smaller than most of its neighbors.

"Look where it sits," said Mr. Gross. Three continents meet near Eretz Yisroel: Asia, Africa and Europe. In ancient times, traders and armies traveling between them had to pass through this small land. That made Eretz Yisroel a crossroads, a place where many roads meet. Whoever ruled it could control the roads between three continents, and that is one reason so many great empires fought to take it.

Gavriel drew the coast first. On the west side of Eretz Yisroel is the Mediterranean Sea, a great sea that stretches all the way to Greece, Italy and Spain. Then he added the neighbors. To the north is Lebanon, a land of tall mountains and cedar trees. To the northeast is Syria. To the east is the country of Jordan. To the southwest is Egypt, the land the Torah calls Mitzrayim.

"North is always at the top of the map," Gavriel reminded Yoni Kaplan, who had turned the paper sideways.
===
Next came the water. Water decides where people can live, so Gavriel drew it carefully in blue.

In the north of Eretz Yisroel lies the Kinneret, a lake of fresh water shaped a little like a harp. Fishermen have worked its waters since ancient times. Out of its southern end flows the Jordan River. The Jordan winds south through a long, deep valley, and it ends in the Dead Sea.

Water flows into the Dead Sea, but no river flows out of it. The hot sun dries the water away and leaves the salt behind. Over a very long time, the sea has become so salty that people float on top of it like corks, and no fish can live in it. In lashon hakodesh it is called the Yam HaMelach, the Sea of Salt. Its shore is the lowest place on land on earth.

Eli Mizrahi, who likes to argue every side, frowned at the map. "A river that flows south? Rivers flow down, and south is down."

"South is down on the paper," said Akiva Feldman, the editor, "not down on the ground." Akiva is strict about facts. "Rivers flow downhill. The Jordan flows south because the Dead Sea is far lower than the Kinneret."

Gavriel colored the Kinneret light blue for fresh water and the Dead Sea white for salt. Last, he drew a red dot for Yerushalayim, in the hills just west of the Dead Sea.
===
Then Gavriel moved his pencil southwest, to Mitzrayim. Our people lived there before Yetzias Mitzrayim, so the boys wanted to know this land well. Mitzrayim is in Africa.

Most of Mitzrayim is desert, and almost no rain falls there. But one great river runs through it: the Nile, one of the longest rivers in the world. The Nile flows north through the desert until it empties into the Mediterranean Sea.

Every year, the Nile flooded. When the water went down, it left a layer of rich black mud on the fields. Farmers planted their seeds in that mud, and wheat grew in the middle of the desert. Away from the river, the land was dry sand where nothing grew. So most people in Mitzrayim lived on a narrow green strip along the Nile.

The kings of Mitzrayim were called Pharaohs. Some Pharaohs had giant pyramids of stone built as their tombs, the places where they would be buried. Many pyramids still stand today. The people of Mitzrayim also had their own writing, called hieroglyphics. Instead of letters, it used small picture-signs. They carved hieroglyphics on stone walls and wrote them on papyrus, a kind of paper made from a plant that grew by the Nile. Much later, a stone carved with the same words in three kinds of writing, the Rosetta Stone, helped people learn to read hieroglyphics.

Bnei Yisroel were slaves in Mitzrayim until Hashem took them out at Yetzias Mitzrayim.

Benny Okafor-Levine studied the finished map. "Every place on here has a story," he said. "And in this whole unit, the stories keep coming back to this one small land in the middle."
--- words
continents: the seven great areas of land on earth
crossroads: a place where many roads meet
northeast: the direction halfway between north and east
flooded: rose over its banks and covered the land
tombs: places where people are buried
hieroglyphics: the writing of ancient Mitzrayim, made of picture-signs
papyrus: a kind of paper made from a plant that grew by the Nile
