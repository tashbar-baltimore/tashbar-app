title: The Ground Is Moving
grade: 6

Yoni Kaplan's uncle in Yerushalayim sent him a strange photo: a crack running across an old stone wall. "An earthquake did this, a long time ago," he wrote. Yoni brought the photo to the Beacon newsroom. "How can solid ground just break?" he asked.

The answer is that the ground is not one solid piece. The earth's crust is broken into giant slabs called plates. They fit together like the pieces of a puzzle, and they float on the hot, slowly flowing rock of the mantle below. The plates are always moving, but very slowly: usually 2 to 10 centimeters a year. That is about as fast as your fingernails grow. A moving plate is slower than a growing blade of grass, slower than a crawling snail, and much slower than a boy walking to shul.
===
Where two plates meet, the edge is called a boundary. Plates meet in three ways.

Some plates push into each other. The crust crumples and folds upward, building tall mountains. The Himalayas, the highest mountains on earth, are still being pushed up today by two plates pressing together.

Some plates pull apart. In the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, two plates are slowly moving away from each other. As the gap opens, magma rises up from below, fills the gap and hardens into new crust. Iceland sits right on top of this gap. It is pulling apart by about 2 centimeters every year.

Some plates slide past each other. The San Andreas Fault in California is a boundary like this. Eretz Yisroel has one too. The long valley of the Yarden and the Dead Sea lies along a boundary where two plates are slowly sliding past each other.
===
Plates do not always slide smoothly. Their rough edges can get stuck, while the plates keep pushing. Stress builds up in the rock, the way a bent stick stores more and more force. Then, suddenly, the rocks slip. The stored energy rushes out as seismic waves, which spread out in all directions and shake the ground, sometimes hundreds of kilometers away. That shaking is an earthquake. In 1927, an earthquake along the Yarden valley damaged buildings in Yerushalayim, maybe even the wall in Yoni's photo.

Scientists record earthquakes with a machine called a seismometer. They rate each earthquake on a magnitude scale. Each step up means about 10 times more shaking. A magnitude 6 shakes about 10 times more than a 5. A magnitude 6 shakes about 100 times more than a 4, because that is two steps of 10: 10 times 10.

When an earthquake happens under the ocean floor, it can lift or drop a huge block of sea bottom all at once. All the water above it is pushed up too. The water races outward in giant waves called a tsunami. Out at sea, a tsunami can pass under a ship almost unnoticed. But when it reaches shallow water near the shore, it slows down and piles up into a wall of water that floods the land far inland. Countries near the edges of the Pacific Ocean have warning systems that measure undersea earthquakes and tell people living by the shore to move to high ground.

Engineers also design buildings that can ride out the shaking. In Japan, where earthquakes are common, tall towers stand on giant rubber pads, or have heavy weights inside that swing against the sway. Instead of standing stiff and cracking, the buildings bend a little and stay standing.
===
How do scientists know the plates are moving right now, and not just long ago? They use GPS, the same satellites that guide cars. First they bolt a GPS station to solid rock. They record its exact spot this year. They record its spot again next year. Then they compare the two spots. The station has moved, a few centimeters, carried along on its plate.

Earthquakes cannot be stopped, but people can stay safe. If the ground starts shaking while you are indoors, drop to your hands and knees, take cover under a sturdy table, and hold on until the shaking stops. Stay away from windows. If you are outside, move to an open spot, away from buildings, walls, trees and power lines. When the shaking stops, check around you for damage and listen to the grown-ups in charge.

Yoni wrote his article that night. He called it "Our Moving Ground." The last line said: "The ground under Yerushalayim has moved a little every year since the time of Dovid HaMelech, and it is moving still."
--- words
plates: giant slabs of the earth's crust that slowly move
boundary: a line where two areas meet
crumples: bends and folds into wrinkles
stress: a pushing or pulling force that builds up inside something
seismometer: a machine that measures and records an earthquake's shaking
magnitude: a number that tells how strong an earthquake is
sturdy: strong and hard to break
