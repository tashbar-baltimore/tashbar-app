Mr. Gross wrote one number on the newsroom whiteboard: 1939. "This unit of The Beacon covers the century your grandparents lived through," he said. "It begins with the biggest war the world has ever seen."

Benny Okafor-Levine took the first story. He spread a map of Europe across the newsroom table and began.

Only twenty-one years after World War I ended, the world was at war again. In September 1939, Germany invaded Poland. Its army rolled across the border with tanks and airplanes, and within weeks Poland had fallen. Two days after the invasion began, Britain and France declared war on Germany. World War II had begun.

The countries that fought were split into two sides. On one side were the Axis powers: Germany, Italy and Japan. On the other side were the Allies. The leading Allies were Britain, the United States and the Soviet Union, with many smaller countries beside them.

At first, the Axis seemed impossible to stop. By the summer of 1940, the German army had taken much of Europe, including France. Britain stood almost alone. Night after night, German planes bombed London, and families slept in shelters under the ground. The British would not give up.

In 1941, the war spread across the whole world. In June, Germany attacked the Soviet Union. Then, on the morning of December 7, 1941, Japanese planes made a surprise attack on Pearl Harbor, an American navy base in Hawaii. Many ships were sunk, and more than two thousand Americans were killed. The next day, the United States declared war. Until then, America had stayed out of the fighting. Now it joined the Allies.

"So Pearl Harbor did not start the war," said Eli Mizrahi. "It brought America into it."

"Exactly," said Benny. "Europe had already been at war for more than two years."
===
Gavriel Shirazi wrote the next story, about the day the tide turned. He pinned a photo of a wide, empty beach to the board.

America built ships, planes and tanks faster than any country ever had. Millions of young Americans became soldiers. Factories that had made cars now made airplanes, and families at home saved metal and rubber to help. In the Pacific Ocean, American ships and planes fought Japan island by island. Slowly, the Allies began to push the Axis back.

The Allies planned a huge invasion to free Western Europe. On June 6, 1944, a day known as D-Day, thousands of ships crossed the English Channel. More than 150,000 Allied soldiers landed on the beaches of Normandy, a region on the northern coast of France. It was the largest landing from the sea in history. Many soldiers fell on those beaches, but by nightfall the Allies held the shore.

D-Day did not end the war. The Allies still had to fight their way across France and into Germany, while the Soviet army pushed in from the east. In May 1945, Germany surrendered, and the war in Europe was over. In August 1945, Japan surrendered too. World War II ended in 1945, and the Allies had won.

The war cost more lives than any war before it. Among them were six million Jews, murdered by the Nazis, who ruled Germany. We call them the kedoshim, the holy ones, and we never forget them.

Gavriel looked at his photo for a long time. "Those soldiers were not much older than our brothers," he said quietly.
===
Akiva Feldman, the editor, took the last story of this chapter. "The war ended," he wrote, "but the peace did not last long."

After 1945, the two strongest countries in the world were the United States and the Soviet Union. They had fought on the same side against Germany, but they did not trust each other. The United States had free elections and free speech. The Soviet Union was a communist country: the government owned the farms and factories, ran every newspaper, and punished people who spoke against it.

From about 1947 to 1991, these two rivals struggled for power around the world. This long struggle is called the Cold War. It was called "cold" because the two sides never fought each other directly in a real war. Instead, they built huge piles of weapons, raced each other into space, and each tried to win other countries to its side.

The city of Berlin, in Germany, showed the Cold War on a single street. After World War II, the city was split in two. In 1961, the communist side built a wall of concrete and barbed wire right through the middle of Berlin, so that its people could not escape to the free side. Families were cut apart for years.

Then, in 1989, crowds gathered at the wall, and the guards opened the gates. People climbed on top of the Berlin Wall and began to break it apart with hammers. Two years later, in 1991, the Soviet Union itself broke up into separate countries. The Cold War was over.

"From 1939 to 1991," said Mr. Gross, looking at the dates on the board. "One hot war, and then a long cold one. Next, we find out what those years meant for our own people."
--- words
invaded: entered another country with an army to take it over
Allies: the side of Britain, the United States and the Soviet Union in World War II
surrendered: gave up and stopped fighting
kedoshim: holy ones; Jews who were killed because they were Jews
communist: run by a government that owns the farms and factories and controls what people may say
rivals: two sides that compete against each other
