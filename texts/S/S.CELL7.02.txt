The yeshiva's science room has a new microscope, and Gavriel Shirazi was the first to sign up. Mr. Gross set it on the table. "Before you look at anything," he said, "you need to know your tool."

A microscope has a few main parts. At the top is the eyepiece, the lens you look into. Below it, on a turning wheel, are the objective lenses: usually a short one, a medium one and a long one. The short lens makes things look a little bigger, and the long lens makes them look much bigger. Under the lenses is the stage, a flat platform where you lay the glass slide. Two metal stage clips hold the slide still. Underneath the stage, a small light shines up through the slide, so you can see right through a thin sample. On the side are two focus knobs: a big one that moves a lot, and a small one for fine, sharp focus.
===
How much bigger does a microscope make things? Each lens magnifies, or makes things look bigger, by a certain amount. That number is printed on the lens with an "×" after it. To find the total magnification, multiply the eyepiece number by the objective lens number.

Gavriel's eyepiece says 15×. His smallest objective lens says 4×. Fifteen times four is 60, so with that lens the sample looks 60 times bigger. With the 10× lens it looks 15 times 10, or 150 times bigger. With the long 40× lens it looks 15 times 40, or 600 times bigger. At 600 times, a single cell can look as big as a coin.

Notice that you multiply, not add. A 10× eyepiece and a 40× lens do not make 50 times. Each lens enlarges what the other lens already enlarged.
===
Mr. Gross showed the boys how to make a slide. Eli Mizrahi peeled a thin, clear skin from a piece of onion. He laid it flat on a glass slide, added one drop of water, and gently lowered a thin square of glass called a cover slip on top. A good sample is thin and flat, so light can pass through it. A thick chunk of potato would just look dark.

Then comes the most important rule: always start with the lowest-power lens. At low power you see a wide area, so it is easy to find your sample. Place the slide on the stage and clip it. Click the shortest lens into place. Turn the big focus knob until the picture is clear. Only then turn to a higher lens, and use the small knob to sharpen it. If everything turns into a gray blur, do not turn up the light. Go back to low power, find your sample again, and focus.

A microscope is a delicate tool, so the boys learned to care for it too. Carry it with two hands: one on the arm and one under the base. Clean the lenses only with soft lens paper, never with a shirt or a tissue, which can scratch the glass. And keep a lab notebook. Each time you look at a sample, draw what you see, label the parts you can name, and write the total magnification next to your drawing. A scientist who does not write things down soon forgets what he saw.
===
Gavriel bent over the eyepiece. At 150 times, the onion skin turned into rows of long boxes, packed side by side, each with a dark round spot inside. "Hooke's little rooms," he said quietly. "Living ones."

Long ago, a Dutch shopkeeper named Antonie van Leeuwenhoek made tiny, powerful lenses of his own. In a drop of pond water, he saw creatures swimming that no one had ever seen before. He called them "little animals." Today we know they were one-celled living things. A whole world had been in every drop of water all along. It only took the right tool to see it.
--- words
eyepiece: the lens at the top of a microscope that you look into
objective: one of the lenses near the slide that makes the sample look bigger
stage: the flat platform of a microscope where the slide lies
magnifies: makes something look bigger than it really is
magnification: how many times bigger something looks
sample: a small piece of something, used for looking or testing
