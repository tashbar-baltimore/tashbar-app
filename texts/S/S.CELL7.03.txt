title: A Cell Is a Busy City
grade: 7
kind: article
topic: Cells
source: original
---
Benny Okafor-Levine had a problem. He had to explain cells to the readers of The Beacon, and most of the names sounded like a foreign language. Then he had an idea. "A cell," he wrote, "is like a busy city. Every part has a job, and the city only works when every part does its job."

Every city has a border with gates. A cell has a cell membrane: a thin, soft layer around the whole cell. The membrane is the gate. It lets in what the cell needs, like water, food and oxygen, and it lets out waste. It also keeps harmful things out. Both plant cells and animal cells have a cell membrane.

Inside the membrane is the cytoplasm, a watery jelly that fills the cell. All the other parts float in it, the way buildings sit on the land of a city. Food and materials move through the cytoplasm from one part to another.
===
Every city has a city hall where the plans are kept. A cell has a nucleus. The nucleus is the control center. It holds the cell's instructions, written in a long, coiled material called DNA. These instructions tell the cell what to build and what to do. When a cell splits, it makes a copy of its instructions, so each new cell gets a full set.

Every city needs power. A cell gets its power from the mitochondria (one is called a mitochondrion). They are the cell's power plants. Food, like a bit of sugar, passes in through the cell membrane and floats through the cytoplasm. A mitochondrion breaks the sugar down and releases its energy. The cell then uses that energy to move, grow and do its work. Busy cells, like muscle cells, have many mitochondria.
===
Plant cells have three extra parts that animal cells do not have.

The first is the cell wall, a stiff box outside the membrane. It gives a plant cell its neat, boxy shape and helps a plant stand tall without bones. An animal cell has no stiff wall, so its shape is round or uneven.

The second is the chloroplasts, small green parts that make food. Inside them, the plant uses sunlight, water and carbon dioxide from the air to make sugar. This is called photosynthesis. Chloroplasts are what make leaves green. If you see a cell with green dots and a stiff, boxy edge, it is a plant cell.

The third is a big vacuole, a large sac of water in the middle of the cell. When the vacuoles are full, they push out against the cell walls, and the plant stands firm and crisp.

In a big living thing, cells do not work alone. Cells of the same kind, doing the same job, work together in a team called a tissue. Muscle tissue moves, and nerve tissue carries messages. Different tissues work together to make an organ, like the heart, the stomach or a leaf. And organs work together in systems, like the system that carries blood around the body. Cell, tissue, organ, system: each level is built from the one before it.
===
Yehoshua, Benny's little brother, left a stalk of celery on the counter for two days. It went soft and droopy. Benny used it for his article. The celery's cells had lost water, and their vacuoles had shrunk. Then he stood the stalk in a glass of cold water. Water moved into the cells, each vacuole filled up again, and by the next morning the stalk stood crisp and straight.

Benny ended his article with a list for readers to remember. Membrane: the gate. Cytoplasm: the jelly. Nucleus: the control center. Mitochondria: the power plants. And for plants only: the cell wall, the chloroplasts and the big vacuole. "Trillions of tiny cities," he wrote, "running all day inside every living thing."
--- words
membrane: a thin, soft layer that lets some things through
cytoplasm: the watery jelly that fills a cell
nucleus: the control center of a cell, which holds its instructions
mitochondria: the parts of a cell that release energy from food
chloroplasts: the green parts of a plant cell that make food from sunlight
vacuole: a sac inside a cell that stores water
photosynthesis: how plants make sugar from sunlight, water and air
