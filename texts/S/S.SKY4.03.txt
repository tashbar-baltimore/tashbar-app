On Rosh Chodesh, Avrumi Katz looked for the new moon. All he could see in the evening sky was a thin, silver curve, like a fingernail. A week later the moon was half a circle. A week after that, it was a full, round moon. "Does the moon really change its shape?" Avrumi asked his Abba.

The answer is no. The moon is always a round ball of rock. It does not make any light of its own. The moonlight we see is really sunlight bouncing off the moon. The sun always lights up one half of the moon, the half that faces the sun. As the moon travels around the Earth, we see different amounts of that lit half. These changing shapes are called the phases of the moon.

When the moon is between the Earth and the sun, its lit half faces away from us, and we see almost nothing. Then a thin sliver appears, and it grows a little bigger each night. When the Earth is between the sun and the moon, we see the whole lit half. That is a full moon. Then the moon shrinks back again.
===
One trip through all the phases takes about 29 and a half days. That is why our Jewish months are 29 or 30 days long. Each month starts with the new moon, and the full moon comes in the middle of the month. That is why Pesach and Sukkos, which start on the fifteenth of the month, always have a bright full moon.

The moon has no air and no water. Its ground is covered with gray dust and bowl-shaped holes called craters. The craters were made by rocks from space that crashed into the moon long ago. With no wind or rain to wear them away, the craters stay sharp for millions of years.

People have walked on the moon. In 1969, astronauts landed there for the first time. The moon's pull, called gravity, is much weaker than the Earth's. On the moon, you would weigh only about one sixth of what you weigh at home, so the astronauts bounced as they walked. Their footprints are still there today, because no wind blows them away.
===
The moon is small, but it pulls on the Earth. Every object pulls on every other object. This pull is called gravity. The moon's gravity pulls on the oceans and makes the water swell into a bulge on the side of the Earth that faces the moon. There is a second bulge on the opposite side of the Earth.

The Earth spins through both bulges every day. When a beach passes through a bulge, the water rises up the sand. That is high tide. Between the bulges, the water goes back down. That is low tide. Because there are two bulges, most beaches get two high tides and two low tides each day. A high tide comes about every twelve and a half hours.

The sun pulls on the oceans too, but more weakly. At new moon and full moon, the sun and moon pull in a straight line, and the tides are extra high and extra low. Avrumi smiled. The thin silver moon over his house was pulling on oceans thousands of miles away.
--- words
phases: the different shapes the moon seems to have during a month
sliver: a thin, narrow piece
craters: bowl-shaped holes in the ground made when something crashed into it
gravity: the pull that every object has on every other object
bulge: a part that swells out, like a bump
opposite: on the far side, facing the other way
