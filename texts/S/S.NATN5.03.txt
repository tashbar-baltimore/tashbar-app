Nosson Abergel and Tzvi Hirsch Gold were building a timeline for the classroom wall. "The Revolution was more than generals and battles," said Mr. Halpern. "Jews helped too. Let me tell you about a few of them."

Haym Salomon was born in Poland. He traveled across Europe, learned many languages, and came to New York in the 1770s. He loved the idea of a free country, and he joined the patriots, the Americans who wanted freedom from Britain.

In 1776, the British army held New York. They arrested Salomon and said that he was a spy. Because he spoke German, they put him to work talking with the German soldiers who fought for Britain. Two years later he was arrested again and sentenced to death. He escaped and made his way to Philadelphia.

There he became a broker, a man who helps others borrow, lend and trade money. The new American government was very short of money, and its soldiers needed food, clothes and pay. Salomon helped raise the money, and he lent his own money to leaders of the new country. He also gave generously to Mikveh Israel, the shul of the Jews of Philadelphia. When he died in 1785, he was poor, after giving so much.
===
Francis Salvador was a Jew from London who came to South Carolina in 1773 to farm land his family owned. His neighbors came to trust him so much that they elected him to their colony's new government. He was the first Jew elected to a colonial government in America.

In 1776, Salvador rode out with the soldiers to defend his neighbors. He was killed in the fighting that summer. He was the first Jew to die for American independence.

Other Jews served as soldiers and officers, and Jewish families gave money, food and supplies. There were only a few thousand Jews in all the colonies, but many of them stood up for America's freedom.

"Hakaras hatov," said Tzvi Hirsch. "We should thank them." Mr. Halpern agreed. Hakaras hatov means recognizing the good that someone did for you, and saying thank you.
===
The next builder on the timeline was Rebecca Gratz. She was born in Philadelphia in 1781 and lived there all her life, until she passed away in 1869. Chessed, kindness to others, was the center of her life.

In 1815, she helped start a home for orphans, children whose parents had died. In 1819, she helped start a society of Jewish women that brought food, fuel and help to poor Jewish families.

Rebecca Gratz saw that many Jewish children in Philadelphia had no place to learn about their own religion. So in 1838 she started the Hebrew Sunday School. It was the first Jewish Sunday school in America. Its teachers were volunteers who were not paid, and the children learned about the Torah and the history of their people. Soon other cities started schools like it.

In 1855, when she was in her seventies, she helped open a Jewish foster home, a place that cared for children who had no one to raise them.

Nosson pinned up the last card. "A man who gave money, a man who gave his life, and a woman who gave her kindness," he said. "Each one gave something different."

"That is what builds a nation," said Mr. Halpern.
--- words
patriots: Americans who wanted freedom from Britain
spy: someone who secretly gathers news for the other side
broker: a person who helps others borrow, lend and trade money
elected: chosen by people's votes
orphans: children whose parents have died
volunteers: people who help without being paid
society: a group of people who work together for a goal
