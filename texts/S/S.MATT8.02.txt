Benny Okafor-Levine brought a strange purple liquid to the Beacon newsroom in a glass jar. "Red cabbage juice," he announced. "Watch." He poured a little into three clear cups. Into the first cup he squeezed some lemon. The purple turned bright pink. Into the second he stirred a spoonful of baking soda. The purple turned blue-green. The third cup, with plain water, stayed purple.

"It changes color like a mood ring," said Yoni Kaplan.

"Better," said Benny. "It tells you whether something is an acid or a base."

Acids and bases are two big families of substances. You meet them every day. Acids taste sour. Lemon juice, vinegar, tomatoes and orange juice are all acids. Bases often feel slippery and taste bitter. Soap, baking soda dissolved in water and many cleaning liquids are bases. But scientists never test a substance by tasting or touching it. Some strong acids and bases can burn your skin or eyes. That is why scientists use safe tests instead.
===
To measure how acidic or basic something is, scientists use the pH scale. It runs from 0 to 14.

Right in the middle, at 7, is neutral. Pure water has a pH of 7. It is neither an acid nor a base.

Anything below 7 is an acid. The lower the number, the stronger the acid. Lemon juice has a pH of about 2. Vinegar is about 3. Apple juice is about 4, and tomato juice is about 4 as well. Your stomach makes a strong acid, about pH 1 or 2, to help break down your food.

Anything above 7 is a base. The higher the number, the stronger the base. Baking soda mixed with water has a pH of about 9. A bar of soap is about 10. Some strong cleaning liquids are 12 or 13, which is why they must be kept far from children and never mixed with each other.

Each step on the pH scale is a big jump. A pH of 3 is not just a little more acidic than a pH of 4. It is about ten times more acidic. So lemon juice at pH 2 is about ten times more acidic than vinegar at pH 3.
===
How do you find the pH without a lab full of machines? You use an indicator. An indicator is a substance that changes color when it meets an acid or a base.

Red cabbage juice is a natural indicator. In a strong acid it turns red or pink. In a weak acid it turns pinkish purple. In something neutral it stays purple. In a base it turns blue or green, and in a very strong base it can even turn yellow. Many flowers and fruits work as indicators too, for the same reason.

Scientists also use litmus paper. Blue litmus paper turns red in an acid. Red litmus paper turns blue in a base. For a number, they use pH strips: a strip changes to a color, and you match the color to a chart that gives the pH.

When an acid and a base are mixed, they can cancel each other out. This is called neutralizing. A person with a sour stomach may take an antacid tablet, which is a weak base. It neutralizes some of the extra stomach acid, and the burning feeling fades.

The pH of things matters all around us. Your blood must stay very close to a pH of 7.4, just a little basic, and your body works hard to keep it there. Your teeth care about pH too. When you eat sugary food, germs in your mouth turn the sugar into acid, and the acid slowly wears away the hard coating of your teeth. That is one reason to brush after eating. Gardeners test the pH of their soil. Blueberry bushes grow best in acidic soil. And the hydrangea, a flowering bush, has blue flowers in acidic soil and pink flowers in basic soil, like a giant indicator growing in the garden. Swimming pools are tested every day as well, because water that is too acidic or too basic stings the eyes of the swimmers.
===
Eli Mizrahi tested the drinks from the newsroom lunch with pH strips and lined them up on the table from most acidic to most basic: lemonade, then apple juice, then water, then a cup of soapy water he had made for the test. "Nobody drink the last one," he said.

Mr. Gross added one sentence at the bottom, in bold: "Never taste, touch or mix cleaning liquids. Let the indicator do the tasting for you."
--- words
acid: a substance with a pH below 7, often sour, like lemon juice
base: a substance with a pH above 7, often slippery, like soap
neutral: neither an acid nor a base, with a pH of 7
indicator: a substance that changes color to show acid or base
litmus: a special paper that turns red in an acid and blue in a base
neutralizing: mixing an acid and a base so they cancel each other out
