For the science issue of The Beacon, Akiva Feldman gave Gavriel Shirazi a strange job. "Draw a map of a trip nobody can take," he said. "Straight down, from our school to the center of the earth."

Gavriel started with a hard-boiled egg. He cut it in half and set it on the newsroom table. "The earth is built in layers, like this egg," he said. "The shell is the crust. The white is the mantle. The yolk is the core." It is not a perfect model, but it shows the big idea: a thin outside, a thick middle and a heavy center.

The trip to the center is about 6,400 kilometers. No one has ever dug even close. The deepest hole people have drilled goes down about 12 kilometers, and the drill stopped because the rock was too hot. So how do scientists know what is down there? They listen to earthquakes. When the ground shakes, waves of energy called seismic waves travel through the whole planet. The waves speed up, slow down and bend when they pass through different materials. Some waves cannot pass through liquid at all. By timing the waves at stations all over the world, scientists have mapped the layers they will never see.
===
The first layer is the crust, the ground under our feet. It is the thinnest layer by far. Under the oceans, the crust is only about 5 to 10 kilometers thick. Under the continents, it is usually about 30 to 50 kilometers thick, and thicker under tall mountains. On Gavriel's egg, the crust is the shell: a thin, hard skin around everything else.

Below the crust is the mantle, the thickest layer. It is about 2,900 kilometers thick and is made of hot, heavy rock. The mantle is solid, but it is so hot that over millions of years it flows very slowly, like thick tar. Much of the magma that feeds volcanoes forms in the upper mantle, right under the crust.

So if the crust is about 40 kilometers thick where you stand, and the mantle about 2,900, the top of the core lies about 2,940 kilometers below your feet.

Why are the layers stacked this way? When the young earth was very hot and partly melted, the heaviest materials, like iron and nickel, sank toward the center. Lighter rock floated up toward the top, the way oil floats on water in a jar. So the heaviest layer is at the middle and the lightest is on the outside.

The mantle does not sit still, either. Rock deep in the mantle is heated from below, rises very slowly, cools near the top, and sinks again. This slow churning, like soup simmering in a pot, carries the pieces of the crust along on top of it. It takes millions of years, but it moves whole continents. If you could watch the earth sped up, you would see the land drifting like rafts on a slow river.
===
The core is made mostly of iron and nickel, two heavy metals. It has two parts. The outer core is liquid: a deep ocean of melted metal, about 2,200 kilometers thick, that churns and flows. As it moves, it makes the earth's magnetic field. That field is the reason a compass needle points north.

The inner core is a solid ball of metal at the very center. It is the hottest place inside the earth, about 5,200 degrees Celsius. That is about as hot as the surface of the sun! You might expect metal that hot to melt. But the weight of the whole planet presses down on the inner core so hard that the metal is squeezed into a solid.
===
The deeper you go, the hotter it gets. A hot summer day is about 35 degrees Celsius. Lava pouring out of a volcano is about 1,100 degrees. The outer core is thousands of degrees hotter still, and the inner core is hottest of all.

Gavriel finished his map and hung it on the newsroom wall: crust, mantle, outer core, inner core, with the temperatures written beside each layer. Yoni Kaplan stood in front of it for a long time. "We live on a thin shell," he said, "over a ball of fire."

Mr. Gross, the faculty adviser, smiled. "And the shell holds steady for us every day," he said. "Every morning we thank Hashem who spreads the earth over the waters. When you know what is under the ground, that bracha means even more."
--- words
layers: parts that lie one on top of another
seismic: having to do with earthquakes and the waves they send out
continents: the seven great areas of land on earth
mantle: the thick layer of hot rock between the crust and the core
churns: moves and swirls around strongly
magnetic: able to pull iron, like a magnet
