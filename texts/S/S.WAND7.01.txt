Mr. Gross set a pile of library books on the newsroom table. "This unit of The Beacon covers almost a thousand years," he said. "We begin in Europe, in the time called the Middle Ages."

Benny Okafor-Levine took the first story. He opened a book to a drawing of a stone castle on a hill, with a ditch of water all around it.

In the Middle Ages, much of Europe lived by a system called feudalism. At the top was the king. He owned far more land than he could rule by himself, so he gave large pieces of it to nobles. In return, each noble promised to be loyal and to send fighters whenever the king went to war.

The nobles lived in castles. A castle had thick stone walls, narrow windows and heavy gates. Wars were common, and the walls protected the noble's family and people when an enemy came. Below the nobles were the knights, trained fighters on horseback. At the bottom were the peasants, by far the largest group. The peasants farmed the land, and they gave much of each harvest to the noble who ruled them.

"Land goes down the ladder," said Benny, "and loyalty and work go up."

"That is feudalism in one sentence," said Mr. Gross.

The Jews were not on the ladder at all. In most lands they were not allowed to own farmland, so many became traders. They lived on their own streets in the towns, close to the shul, and their safety depended on the king or the local noble.
===
Yoni Kaplan wrote the next story, and he asked his Zeidy to help. "This part is sad," Zeidy said, "but a Jew needs to know it."

In the year 1096, great armies set out from Europe. They planned to march thousands of miles to fight over the Holy Land, Eretz Yisroel. These armies are called the Crusaders, and their march was the First Crusade.

On the way, the Crusaders passed through the Rhineland, the land along the Rhine River in today's Germany. Old and famous Jewish communities stood there, in the towns of Speyer, Worms and Mainz. They were great centers of Torah learning. The Crusaders attacked the Jews of these towns and killed many of them. In some places the town leaders tried to protect the Jews, but they could not stop the armies.

The Jews were told they could live if they gave up their emunah. Most refused, and they gave their lives rather than leave the Torah. This is called Kiddush Hashem, making the Name of Hashem holy in the eyes of the world. We call the Jews who were killed kedoshim, holy ones.

Our people never forgot them. Every Shabbos, many shuls say the tefillah of Av HaRachamim, which remembers the kedoshim of those days. Kinnos about them are said on Tisha B'Av.

Yoni wrote slowly. When he finished, Akiva Feldman read the story and did not change a word. "It is accurate," he said, "and it is respectful. That is how The Beacon writes about kedoshim."
===
Eli Mizrahi took the happiest story: the Torah itself. "Even in the hardest times," he said, "the learning never stopped."

After the Gemara was completed, the heads of the great yeshivos of Bavel, in Sura and Pumbedisa, became the leaders of the Jewish world. They were called the Geonim. Jews in faraway lands sent them letters with questions in halacha, and the Geonim wrote back. Their answer letters are called teshuvos. For hundreds of years, the Geonim kept Klal Yisroel learning the same Torah.

In time, as the yeshivos of Bavel grew weaker, new Torah centers grew up in North Africa, Spain, France and Germany. The great rabbanim of this new time are called the Rishonim, "the first ones." First came the Geonim, and after them the Rishonim.

One of the best-known Rishonim is Rashi, who was born in 1040 and lived in France. Rashi wrote a commentary that explains the Chumash and most of the Gemara, word by word, in short, clear sentences. Almost every boy who opens a Chumash or a Gemara today learns with Rashi beside him.

Another giant was the Rambam, who was born in Spain in 1138. When he was young, a cruel ruler took over his city, and his family had to flee. After years of wandering, they settled in Egypt. There the Rambam became a doctor to the ruler and a leader of the Jews. He wrote the Mishneh Torah, a great sefer that sets out all of halacha in clear order.

Eli finished his story with a short list: the Gemara, the Geonim, the Rishonim.
--- words
feudalism: a way of ruling where the king gave land to nobles, and they owed him loyalty and fighters
nobles: rich, powerful families who ruled land for the king
knights: trained fighters on horseback who served the nobles
peasants: poor farmers who worked the land
Crusaders: armies from Europe that marched to fight over the Holy Land
kedoshim: holy ones; Jews who were killed because they were Jews
teshuvos: letters that answer questions in halacha
commentary: writing that explains another book, line by line
