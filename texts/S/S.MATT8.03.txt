title: Nothing Lost, Nothing Gained

Yoni Kaplan set a plastic bag on the newsroom scale. Inside, in one corner, was a small spoonful of baking soda. In the other corner was a little cup of vinegar, carefully kept apart. Yoni sealed the bag and read the scale: 250 grams.

"Here is the question for our science page," he said. "When I mix them, the bag will fizz and puff up with gas. Will it weigh more, less or the same?"

Eli guessed less: "The gas is light. It floats." Benny guessed more: "Something new is being made." Gavriel did not guess. He got out his notebook.

Yoni tipped the cup. The vinegar splashed into the baking soda, the bag fizzed and swelled up like a pillow, and the boys leaned in to read the scale. It still said 250 grams.

About 250 years ago, a French scientist named Antoine Lavoisier did careful experiments just like this, with closed containers and very exact scales. He discovered one of the great laws of science, the law of conservation of mass. It says that in a chemical reaction, matter is not created and not destroyed. The mass of everything before a reaction equals the mass of everything after it.

The law explains some puzzles that had fooled people for a long time. When wood burns, the pile of ash left over weighs much less than the wood did. Where did the mass go? Into gases that rose into the air with the smoke. But when an iron nail rusts, the rusty nail weighs a little more than the clean nail did. Where did the extra mass come from? From oxygen in the air, which joined with the iron to make rust. In both cases, nothing was lost and nothing was created. The atoms only moved.
===
Why does it work? Because in a reaction, atoms are not made and not destroyed. They only rearrange. The atoms of the baking soda and the vinegar let go of their old partners and join up in new ways, making new substances: water, a new salt dissolved in the water, and carbon dioxide gas. Every atom that was in the bag before the reaction is still in the bag after it, just in a new arrangement. So the mass stays the same.

Then Yoni tried the experiment again, this time in an open cup. Before mixing, the cup with the baking soda and vinegar weighed 250 grams. After it finished fizzing, it weighed only 246 grams.

"Aha," said Eli. "So I was right!"

"Not quite," said Gavriel, looking up from his notebook. "Nothing was destroyed. The gas escaped into the air. In the sealed bag it could not get out, so we weighed it. In the open cup it floated away, so the scale missed it." To find how much gas escaped, he simply subtracted: 250 minus 246 is 4 grams of gas.
===
Reactions do more than make new substances. They also give off heat or take in heat.

Some reactions give off heat. They warm up their surroundings. A hand warmer works this way. Inside the little packet is iron powder. When you open it and shake it, the iron reacts with oxygen from the air, the same reaction that makes iron rust, but much faster. The packet gets warm enough to heat cold fingers on a winter morning. A burning candle gives off heat too.

Other reactions take in heat. They pull heat from their surroundings, so they feel cold. Baking soda and vinegar is one of them. A cold pack for a sports injury feels the same way. When you squeeze it, a small bag of water breaks inside, and a powder dissolves in the water, pulling in so much heat that the pack quickly turns icy cold. Dissolving is a physical change, not a reaction, but it takes in heat the same way. If you put a thermometer in a cup of baking soda and vinegar, you can watch the temperature drop a few degrees as it fizzes.
===
To tell which kind of reaction you have, measure the temperature before and after. If it goes up, the reaction gave off heat. If it goes down, the reaction took in heat. To find out by how much, subtract the smaller number from the bigger one.

Gavriel tested a hand warmer: it started at 20 degrees Celsius and reached 44 degrees, a rise of 24 degrees. A cold pack started at 22 degrees and dropped to 5 degrees, a drop of 17 degrees. When he ordered all his tests from the biggest rise to the biggest drop, the hand warmer came first, and the cold pack came last.

The boys called their article "Nothing Lost, Nothing Gained." At the end, Akiva added a line his rebbe had taught him from Koheles, where Shlomo HaMelech writes that there is nothing new under the sun. "In chemistry," Akiva wrote, "that is almost exactly right. Every reaction makes new substances, but never a single new atom. The same atoms, created at the very beginning, just keep finding new partners."
--- words
conservation: keeping something the same, so none is lost
mass: how much matter is in something, measured with a scale
rearrange: to put the same pieces together in a new way
surroundings: everything around something
oxygen: a gas in the air that we breathe and that many reactions use
thermometer: a tool that measures temperature
