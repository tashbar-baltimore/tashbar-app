On Friday morning, the Beacon newsroom smelled like a bakery. Akiva Feldman had brought a challah from home, still warm, for the staff meeting. Eli Mizrahi held it up and turned it in the light. "Here is a question for the science page," he said. "When dough turns into a golden, crusty challah, is that the same stuff, just changed? Or did something new get made?"

It was a good question, and the answer takes us to one of the biggest ideas in science: the difference between a physical change and a chemical change.

In a physical change, a substance changes its size, shape or state, but it is still the same substance. When you tear paper, it is still paper. When you melt an ice cube, it becomes liquid water, but it is still water. When you boil that water, it turns into steam, but steam is still water, just in the form of a gas. When you stir sugar into tea, the sugar seems to disappear, but it is still sugar, spread out in tiny bits. Taste the tea and you will know. Grinding, cutting, melting, freezing, boiling and dissolving are all physical changes. Often, they can be undone: freeze the water again, and you have ice.
===
In a chemical change, also called a chemical reaction, something new is made. The tiny particles that make up the starting substances, called atoms, break apart from their old partners and join up in new ways. The result is one or more new substances, with new properties. You cannot get the old substances back just by cooling or drying them.

How can you tell that a reaction has happened? You cannot see atoms, so scientists look for clues.

The first clue is a gas that forms when two things are mixed. Drop baking soda into vinegar, and it fizzes and foams. Those bubbles are a brand-new gas, carbon dioxide, that was not in either the baking soda or the vinegar.

The second clue is a color change that comes from a new substance. A shiny copper penny left outside for years turns dull and green. The copper has reacted with the air and water to make a new green coating. A cut apple slowly turns brown for the same kind of reason.

The third clue is a new smell. Milk that has gone sour smells different because new substances have formed in it.
===
The fourth clue is light or heat that comes from the change itself. A burning candle gives off light and heat as the wax reacts with the air. The fifth clue is a solid that suddenly forms when two clear liquids are mixed, making the mixture cloudy.

So is the golden challah crust a chemical change? Yes. In the hot oven, sugars and other materials in the dough react, making new substances that are brown, crisp and full of new smells and flavors. You can never turn the crust back into dough.

But be careful. A clue is not proof by itself. Bubbles are the most common trap. When a pot of water boils, it bubbles hard, but no new substance is made. The bubbles are only water in the form of a gas. That is a physical change. In the same way, melting ice changes how water looks, but not what it is. A good scientist asks: did something new get made, or is it the same substance in a new form?

Chemical reactions are not only in labs and kitchens. They are happening inside you right now. When you eat, your body breaks food down into new, smaller substances it can use. When you breathe, your cells use oxygen to release the energy stored in food, making carbon dioxide that you breathe out. Every second, thousands of reactions are keeping you alive, all without a single test tube. Scientists have learned to use reactions on purpose too: to make medicines, to make plastics and fabrics, and to make the fertilizer that helps farmers grow more food.
===
Gavriel Shirazi made a chart for the article with two columns. On one side he wrote physical changes: melting butter, boiling water, tearing paper, dissolving salt, freezing juice. On the other side he wrote chemical changes: baking challah, a rusting nail, a burning candle, souring milk, baking soda fizzing in vinegar.

Then Yoni Kaplan added a puzzle for readers. "In the lab, Chaim tested 12 changes. 5 of them were physical. How many were chemical reactions?" Readers who know that every change is either one or the other can solve it: 12 minus 5 leaves 7 chemical reactions.

Mr. Gross, the faculty adviser, read the article and nodded. "When you bake challah for Shabbos," he said, "you are a chemist, whether you know it or not." Eli tore off a piece of the challah. "Then I am doing an experiment," he said, "on taste."
--- words
substance: one kind of matter, like water, sugar or iron
physical: having to do with how matter looks or what form it is in, not what it is made of
chemical: having to do with what substances are made of and how they change into new ones
reaction: a change in which new substances are made
atoms: the tiny particles that everything is made of
properties: the features of a substance, like its color, smell and hardness
dissolving: mixing into a liquid so completely that it seems to disappear
