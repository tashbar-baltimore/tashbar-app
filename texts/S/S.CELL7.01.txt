In the year 1665, an English scientist named Robert Hooke cut a very thin slice of cork, the light bark that bottle stoppers are made of. He put it under a simple microscope he had built himself. What he saw surprised him. The cork was full of tiny empty boxes, lined up in rows like the rooms of a building. Hooke called them cells, from a Latin word for small rooms.

Hooke did not know it yet, but he had found the building blocks of every living thing. The cork cells he saw were empty, because cork is dead bark. But living cells are full of busy parts, and they are everywhere. A leaf is made of cells. So is a fish, a mushroom, a blade of grass and your own body. Every living thing is built from cells, and a cell is the smallest thing that is truly alive.

Gavriel Shirazi read about Hooke for The Beacon's science page. "So a cell is not a thing inside living things," he wrote. "A cell is what living things are made of."
===
Some living things are made of just one cell. A single bacterium is one cell. So is a yeast cell, the kind that makes challah dough rise, and an amoeba that creeps through a drop of pond water. These one-celled living things are far too small to see without a microscope, yet each one eats, grows and makes more of itself.

Other living things are made of a huge number of cells working together. An oak tree, a goldfish and a person are all made of many cells. Scientists estimate that a grown man's body has about 37 trillion cells. If you counted one cell every second, without stopping to eat or sleep, it would take you more than a million years.

Cells come in many shapes, because they do many jobs. Nerve cells are long and thin, like wires, to carry messages. Red blood cells are round and flat, so they can squeeze through tiny tubes. Skin cells are flat and packed tightly, like tiles on a floor.
===
Where do new cells come from? A cell grows, and then it splits into two cells. Each new cell grows and splits again. One cell becomes 2, then 4, then 8, then 16. This is how a small seedling becomes a tall tree, and how a cut on your finger heals. Every cell in your body came from a cell that split before it.

After Hooke, scientists spent almost two hundred years studying cells. They summed up what they learned in three short rules, called the cell theory. First, every living thing is made of one or more cells. Second, the cell is the smallest unit of life. Third, every cell comes from another cell that split. These three rules are still true today.

Splitting can happen fast. Some bacteria can split every 20 minutes. Start with one, and after an hour there are 8. After a few hours there are thousands. That is why food left out on a hot day can spoil so quickly, and why we wash our hands before we eat.
===
How small is a cell? Picture a scale of sizes. Atoms are the tiniest: tens of thousands of them could line up across a single cell. A cell is far bigger than an atom, but most cells are still too small to see. A leaf is made of millions of cells, and a whole tree is made of a huge number of leaves, roots and branches, all built from cells.

Yoni Kaplan finished the article with a thought from his Zeidy. "Every morning, in the bracha Asher Yatzar, we thank Hashem for forming the body with wisdom," Zeidy had told him. "Now you know a little of how much wisdom that means: trillions of tiny cells, each one doing its job."
