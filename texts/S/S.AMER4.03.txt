Zeidy Benaim spread an old map across the dining room table. Yehuda and his friend Nosson Abergel leaned in close. "Here is where the story of the Jews in America begins," said Zeidy. His finger rested on a tiny town at the tip of an island. "New Amsterdam, in the year 1654."

Before that, the Dutch ruled a town called Recife, in Brazil, in South America. Jews lived there in peace. They had a shul and a school for their children. Then, in 1654, the Portuguese took over Recife. The Jews knew they would not be free to live as Jews under Portuguese rule, so they sailed away.

Most of them went back to Holland. But one small group, twenty-three Jews in all, ended up on a ship to New Amsterdam, a little Dutch town in North America. There were men, women and children. They arrived in September 1654, tired and poor, with almost nothing left.

"Twenty-three people," said Nosson. "That is fewer than the boys in our class."
===
The governor of New Amsterdam was a stern man named Peter Stuyvesant. He did not want the Jews to stay. He wrote to the Dutch company that ran the town and asked for permission to send them away.

The Jews wrote letters too. They reminded the company that Jews had been loyal to the Dutch in Brazil and had stood by them in hard times. In 1655, the answer came back from Holland: the Jews could stay.

Even then, life was not fair. The men of the town took turns standing guard at night to protect it. The town leaders said that Jews could not stand guard. A Jewish man named Asser Levy would not accept this. He asked again and again for the right to stand guard like everyone else, and in the end he won.

The little group did not give up their Torah in their new home. They davened together and started a congregation called Shearith Israel. It still meets in New York today. And New Amsterdam itself? Ten years after the Jews arrived, the English took over the town and gave it a new name: New York.
===
Over the next hundred years, small Jewish communities grew in other towns along the coast. In Newport, Rhode Island, Jews built a beautiful shul. It opened in 1763 and is called the Touro Synagogue. It is the oldest shul building still standing in America. In 1790, President George Washington sent a letter to the Jews of Newport. He wrote that the new country would give "to bigotry no sanction." That meant the government would not support hatred against anyone because of his religion.

Our own city has a piece of this story too. In 1845, Jews in Baltimore built the Lloyd Street Synagogue. It is the oldest shul building in Maryland, and people still visit it today. Rabbi Avraham Rice, who came to Baltimore in 1840, was the rav of the congregation that built it.

Yehuda looked down at the map, then up at his grandfather. "From twenty-three people to all the Jews in America today," he said.

Zeidy nodded and folded the map with care. "They stood up for their rights, they held on to the Torah, and they stayed," he said. "That is a big part of why we are here."
--- words
governor: the leader in charge of a town, colony or state
stern: strict and serious, not easy to please
permission: being told by someone in charge that you may do something
loyal: always faithful to someone and on their side
congregation: a group of Jews who daven together in one shul
bigotry: hating people because they are different from you
