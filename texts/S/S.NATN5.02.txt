Avrumi Katz drew a big tree on the board, with three thick branches. "Mr. Halpern says our government is like this," he told his friends. "One plan, three branches."

After the war, the new country needed rules for how it would be run. In 1787, leaders from the states met in Philadelphia, with George Washington in charge of the meeting. At last they wrote the Constitution, the plan for America's government. It is still our plan today.

The Constitution splits the government into three branches, so that no one person or group has too much power. The legislative branch is Congress. It makes the laws, and it has two parts, the House of Representatives and the Senate. The executive branch is led by the President. It carries out the laws. The judicial branch is led by the Supreme Court. It decides what the laws mean and whether they follow the Constitution.

Each branch can stop the others from going too far. This is called checks and balances. If Congress passes a law that the President does not like, he can say no. That is called a veto. Then Congress can vote again, and if two out of every three members agree, the law passes anyway. In 1789, George Washington became the first President.
===
Many Americans still worried. What if the new government grew too strong and took their freedoms away? They wanted those freedoms written down. So ten amendments, or changes, were added to the Constitution in 1791. Together they are called the Bill of Rights.

The first one, the First Amendment, protects some of our most important freedoms: freedom of religion, freedom of speech, freedom of the press and the freedom to gather peacefully. Freedom of religion means the government may not force anyone to follow a religion, and may not stop people from keeping their own.

For Jews this was something new. In many lands, Jews had been chased away or kept from living as Jews. In 1790, President Washington visited Newport, Rhode Island. The Jews of the town wrote him a letter of welcome, and he wrote back. He promised that the government of the United States gives "to bigotry no sanction, to persecution no assistance." That means the government will not approve of hatred, and it will not help anyone who hurts people because of their religion.
===
In 1803, President Thomas Jefferson bought a huge piece of land called Louisiana from France. American farmers needed the Mississippi River and the port of New Orleans to ship their crops. The Louisiana Purchase doubled the size of the United States.

But the country had a terrible wrong inside it: slavery. In the South, millions of Black men, women and children were enslaved. They were owned like property and forced to work without pay, and their families could be sold apart. In the North, the states had ended slavery one by one.

In 1860, Abraham Lincoln was elected President. He wanted to stop slavery from spreading. Many Southern states broke away and formed their own country, the Confederacy. The North, called the Union, fought to keep the country together. The Civil War began in 1861 and lasted four long years. In 1865, the North won. That same year, the 13th Amendment ended slavery in all of the United States.

"We say it at the Seder every year," said Binyamin Yusupov quietly. "We were slaves in Mitzrayim. We know how terrible slavery is."

veto: when the President says no to a law that Congress passed
amendments: changes added to the Constitution
bigotry: hating people because they are different
persecution: hurting people because of who they are or what they believe
enslaved: forced to work for others, with no pay and no freedom
