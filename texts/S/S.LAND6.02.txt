Eli Mizrahi held up a bottle of seltzer for the Beacon team. "Watch," he said. He shook it hard and twisted the cap. Fizz exploded out of the bottle and all over his sleeve. "That," Eli said, wiping his arm, "is how a volcano works."

He was almost right. A volcano is an opening in the earth's crust where melted rock, gas and ash escape from deep inside. It starts far underground. In some places, rock in the upper mantle and lower crust gets so hot that it melts. Melted rock that is still underground is called magma.

Magma is lighter than the solid rock around it, so it slowly rises, the way a cork rises in water. It collects in a huge underground pocket called a magma chamber. There it waits, sometimes for hundreds of years. Gas dissolved in the magma, like the gas in Eli's seltzer, pushes harder and harder. When the pressure is too great, the magma bursts upward through a tube called the vent and out of an opening at the top called the crater. Once magma reaches the surface, it has a new name: lava.
===
Not every volcano explodes. The kind of eruption depends on the lava.

Some lava is runny, almost like hot syrup. Gas bubbles slip out of it easily, so the pressure never builds very high. These volcanoes ooze rivers of glowing lava that flow down their sides for miles. The volcanoes of Hawaii are like this. Their runny lava builds wide, gently sloping mountains, and over a very long time it built the Hawaiian Islands themselves, rising from the bottom of the Pacific.

Other lava is thick and sticky, like cold honey. Gas cannot escape from it, so the pressure builds and builds, the way it did inside Eli's shaken bottle. When the volcano finally erupts, it explodes. In 1980, Mount St. Helens in Washington State blew off the whole top of the mountain. The blast flattened forests for miles, and a cloud of ash rose more than 20 kilometers into the sky.
===
A volcano can send out more than lava. It can throw out chunks of rock called volcanic bombs, pour out poisonous gases, and spread clouds of ash that block the sun and fall like gray snow on towns far away. Pilots must steer their planes around ash clouds, because ash can stop a jet engine.

Lava is very hot when it leaves the vent, often about 1,100 to 1,200 degrees Celsius. As it flows, it cools. By about 700 degrees, most lava has stiffened and stops flowing. Then it hardens into new rock. Every eruption adds new land to the earth.

Volcanoes have changed history. Almost two thousand years ago, Mount Vesuvius in Italy erupted. Hot ash and gas raced down its sides and buried the Roman town of Pompeii in a single day. The ash covered the town so completely that it stayed hidden for more than 1,500 years. When diggers finally uncovered it, they found streets, bakeries and even loaves of bread burned black, just as they were on the day of the eruption.

Yet volcanoes also give. Volcanic ash breaks down into some of the richest soil on earth, so farmers grow grapes, olives and vegetables on the slopes of old volcanoes. In Iceland, heat from deep underground warms the water that heats people's homes, and steam spins machines that make electricity. The same fire that can destroy a town can also warm a house and feed a family.
===
From deepest to highest, a volcano has the magma chamber far below, then the vent, then the crater at the top, and above it all, the ash cloud of an eruption.

Scientists who study volcanoes are called volcanologists. They measure small earthquakes, gases and even tiny bulges in the ground to warn people before an eruption. Many lives have been saved by their warnings.

Benny Okafor-Levine wrote the headline for Eli's article: "The Earth Has a Pressure Valve." Akiva liked it. "And next time," he told Eli, "do the seltzer experiment outside."
--- words
crust: the thin, hard outer layer of the earth
magma: melted rock while it is still underground
chamber: a large, closed space or room
dissolved: mixed into a liquid so it cannot be seen
vent: a tube or opening that lets something out
eruption: the bursting out of lava, gas and ash from a volcano
volcanologists: scientists who study volcanoes
