# Kindergarten Kriyah: do the games match the research?

**The request (HoS, 2026-11-26):** "Check each game against what research says works for teaching young children to read, and write a short report that says, game by game, what is strong and what to change. Then make the changes, starting with the biggest gains."

## The nine points, and what the research says
| # | Point | What works (short) | Main sources |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Explicit, systematic letter-sound teaching | Teach each letter's name **and** its sound together, in a fixed order, one small step at a time. | National Reading Panel 2000; Piasta & Wagner 2010; Roberts, Vadasy & Sanders |
| 2 | "I do, we do, you do" | Show one example, do one together with help, then let him try alone. The "we do" step is the one most often skipped. | Rosenshine 2012; Archer & Hughes 2011 |
| 3 | Short sessions | 2–4 minutes per game, 10–15 minutes a day. Many short cycles through the letters beat one long lesson. | Jones & Reutzel 2012 ("Enhanced Alphabet Knowledge"); Sunde et al. 2020 |
| 4 | Immediate, kind correction that teaches the difference | Right after a mistake, show **what is different** (not just "try again"), then let him do it right. | Hattie & Timperley 2007; Carnine 1976 |
| 5 | Spaced review of earlier letters | Letters come back again and again over days and weeks; the ones he misses come back sooner. | Cepeda et al. 2006; Sunde et al. 2020 |
| 6 | Mixed practice | Old and new letters mixed together, especially look-alikes, so he must really look. | Rohrer & Taylor 2007; Carnine 1981 |
| 7 | Multisensory: trace, **say**, hear | Seeing, hearing, touching **and saying it aloud** together. Saying it aloud is its own strong help (the "production effect"). | Bara & Gentaz 2004, 2007; MacLeod et al. 2010; Shmidman & Ehri 2010 |
| 8 | Few distractions | Busy pictures and long celebrations take a 5-year-old's eyes off the letter. | Fisher, Godwin & Seltman 2014 |
| 9 | Mastery before moving on | The next step opens only when this one is solid; a boy who isn't there gets more practice, not a harder step. | Bloom 1968; Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns 1990 |

## What the whole set already does well
- **Order:** strict Alef-Beis order, right to left, one unit at a time (point 1).
- **Name and sound together** in every game; Alef and Ayin are taught as "quiet" (point 1).
- **Picture inside the letter** in every opener (the camel turns into Gimmel): the method tested on Hebrew letters by Shmidman & Ehri 2010 (points 1 and 7).
- **Look-alikes taught apart, then side by side** with an arrow at the one difference, in the same words in every game (points 4 and 6).
- **Kind correction** that names the letter he tapped and shows the difference, then lets him do it right (point 4).
- **Mastery gate:** the next game opens at 2 of 3 stars, and a boy who struggled gets an easier start next time (point 9).
- **Short games:** each one is about 2–4 minutes (point 3).

## Where the whole set is weak (the biggest gains, in order)
1. **Spaced review is too thin (point 5).** Each unit reviews only the unit right before it. By Unit 7, letters from Units 1–4 almost never come back, and nothing remembers which letters a boy keeps missing. The map promised "every game ends with 2–3 items from earlier units" and "something he got wrong comes back sooner"; neither is built.
2. **He never says the letter himself (point 7).** He hears, sees and touches, but no game asks him to say "Beis!" out loud. Saying it is cheap and strong.
3. **"We do" is missing (point 2).** Every game goes straight from "Watch me" to "Now you!". There's no step where he does one with the hand still guiding him.
4. **Too much going on behind the letter (point 8).** The painted backgrounds are lovely but busy, and the confetti and sparkles run long. On a phone the letter can get lost.
5. **The mastery check mixes in only 2–3 older letters (points 5, 6, 9).** "Show what you know" mostly checks the new letters, so a boy can pass Unit 7 having forgotten Gimmel.

## What changed (biggest gains first)
1. **Done: "Remember these?" at the end of every practice game (gains 1 and 5).** Before the stars, three quick "hear it, tap it" questions about **earlier** letters, chosen by spacing: a letter he got wrong comes back first, then the one he hasn't seen for longest. Every answer (here and in the unit check) is remembered on his device, letter by letter. Practice only: it never costs a star. Units 2 and up.
2. **Done: the unit check asks the older letter he finds hardest (gains 5 and 9).** One of the check's older-letter questions is swapped for his weakest older letter.
3. **Done: "Say it with me" (gain 2).** In every opener story and in Feel the Shape, after each new letter: "Say it with me: Beis!", a short pause for him to say it, then the voice says it with him.
4. **Done: quieter screens (gain 4).** Shorter, lighter celebrations and a soft light veil over the painted backgrounds, so the letter stands out.
5. **Next: "Let's do one together" (gain 3).** After "Watch me", one turn with the hand and glow still guiding him before he is on his own. To be built game by game, starting with the Look-Alike Detective and Sound Match.
