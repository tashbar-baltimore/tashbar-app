# Tashbar Math Ladder

**PLAN ONLY, waiting for Mrs. Khaver. Nothing is built from it until she approves it.**

**What was asked:** Mrs. Khaver (2026-11-24): "I don't want to work on faster or grade. I want to map the progression of skills and the most efficient way to teach each one."

130 skills on 11 ladders. No grades: each rung lists what it needs first.

## How the ladder works
- Math is 11 ladders, side by side. Each rung is one real skill, in the order a child's thinking grows.
- There are no grades on the ladder. Each rung says what must come first ("needs"), even from another ladder.
- A short placement check finds his rung on each ladder. He moves up when he passes a rung's check with no help, two days apart.
- If he gets stuck twice, he drops to the rung it needs for a short re-teach, then climbs back.
- Every rung is taught the same way: an object he can move, then a picture, then the written rule. One clear idea in plain words. A worked example, then his turn. The usual mistake is shown and fixed.
- Every practice set mixes in earlier rungs, so nothing is forgotten.

## Built on
- Building Blocks (Clements & Sarama): the order of the early rungs and their level names.
- Singapore: the bar model, and object, then picture, then rule.
- Japan and China: few skills, taught deeply; meaning before shortcut rules.
- Russia (Davydov, Toom): part–whole thinking and word problems before algebra.
- Research on fractions (Siegler), mixed practice (Rohrer) and worked examples.
- The report "The World's Best Math Programs for Grades 3–8" (November 2026).

## CN. Counting and the number line
Saying numbers, counting things, counting on and back, skip counting, then the whole number line: negatives, fractions and roots.

### CN1. Say the numbers to 10
- **He can:** Says 1 to 10 in order, without skipping.
- **Needs first:** nothing (a starting rung)
- **Quick check:** Count with me from 1. Where did you stop?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Reciter

### CN2. One number for each thing
- **He can:** Touches each thing once and says one number for it.
- **Needs first:** CN1
- **Quick check:** Count these 6 apples. Touch each one.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Corresponder

### CN3. The last number tells how many
- **He can:** After counting, knows the last number is how many there are.
- **Needs first:** CN2
- **Quick check:** You counted 1 to 5. So how many are there?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Counter (small numbers)

### CN4. Give me 7
- **He can:** Counts out a number of things when asked, to 10, then 20.
- **Needs first:** CN3
- **Quick check:** Put 7 kugels on the plate.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Producer; Counter and Producer (10+)

### CN5. One more, one less, count back
- **He can:** Counts back from 10. Knows the number just after and just before.
- **Needs first:** CN4
- **Quick check:** There are 6. One more comes. How many now, without counting all?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Counter Backward; Counter from N (N+1, N–1)

### CN6. Count to 100 by ones and tens
- **He can:** Counts to 100 by ones and by tens.
- **Needs first:** CN5
- **Quick check:** Count by tens to 100.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Skip Counter by 10s; Counter to 100

### CN7. Start anywhere and count on
- **He can:** Starts at any number (like 47) and counts on, to 120.
- **Needs first:** CN6
- **Quick check:** Start at 58. Count on 5.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Counter On Using Patterns

### CN8. Skip count; odd and even
- **He can:** Skip counts by 2s, 5s and 10s from any number. Tells odd from even by pairing.
- **Needs first:** CN7
- **Quick check:** Count by 5s from 35. Is 14 odd or even? Show me with pairs.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Skip Counter

### CN9. Count to 1,000 by 1s, 10s and 100s
- **He can:** Counts on and back by ones, tens and hundreds anywhere to 1,000.
- **Needs first:** CN8
- **Quick check:** Count by tens from 370 to 450.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Multidigit Counter

### CN10. Below zero: negative numbers
- **He can:** Places negatives on the number line, compares them, and uses all four parts of a grid.
- **Needs first:** FR4, SH9
- **Quick check:** Which is colder, –8° or –3°? Put both on the line.
- **Where it comes from:** Number line (Russia, Japan: before equations)

### CN11. All the numbers on one line
- **He can:** Puts fractions, decimals and negatives on one line. Knows which decimals end and which repeat.
- **Needs first:** CN10, FR8
- **Quick check:** Put –1½, 0.75 and –0.3 on the line.
- **Where it comes from:** Number system (all top programs)

### CN12. Roots and real numbers
- **He can:** Finds square roots and squeezes a root between two whole numbers.
- **Needs first:** CN11, PV8
- **Quick check:** √50 is between which two whole numbers?
- **Where it comes from:** Number system


## SC. Seeing, comparing and breaking numbers
Seeing how many at a glance, comparing amounts, and knowing the parts inside a number. This is the ground that adding stands on.

### SC1. See 1, 2, 3 at a glance
- **He can:** Says how many (up to 3, then 4) without counting.
- **Needs first:** nothing (a starting rung)
- **Quick check:** Flash 3 dots for one second. How many?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Perceptual Subitizer to 4

### SC2. See 5 at a glance
- **He can:** Sees up to 5 at once in dice and finger patterns.
- **Needs first:** SC1
- **Quick check:** Flash a 5 on a dice. How many?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Perceptual Subitizer to 5

### SC3. More, fewer, the same
- **He can:** Compares two groups by matching or counting, even when one is spread out.
- **Needs first:** CN3
- **Quick check:** 7 spread-out cups and 8 close-together cups. Which has more?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Matching Comparer; Counting Comparer

### SC4. Parts of numbers to 5
- **He can:** Knows 5 is 4 and 1, 3 and 2.
- **Needs first:** SC2, CN4
- **Quick check:** 5 apples, 2 in the bag. How many are hiding?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Composer to 4, then 5

### SC5. Parts of 6 to 10; friends of 10
- **He can:** Knows all pairs that make 10, and the parts of 6 to 9.
- **Needs first:** SC4
- **Quick check:** 7 on the ten-frame. How many more make 10?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Composer to 7, then 10

### SC6. See numbers to 20 in parts
- **He can:** Sees "10 and 4" on ten-frames without counting.
- **Needs first:** SC5, PV1
- **Quick check:** Flash two ten-frames (10 and 6). How many?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Conceptual Subitizer to 20

### SC7. Compare with < > =
- **He can:** Compares numbers by the biggest place first and writes < > =.
- **Needs first:** PV2
- **Quick check:** Which is more, 49 or 52? Write it with the sign.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Mental Number Line


## AS. Adding and subtracting
From joining things with his hands, to smart strategies, to written methods for big numbers, decimals and negatives.

### AS1. Put together, take away (within 5)
- **He can:** Joins and takes away with things he can move.
- **Needs first:** SC4
- **Quick check:** Moshe has 3 cookies. Bubby gives him 2. How many now?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Small Number +/–; Find Result

### AS2. Add and take away within 10
- **He can:** Adds and subtracts within 10, including "how many more?".
- **Needs first:** AS1, SC5
- **Quick check:** 4 + __ = 9.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Find Change +/–

### AS3. Count on, count back
- **He can:** Adds by counting on from the bigger number, not from 1.
- **Needs first:** AS2, CN7
- **Quick check:** 8 + 3: start at 8 and hop.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Counting Strategies +/–

### AS4. Smart facts: doubles and make ten
- **He can:** Uses doubles, near doubles and make-ten within 20.
- **Needs first:** AS3, SC5
- **Quick check:** 8 + 6: make a ten first. Show me.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Part-Whole; Deriver +/–

### AS5. The = sign means "the same as"
- **He can:** Finds a missing number anywhere and judges true or false number sentences.
- **Needs first:** AS4
- **Quick check:** 7 + __ = 12. Is 5 + 4 = 4 + 5 true?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Problem Solver +/–; Davydov

### AS6. Facts within 20, fast
- **He can:** Knows every fact within 20 without counting.
- **Needs first:** AS4

- **Where it comes from:** Fluency (Japan, Singapore, Saxon)

### AS7. Add and subtract within 100
- **He can:** Adds and subtracts two-digit numbers by tens and ones, in his head and on a number line.
- **Needs first:** AS6, PV3
- **Quick check:** 46 + 38. 70 – 24.
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Multidigit +/–

### AS8. Written method within 1,000
- **He can:** Adds and subtracts with regrouping in columns.
- **Needs first:** AS7, PV4
- **Quick check:** 352 + 179. 600 – 247.
- **Where it comes from:** All top programs

### AS9. Estimate first, then check
- **He can:** Rounds to estimate and tells if an answer makes sense.
- **Needs first:** AS8, PV5
- **Quick check:** Is 398 + 207 about 400, 600 or 800?
- **Where it comes from:** Russia, Japan: estimation

### AS10. Big numbers in columns
- **He can:** Adds and subtracts any whole numbers in columns.
- **Needs first:** AS9, PV6
- **Quick check:** 40,006 – 18,297.
- **Where it comes from:** All top programs

### AS11. Adding and subtracting decimals
- **He can:** Lines up the places to add and subtract decimals.
- **Needs first:** AS10, PV7
- **Quick check:** 11 – 1.69.
- **Where it comes from:** All top programs

### AS12. Adding and subtracting negatives
- **He can:** Adds and subtracts with negatives on a number line.
- **Needs first:** CN10, AS11
- **Quick check:** –5 + 8. 3 – 7.
- **Where it comes from:** Number line (Russia, Japan)

- **Needs first:** PV1, CN6

### PV3. 10 more, 10 less

- **Needs first:** PV2

### PV4. Hundreds, tens and ones

- **Where it comes from:** All top programs

- **He can:** Shares things fairly, one for you, one for me, and checks it is fair.
- **Needs first:** CN4

### MD2. Equal groups; skip counting
- **He can:** Counts equal groups by skip counting.
- **Needs first:** MD1, CN8
- **Quick check:** 5 plates, 2 rolls on each. How many rolls?
- **Where it comes from:** Building Blocks: Grouper; Skip Counter ×/÷

### MD3. Arrays and what × means
- **He can:** Knows 3 × 4 means 3 groups of 4. Sees rows and columns. Order does not matter.

### MD7. Times tens and hundreds

## FR. Fractions, decimals, ratio and percent
The gateway to algebra. From fair halves of a challah, to fractions as numbers on a line, to all four operations, to percent and ratio.

### FR1. Fair shares
- **He can:** Cuts a whole into equal parts and knows equal means the same size, not the same shape.
- **Needs first:** MD1
- **Quick check:** Is this challah cut in fair halves? Why not?
- **Where it comes from:** Equipartitioning (Confrey); Building Blocks shapes
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Paper folding, then a picture of a challah, pizza or chocolate bar cut into parts.
  - The one idea: "Fair" means every piece is the same size. Count the pieces to name them: 2 fair pieces are halves, 4 fair pieces are fourths.
  - Steps: 1. Fold a paper in half. Do the two parts match exactly? 2. Cut a picture into 2, 3, then 4 fair pieces. 3. Say fair or not fair, and tell why.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He calls any 2 pieces "halves", even when one is bigger. Fix: lay one piece on the other. If they do not match, it is not fair.
  - Practice: Fair or not fair: tap to judge. Cut it fairly: drag a knife line. Two different-looking halves of a square: are both fair?
  - Why this way: Children understand fair sharing long before they understand fraction symbols. Starting here builds on what they already know.

### FR2. One part: unit fractions
- **He can:** Names one part of equal parts as 1/2, 1/3, 1/4. Knows more parts means smaller pieces.
- **Needs first:** FR1
- **Quick check:** Which piece is bigger, 1/8 or 1/4 of the same cake?
- **Where it comes from:** Japan, Singapore: unit fractions first
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Fraction strips: the same long strip cut into 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8 equal parts, laid one above the other.
  - The one idea: The bottom number tells how many equal parts the whole has. The more parts, the smaller each one.
  - Steps: 1. Take one strip. Count its equal parts. That tells the bottom number. 2. One part is "one ___th". 3. Lay strips under each other to compare.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He thinks 1/8 is bigger than 1/4 "because 8 is bigger than 4". Fix: show both pieces side by side on strips, and share one cake among 8 brothers vs 4 brothers.
  - Practice: Name the piece. Order 1/2, 1/8, 1/3, 1/6 from biggest to smallest. "Would you rather have 1/3 or 1/5 of the kugel?"
  - Why this way: Unit fractions are the building block of all other fractions; Japan and Singapore start every fraction topic from them.

### FR3. Fractions are counted in units
- **He can:** Sees 3/4 as three 1/4s, and counts on by unit fractions past 1.
- **Needs first:** FR2
- **Quick check:** How many fourths make 3/4? What comes after 4/4?
- **Where it comes from:** Japan; Common Core Progressions
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Fraction strips, then tiles of one unit fraction counted out one by one.
  - The one idea: We count fourths the way we count apples: one fourth, two fourths, three fourths, four fourths, five fourths.
  - Steps: 1. Pick the unit (one fourth). 2. Count how many units. 3. Say it: "three fourths" = 3/4.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He reads 3/4 as "3 and 4", two separate numbers. Fix: always say it as a count of units ("three fourths") and build it from unit tiles.
  - Practice: Build 5/6 from sixths. Count on: 2/3, 3/3, 4/3, __. Which is made of more pieces?
  - Why this way: Seeing a fraction as a count of unit fractions makes adding fractions and fractions bigger than 1 obvious later.

### FR4. Fractions on the number line
- **He can:** Places fractions between 0 and 1 and beyond 1. Knows a fraction is a number, and 4/4 = 1.
- **Needs first:** FR3, ME3
- **Quick check:** Put 3/4 on the line. Where is 5/4?
- **Where it comes from:** Siegler: fraction size on the number line predicts later algebra
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: A measuring tape or a long strip, then a number line from 0 to 1 cut into equal spaces.
  - The one idea: The whole is the distance from 0 to 1. Cut it into equal spaces. Each space is one unit fraction long.
  - Steps: 1. Find 0 and 1. 2. Cut the space between them into equal parts (the bottom number). 3. Count the spaces from 0 (the top number).
  - Usual mistake and fix: He counts tick marks instead of spaces, so 3/4 lands in the wrong place. Fix: hop along the spaces from 0, saying "one fourth, two fourths…".
  - Practice: Drag the fraction to its place. Find 1 on a line where only 0 and 1/3 are marked. Spot the wrong label.
  - Why this way: Placing fractions on a line is the strongest early sign of later algebra success, so this rung is the heart of the ladder.

### FR5. Compare fractions
- **He can:** Compares using the same bottom, the same top, or a benchmark of 1/2 and 1.
- **Needs first:** FR4
- **Quick check:** Which is more, 3/8 or 3/5? 5/8 or 4/10? Explain.
- **Where it comes from:** Common Core Progressions; Japan
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Two number lines or two strips, one above the other.
  - The one idea: Same-size pieces: more pieces is more. Same number of pieces: bigger pieces is more. Or check: is it more or less than a half?
  - Steps: 1. Same bottom? Compare the tops. 2. Same top? The smaller bottom means bigger pieces. 3. Neither? Compare each to 1/2 or to 1.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He compares only the top numbers or only the bottom numbers. Fix: always ask "how big is each piece?" and check on the line.
  - Practice: Tap the bigger fraction. Put 3 fractions in order. "Explain why" taps a reason, not just an answer.
  - Why this way: Reasoning about size, not following a rule, is what makes fraction work stick.

### FR6. Equal fractions
- **He can:** Finds equal fractions by splitting every piece. Then the rule: multiply top and bottom by the same number.
- **Needs first:** FR5, MD6
- **Quick check:** 1/2 = ?/8. Is 2/3 = 4/6? Show it.
- **Where it comes from:** Singapore, Japan: model before rule
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: A strip folded in half, then folded again: the half is now 2 fourths, then 4 eighths.
  - The one idea: Cutting every piece into 2 makes twice as many pieces, each half as big. The amount stays the same.
  - Steps: 1. Draw the fraction. 2. Split every piece into the same number of smaller pieces. 3. Count the new pieces: top and bottom were both multiplied.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He adds the same number to top and bottom (1/2 = 2/3). Fix: draw both. They do not cover the same amount.
  - Practice: Match equal fractions. Fill the missing number. Simplify 6/8 by grouping pieces back together.
  - Why this way: Equal fractions are the tool for comparing, adding unlike fractions and later ratio. The picture keeps the rule from feeling like magic.

### FR7. Add and subtract like fractions; mixed numbers
- **He can:** Adds and subtracts fractions with the same bottom, including mixed numbers.
- **Needs first:** FR4
- **Quick check:** 2/5 + 4/5. 3 1/4 – 1 3/4.
- **Where it comes from:** Japan, Singapore
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Hops on a number line, and unit tiles.
  - The one idea: 2 fifths plus 4 fifths is 6 fifths, the same way 2 apples plus 4 apples is 6 apples. The bottom names the piece. It does not change.
  - Steps: 1. Check the pieces are the same size (same bottom). 2. Add or take away the number of pieces. 3. Change extra wholes: 6/5 = 1 1/5.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He adds the bottoms too (2/5 + 4/5 = 6/10). Fix: say it in words ("fifths plus fifths are fifths") and check it is bigger than each part.
  - Practice: Add with tiles, then without. Regroup a mixed number like trading a ten. Find the mistake in a worked example.
  - Why this way: Counting units makes the right answer obvious and blocks the most common fraction error.

### FR8. Tenths and hundredths as decimals
- **He can:** Writes tenths and hundredths as decimals and compares them.
- **Needs first:** FR6, PV4
- **Quick check:** 0.7 = ?/100. Which is more, 0.25 or 0.3?
- **Where it comes from:** Japan, Singapore: decimals from measuring
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Money (dimes and pennies), a meter stick, a 10 × 10 grid.
  - The one idea: The places keep going to the right of the ones: tenths, then hundredths. Each place is 10 times smaller.
  - Steps: 1. Read the fraction with a bottom of 10 or 100. 2. Write it in places after the point. 3. Compare place by place, starting from the left.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He thinks 0.25 is bigger than 0.3 because "25 is bigger than 3". Fix: shade both on a hundred grid, or say them as money: 25¢ vs 30¢.
  - Practice: Shade the grid. Write it as a decimal. Put decimals in order on a line.
  - Why this way: Decimals are fractions written in place value. Linking the two avoids treating decimals as whole numbers with a dot.

### FR9. Add and subtract unlike fractions
- **He can:** Finds a common piece size, then adds or subtracts.
- **Needs first:** FR6, FR7, MD10
- **Quick check:** 2/3 + 1/4.
- **Where it comes from:** All top programs
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Two strips with different cuts, both cut further until the pieces match.
  - The one idea: You can only add pieces that are the same size. Cut both into the same size pieces first.
  - Steps: 1. Find a bottom number both can become (a common multiple). 2. Change each fraction into an equal fraction. 3. Add or subtract the pieces.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He adds across: 2/3 + 1/4 = 3/7. Fix: estimate first. 2/3 + 1/4 is more than 2/3, and 3/7 is less than 1/2, so it is wrong.
  - Practice: Split the strips to match. Solve, then check with an estimate. Spot the wrong method.
  - Why this way: Builds directly on equal fractions (FR6), so it is a short step, not a new rule to memorize.

### FR10. A fraction of an amount
- **He can:** Finds 3/4 of 20 with a bar model, and multiplies a fraction by a whole number.
- **Needs first:** FR3, MD6
- **Quick check:** 3/4 of 20. 5 × 2/3.
- **Where it comes from:** Singapore bar model
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: A bar model: one bar for the whole amount, cut into equal parts.
  - The one idea: Cut the whole into 4 equal parts. One part is 20 ÷ 4 = 5. Three parts are 15.
  - Steps: 1. Draw a bar for the whole and write its amount. 2. Cut it into equal parts (the bottom number) and find one part. 3. Take the number of parts (the top number).
  - Usual mistake and fix: He multiplies 20 × 4 or divides by the top number. Fix: draw the bar every time until it is automatic.
  - Practice: 3/5 of the 30 boys go on the trip. How many? Story problems with bars that fill in, then bars he draws.
  - Why this way: The bar model turns "fraction of" into simple division and multiplication, and it is the same picture used later for ratio and percent.

### FR11. Fraction times fraction
- **He can:** Multiplies fractions with an area model, then the rule. Knows multiplying by less than 1 makes it smaller.
- **Needs first:** FR10, FR6
- **Quick check:** 2/3 × 3/4. Is 5 × 2/3 more or less than 5?
- **Where it comes from:** Area model (Japan, Singapore)
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Fold a paper into thirds one way and fourths the other way. Shade 2/3 of 3/4.
  - The one idea: 2/3 × 3/4 means "2/3 of 3/4". Multiply the tops to count shaded pieces, and the bottoms to count all the pieces.
  - Steps: 1. Shade the first fraction. 2. Take a fraction of the shaded part. 3. Count: top × top over bottom × bottom.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He thinks multiplying always makes bigger. Fix: "half of 6 is 3". Taking a part of something makes it smaller.
  - Practice: Shade and solve. Bigger or smaller than the starting number? Recipe problems: 2/3 of a 3/4-cup measure.
  - Why this way: The area picture explains the rule, so he can rebuild it if he forgets.

### FR12. Dividing with fractions
- **He can:** Answers "how many 1/4s in 3?" with strips. Then common bottoms. Then why "flip and multiply" works.
- **Needs first:** FR11, MD9
- **Quick check:** 3 ÷ 1/4. 2/3 ÷ 1/6. 3/4 ÷ 2/3.
- **Where it comes from:** Japan, Singapore, Russia: meaning before rule
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: Strips: lay 1/4 pieces along 3 wholes and count them.
  - The one idea: Dividing asks "how many of these fit in that?". 3 ÷ 1/4 asks how many quarters fit in 3. 12.
  - Steps: 1. Ask the "how many fit" question. 2. With the same bottom, divide the tops. 3. Shortcut: multiply by the flipped divisor (show why with one example).
  - Usual mistake and fix: He flips the wrong fraction, or flips both. Fix: estimate with the "how many fit" question first.
  - Practice: How many 1/3-cup scoops in 2 cups? Strips, then common bottoms, then the shortcut. Find the wrong flip.
  - Why this way: Japan and Singapore teach this rung only after the meaning is clear. That keeps it from being the most-forgotten rule in school math.

### FR13. Percent
- **He can:** Knows percent means "out of 100". Finds 10%, 25%, 50% and any percent of an amount.
- **Needs first:** FR8, FR10
- **Quick check:** 20% of 45. What percent is 18 out of 24?
- **Where it comes from:** Singapore bar model; Dutch double number line
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: A 10 × 10 grid, then a bar marked 0% to 100%, then a double number line.
  - The one idea: Percent means "out of 100". The whole bar is 100%. 10% is one tenth of it.
  - Steps: 1. Draw a bar for 100%. 2. Find 10% (divide by 10) or 1%. 3. Build the percent you need from those.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He thinks 20% of 45 is 20, or adds the percent. Fix: start from the 100% bar and find 10% first.
  - Practice: Shade percents. Discount and tip stories with bars. Benchmarks: is 48% about a half?
  - Why this way: Builds on fractions and decimals with the same bar picture, so percent is not a separate topic to learn.

### FR14. Ratio and rate
- **He can:** Uses ratio tables and bar models. Finds a unit rate.
- **Needs first:** FR10, MD6
- **Quick check:** 3 bottles cost $6. What do 7 cost? Boys to girls 2:3.
- **Where it comes from:** Singapore, Japan, Russia
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: A ratio table and a bar model with equal units.
  - The one idea: A ratio compares two amounts in the same units. Find the price of one, then any number.
  - Steps: 1. Write both amounts in a table. 2. Find the amount for 1 (unit rate). 3. Multiply to get the amount you need.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He adds the same amount to both sides (2:3 becomes 4:5) instead of multiplying. Fix: check with the table that both sides were multiplied.
  - Practice: Recipe scaling for a simcha. Better buy: which pack is cheaper per item? Fill in ratio tables.
  - Why this way: Ratio is the bridge from arithmetic to algebra. It uses multiplying, not adding.

### FR15. Proportions and percent change
- **He can:** Spots proportional relationships, writes y = kx, and solves discount, tax and percent-change problems.
- **Needs first:** FR14, FR13, PA7
- **Quick check:** A $40 coat is 15% off. What does it cost?
- **Where it comes from:** Common Core; Japan, Singapore
- **How to teach it**
  - What he sees and touches: A before-and-after bar model and a straight-line graph through 0.
  - The one idea: When two amounts grow together at the same rate, one is always k times the other.
  - Steps: 1. Check that the rate stays the same. 2. Find k (the amount for 1). 3. Use y = kx or a before-and-after bar.
  - Usual mistake and fix: He takes 15% off and then adds tax to the wrong amount. Fix: draw the before-and-after bar for each step.
  - Practice: Is it proportional? Find k from a table or graph. Discount and interest stories.
  - Why this way: Finishes the fraction ladder and leads straight into linear functions (PA10).
